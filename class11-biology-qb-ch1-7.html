Chapter 1 — The Living World (Pg 1–5)
Q.1
What is living? State the characteristics of living organisms.
Characteristics
  • Growth: Increase in size/mass due to cell division. Both from inside (plants) and outside (animals).
  • Metabolism: All chemical reactions inside body (anabolism + catabolism)
  • Reproduction: Ability to produce offspring (not universal — mules cannot)
  • Cellular organization: Cell is basic unit of life
  • Response to stimuli: React to environmental changes
  • Homeostasis: Maintain internal environment constant
  • Consciousness: Awareness of surroundings
∴ Metabolism is the most reliable criterion to define life.
Q.2
Define taxonomy, systematics and binomial nomenclature. Give rules of binomial nomenclature.
Taxonomy
Science of identification, classification and nomenclature of organisms.
Systematics
Study of diversity of living organisms and their evolutionary relationships (broader than taxonomy). Term coined by Linnaeus.
Binomial Nomenclature
System of giving two-part scientific names: Genus name + Species name. Introduced by Carolus Linnaeus.
Rules
  • Name in Latin or Latinised. Written in italics when printed.
  • Genus name starts with Capital letter; species name in small letter
  • Underlined separately when handwritten
  • Author name in abbreviated form after species name
  • e.g. Mangifera indica Linn. (Mango)
Q.3
Write the hierarchical arrangement of taxonomic categories with example (Mango).
Hierarchy
Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species
Mango Example
Kingdom: Plantae → Division: Angiospermae → Class: Dicotyledonae → Order: Sapindales → Family: Anacardiaceae → Genus: Mangifera → Species: indica

Chapter 2 — Systematics of Living Organisms (Pg 6–18)
Q.1
Explain Whittaker's Five Kingdom Classification with characteristics of each kingdom.
Kingdom 1: Monera
Prokaryotes (no nuclear membrane). Unicellular. e.g. Bacteria, Cyanobacteria (Blue-green algae). Cell wall present (peptidoglycan).
Kingdom 2: Protista
Eukaryotes. Unicellular. Aquatic. e.g. Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, Diatoms. Nutrition: autotrophic or heterotrophic.
Kingdom 3: Fungi
Eukaryotes. Multicellular (except yeast). Heterotrophic saprophytes. Cell wall of chitin. e.g. Mushroom, Aspergillus, Rhizopus.
Kingdom 4: Plantae
Eukaryotes. Multicellular. Autotrophic (photosynthesis). Cell wall of cellulose. e.g. Mosses, Ferns, Gymnosperms, Angiosperms.
Kingdom 5: Animalia
Eukaryotes. Multicellular. Heterotrophic. No cell wall. e.g. Sponges to Vertebrates.
∴ 5 Kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia (R.H. Whittaker, 1969)
Q.2
Distinguish between Prokaryotes and Eukaryotes.
Prokaryotes
  • No nuclear membrane (nucleoid region)
  • No membrane-bound organelles
  • Smaller ribosomes (70S)
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan
  • e.g. Bacteria, Archaebacteria, Cyanobacteria
Eukaryotes
  • Nuclear membrane present (true nucleus)
  • Membrane-bound organelles present (mitochondria, ER, Golgi)
  • Larger ribosomes (80S)
  • Cell wall of cellulose (plants) or absent (animals)
  • e.g. Protista, Fungi, Plants, Animals
Q.3
Write the characteristics and examples of Kingdom Fungi.
Characteristics
  • Eukaryotic, multicellular (except yeast — unicellular)
  • Heterotrophic — saprophytic, parasitic or symbiotic
  • Cell wall made of chitin
  • Body called mycelium (made of hyphae)
  • Reproduce by spores (sexual and asexual)
  • Store food as glycogen
Examples
Rhizopus (bread mould), Aspergillus, Penicillium, Agaricus (mushroom), Yeast (Saccharomyces)

Chapter 3 — Kingdom Plantae (Pg 19–28)
Q.1
Give the classification of Kingdom Plantae. Distinguish between Bryophyta, Pteridophyta, Gymnosperms and Angiosperms.
Bryophyta
Amphibians of plant kingdom. No vascular tissue. No true roots, stem, leaves (rhizoids, thallus). Dominant gametophyte. e.g. Funaria (moss), Riccia, Marchantia (liverwort).
Pteridophyta
First vascular plants. True roots, stem, leaves. Dominant sporophyte. No seeds. e.g. Ferns (Dryopteris), Selaginella, Equisetum (horsetail).
Gymnosperms
Naked seeds (no fruit/flower). Vascular tissue. Dominant sporophyte. e.g. Pinus, Cycas, Gnetum, Ephedra.
Angiosperms
Enclosed seeds (inside fruit). Flowers present. Most advanced. Divided into Monocots and Dicots. e.g. Mango, Wheat, Rose.

Q.2
Distinguish between Monocotyledonae and Dicotyledonae with examples.
Monocots
  • One cotyledon in seed
  • Parallel venation in leaves
  • Fibrous root system
  • Flowers trimerous (parts in 3s)
  • Vascular bundles scattered in stem
  • e.g. Wheat, Maize, Sugarcane, Onion, Grass
Dicots
  • Two cotyledons in seed
  • Reticulate (network) venation in leaves
  • Tap root system
  • Flowers tetramerous/pentamerous (parts in 4s or 5s)
  • Vascular bundles in a ring in stem
  • e.g. Mango, Rose, Pea, Sunflower, Bean

Chapter 4 — Kingdom Animalia (Pg 29–43)
Q.1
State the basis of classification of Kingdom Animalia. Define key terms.
Basis
  • Levels of organisation: cellular → tissue → organ → organ system
  • Symmetry: Asymmetrical / Radial / Bilateral
  • Coelom: Acoelomate / Pseudocoelomate / Coelomate
  • Segmentation: Segmented or unsegmented body
  • Notochord: Present or absent
  • Body wall layers: Diploblastic or Triploblastic
Coelom
True body cavity lined by mesoderm. Acoelomate: no coelom (Platyhelminthes). Pseudocoelomate: false coelom (Nematoda). Coelomate: true coelom (Annelida onwards).
Q.2
Write characteristics and examples of Phylum Porifera, Coelenterata, Platyhelminthes and Nematoda.
Porifera
Sponges. Cellular level. Asymmetrical. Pores (ostia) on body. Spicules for support. e.g. Sycon, Spongilla, Euspongia.
Coelenterata
Tissue level. Radial symmetry. Diploblastic. Cnidoblasts (nematocysts). Two forms: polyp (sessile) and medusa (free). e.g. Hydra, Jellyfish (Aurelia), Sea anemone, Coral.
Platyhelminthes
Flatworms. Organ level. Bilateral symmetry. Triploblastic. Acoelomate. Parasitic. e.g. Liver fluke (Fasciola), Tapeworm (Taenia), Planaria.
Nematoda
Roundworms. Bilateral symmetry. Pseudocoelomate. Triploblastic. Parasitic or free-living. e.g. Ascaris (roundworm), Wuchereria (filaria), Pinworm.
Q.3
Write characteristics and examples of Phylum Annelida, Arthropoda, Mollusca and Echinodermata.
Annelida
Segmented worms. Triploblastic. True coelom. Setae for locomotion. Closed circulation. e.g. Earthworm (Pheretima), Leech (Hirudo), Nereis.
Arthropoda
Largest phylum. Jointed appendages. Exoskeleton (chitin). Open circulation. e.g. Cockroach, Prawn (Palaemon), Butterfly, Scorpion, Spider.
Mollusca
Soft body. Shell present (mostly). Mantle. Radula (file-like organ) for feeding. e.g. Snail (Pila), Octopus, Squid, Chiton.
Echinodermata
Spiny skin. Radial symmetry (adult). Water vascular system. Tube feet for locomotion. e.g. Starfish (Asterias), Sea urchin, Holothurian (sea cucumber).
Q.4
Write characteristics of Phylum Chordata and its subphyla. Define Notochord.
Notochord
A flexible rod-like structure present at some stage of life. Provides support. Present in all chordates (at least in embryonic stage).
Characteristics
  • Notochord present at some stage
  • Dorsal hollow nerve cord
  • Pharyngeal gill slits
  • Post-anal tail
Subphyla
Urochordata (notochord only in larva) → Cephalochordata (notochord throughout life) → Vertebrata (notochord replaced by vertebral column).
Classes
Cyclostomata → Chondrichthyes (cartilaginous fish) → Osteichthyes (bony fish) → Amphibia → Reptilia → Aves (birds) → Mammalia

Chapter 5 — Cell Structure and Organization (Pg 44–58)
Q.1
State the cell theory. Who proposed it?
Cell Theory
  • Proposed by Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839)
  • All living organisms are made of cells
  • Cell is the structural and functional unit of life
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells (added by Virchow, 1855)
Exceptions
Viruses — not made of cells. First cell — arose from non-living matter (abiogenesis).
Q.2
Describe the structure and functions of the following organelles: Mitochondria, Chloroplast, Endoplasmic Reticulum, Golgi apparatus, Ribosome, Lysosome.
Mitochondria
Double membrane (inner = cristae). Matrix contains enzymes. Site of aerobic respiration (ATP production). Called "powerhouse of cell". Has own DNA and ribosomes (70S).
Chloroplast
Double membrane. Thylakoid (grana) + stroma. Site of photosynthesis. Has chlorophyll. Has own DNA, ribosomes. Called "kitchen of cell".
ER (Endoplasmic Reticulum)
Network of membranes. RER (with ribosomes) — protein synthesis. SER (without ribosomes) — lipid synthesis, detoxification. Connected to nuclear envelope.
Golgi Body
Stack of cisternae (cis → trans). Processes and packages proteins. Forms lysosomes and secretory vesicles. Called "traffic police of cell".
Ribosome
80S (eukaryotes: 60S+40S); 70S (prokaryotes: 50S+30S). Site of protein synthesis. Made of rRNA + proteins.
Lysosome
Single membrane. Contains hydrolytic enzymes. Intracellular digestion. Called "suicidal bags" (autolysis). Formed by Golgi apparatus.

Q.3
Describe the structure of nucleus. State its functions.
Structure
  • Nuclear envelope: Double membrane with nuclear pores
  • Nucleoplasm: Fluid-filled interior
  • Nucleolus: Dense body — site of rRNA synthesis; disappears during cell division
  • Chromatin: DNA + histone proteins. Condenses to form chromosomes during division
Functions
  • Controls all cell activities (metabolic activities)
  • Stores genetic information (DNA)
  • Controls cell division
  • Regulates gene expression (transcription)
∴ Nucleus is the "control centre" of the cell.

Chapter 7 — Cell Division (Pg 76–84)
Q.1
Describe the different phases of Mitosis (Karyokinesis) with diagrams.
Mitosis
Type of cell division resulting in two daughter cells with same chromosome number as parent (2n → 2n). For growth and repair.
Prophase
Chromatin condenses into chromosomes. Centrioles move to poles. Spindle fibres form. Nuclear membrane and nucleolus disappear.
Metaphase
Chromosomes align at equatorial plate (metaphase plate). Spindle fibres attach to centromeres. Chromosomes clearly visible — best stage to count chromosomes.
Anaphase
Centromeres split. Sister chromatids (now chromosomes) pulled to opposite poles by spindle fibres. V, J or L shape. Cell elongates.
Telophase
Chromosomes reach poles. Uncoil to form chromatin. Nuclear membrane reforms. Nucleolus reappears. Spindle disappears.
Cytokinesis
Division of cytoplasm. In animals: cleavage furrow. In plants: cell plate forms.

Q.2
Describe the phases of Meiosis. What is significance of meiosis?
Meiosis
Two successive divisions resulting in 4 haploid cells from one diploid cell (2n → n). Occurs in gonads (gamete formation).
Meiosis I (Reductional)
  • Prophase I: Longest phase. Synapsis (homologous chromosomes pair = bivalent). Crossing over occurs (chiasmata). Genetic recombination!
  • Metaphase I: Bivalents align at equatorial plate.
  • Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate to poles (centromere does NOT split). Chromosome number halved.
  • Telophase I: Two haploid cells formed.
Meiosis II (Equational)
Similar to mitosis. Centromeres split. Each haploid cell divides to give 2 cells. Final result: 4 haploid cells.
Significance
  • Maintains chromosome number constant in sexually reproducing organisms
  • Introduces genetic variation through crossing over
  • Produces gametes for sexual reproduction
∴ Meiosis I = reductional (2n→n); Meiosis II = equational (n→n); Final: 4 haploid cells

Q.4
Define cell cycle. Explain interphase and its sub-phases (G1, S, G2).
Cell Cycle
The series of events that take place in a cell from one cell division to the next. Consists of: Interphase + M phase (mitosis/meiosis)
Interphase
Longest phase (90% of cell cycle). Cell prepares for division. 3 sub-phases:
G₁ phase
Gap 1 / Post-mitotic phase: Cell grows in size. Proteins and RNA synthesized. Organelles duplicate. Cell checks if conditions are right for division.
S phase
Synthesis phase: DNA replication occurs. Chromosome number remains same but DNA amount doubles (2C → 4C).
G₂ phase
Gap 2 / Pre-mitotic phase: Cell continues to grow. Tubulin (for spindle) synthesized. Cell gets ready for division.
∴ Cell Cycle: G₁ → S → G₂ → M phase → G₁ (repeats)
