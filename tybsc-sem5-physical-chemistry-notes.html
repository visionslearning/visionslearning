📡 Chapter 1 – Molecular Spectroscopy
1.1 Dipole Moment & Molecular Structure

What is Dipole Moment?

Dipole moment (μ) is a measure of the polarity of a bond or molecule. It is the product of the charge (q) and the distance (d) between the charges.

μ = q × d    Unit: Debye (D)

A molecule has a net dipole moment only if the individual bond dipoles do not cancel each other vectorially.

Important Bond Moments (Table 1.1.2)

BondBond Moment (D)BondBond Moment (D)
H–F1.9H–C0.4
H–Cl1.1C–F1.5
H–Br0.8C–Cl1.5
H–O1.5C–Br1.4
H–N1.3C=O2.3
C–O0.7C≡N1.2

Key Molecules & Their Structures

  • CO₂ (μ = 0): Linear structure — two C=O bond moments cancel each other.
  • SO₂ (μ = 1.63 D): Angular structure — bond moments do NOT cancel; net dipole exists.
  • BF₃ (μ = 0): Planar triangular — three B–F bond moments arranged symmetrically cancel.
  • NH₃ (μ = 1.47 D): Pyramidal structure — bond moments + lone pair contribute to net dipole.
  • H₂O (μ = 1.85 D): Angular structure — two O–H bond moments give net dipole.
  • CH₄ (μ = 0): Tetrahedral — all C–H bonds cancel symmetrically.
💡 Exam Trick: If a molecule is symmetrical → μ = 0. If unsymmetrical → μ ≠ 0.

Dipole Moments of Disubstituted Benzenes (o, m, p)

For ortho, meta, and para isomers of dichlorobenzene:

μ_resultant = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cosθ)
ortho (θ=60°): μ = 2.6 D
meta (θ=120°): μ = 1.5 D
para (θ=180°): μ = 0 D
💡 This formula is used to identify isomers — a very common exam numerical!
1.2 Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy

Types of Molecular Vibrations

A. Stretching Vibrations — change in bond length (along bond axis):

  • Symmetric stretching: Both atoms move away from centre simultaneously.
  • Asymmetric stretching: One atom moves toward centre, other moves away.

B. Bending Vibrations — change in bond angle:

  • In-plane: Rocking, Scissoring
  • Out-of-plane: Wagging, Twisting
💡 Stretching requires MORE energy than bending.

Stretching Frequency Formula

ν̃ = (1/2πc) √(k/μ)

where: k = force constant (bond strength)
μ = reduced mass = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)
c = speed of light

Stronger bonds → higher frequency. Heavier atoms → lower frequency.

Characteristic Group Frequencies (Table 1.1.7)

Fingerprint Region

The spectral region 900–1400 cm⁻¹ is called the fingerprint region. It is unique for each molecule. No two different compounds show the same absorption pattern in this region. Used for identifying unknown compounds.

Isotopic Effect in IR

Replacing a lighter atom with a heavier isotope lowers the stretching frequency (since μ increases).

ν̃(H–X) / ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX / μ_HX) = √2
e.g., O–H absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹, O–D at ~2630 cm⁻¹
1.3 Microwave (Rotational) Spectra & Rigid Rotor

Conditions for Microwave Absorption

Only molecules with a permanent dipole moment can absorb microwave radiation and give rotational spectra. (e.g., HCl, H₂O — YES; N₂, CO₂ — NO)

Rigid Rotor Model

A diatomic molecule is treated as a rigid rotor (two masses joined by a rigid bond). It can rotate about:

  • Bond axis
  • Vertical plane through centre of gravity
  • Horizontal plane
Moment of Inertia: I = μr²
where r = internuclear distance, μ = reduced mass

Vibration-Rotation Spectra & P/R Branch

In a combined vibrational-rotational spectrum:

  • P-branch: ΔJ = –1 (lower frequency side)
  • R-branch: ΔJ = +1 (higher frequency side)
  • Q-branch: ΔJ = 0 (not always observed)

Lines are separated by a spacing of 2B.

B = h / (8π²Ic)
1.4 Molecular Polarisation & Clausius-Mosotti Equation

Types of Polarisation

When a molecule is placed in an electric field, it gets polarised. The total molar polarisation (PT) has three contributions:

PT = Pe + Pa + Po

Pe = Electronic polarisation (due to displacement of electron cloud)
Pa = Atomic / Ionic polarisation (due to displacement of atoms/ions)
Po = Orientation polarisation (due to alignment of permanent dipoles — polar molecules only)
  • Non-polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa (no orientation polarisation)
  • Polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa + Po
💡 Pe and Pa are temperature-independent. Po is temperature-dependent (decreases as T increases).

Clausius-Mosotti Equation

For non-polar molecules (no orientation polarisation), the Clausius-Mosotti equation relates molar polarisation to dielectric constant (εr):

PM = [(εr − 1) / (εr + 2)] × (M / ρ) = (4/3)πNAα

where: εr = dielectric constant (relative permittivity)
M = molecular weight, ρ = density
NA = Avogadro number, α = polarisability

This equation is valid for non-polar molecules only (in the absence of permanent dipoles).

Debye Equation (for Polar Molecules)

Debye extended the Clausius-Mosotti equation to include orientation polarisation for polar molecules:

PT = Po + Pe + Pa

PT = (4/3)πNAα + NAμ² / (3kT)

where: μ = permanent dipole moment
k = Boltzmann constant, T = absolute temperature
α = polarisability

Rearranging (Debye equation):

PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA / 3kT

This is of the form: PT = A + B/T
A = (4/3)πNAα (temperature-independent)
B = μ²NA/3k (gives dipole moment when slope is known)

Plot PT vs 1/T → straight line. Slope = μ²NA/3k → dipole moment (μ) can be calculated.

💡 This is the Debye method for experimental determination of dipole moment!

1.5 Raman Spectroscopy

Raman Effect — Discovery

Discovered by Prof. C.V. Raman in 1928. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1930. When monochromatic light is scattered by a sample, most scattered light has the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering) but a small portion has different frequency — this is the Raman Effect.

Stokes & Anti-Stokes Lines

TypeConditionFrequencyIntensity
RayleighElastic scatteringν₀ (unchanged)Highest (99%)
Stokesν_s < ν₀ν₀ − ΔνStronger
Anti-Stokesν_as > ν₀ν₀ + ΔνWeaker
💡 Stokes lines are more intense because more molecules are in the ground state at room temperature.

Raman Activity Rule

A vibration is Raman active if there is a change in polarisability of the molecule during vibration.

A vibration is IR active if there is a change in dipole moment.

💡 Mutual Exclusion Rule: In centrosymmetric molecules, IR active vibrations are Raman inactive and vice versa (e.g., CO₂).

Applications of Raman Spectroscopy

  • Study of gases, liquids, and solids
  • Complementary to IR — gives information IR cannot
  • Useful for molecules with no dipole moment (N₂, O₂)
  • Combined with IR spectra gives complete molecular structure

⭐ Chapter 1 – Important Exam Q&A

Q1. What is dipole moment? How is it used to determine the structure of a molecule? 5 Marks
Dipole Moment (μ): It is the product of the magnitude of the charge (q) and the distance (d) between them.
μ = q × d   (Unit: Debye, 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m)
Use in determining molecular structure:
  • If μ = 0 → molecule is symmetrical (e.g., CO₂ is linear, BF₃ is planar)
  • If μ ≠ 0 → molecule is unsymmetrical (e.g., SO₂ is angular, NH₃ is pyramidal)
  • Used to distinguish cis/trans isomers: cis-isomer has higher μ than trans-isomer
  • Used to identify ortho, meta, para isomers of benzene derivatives
Q2. Explain with example why CO₂ has zero dipole moment but SO₂ has 1.63 D. 4 Marks
CO₂ (μ = 0): Carbon dioxide has a linear structure (O=C=O). The two C=O bond moments are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction — they cancel each other vectorially → net μ = 0.

SO₂ (μ = 1.63 D): Sulphur dioxide has an angular (bent) structure due to the lone pair on sulphur. The two S=O bond moments do not cancel because of the angular geometry → net dipole moment = 1.63 D.
Q3. Explain the identification of ortho, meta, and para isomers using dipole moment. 5 Marks
For disubstituted benzene (e.g., dichlorobenzene):
μ_net = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cosθ)
Since both substituents are same (μ₁ = μ₂ = μ):
μ_net = μ√(2 + 2cosθ) = 2μ cos(θ/2)
  • ortho (θ = 60°): μ = 2.6 D (cos30° = √3/2)
  • meta (θ = 120°): μ = 1.5 D (cos60° = 0.5)
  • para (θ = 180°): μ = 0 D (cos90° = 0)
Calculated values match experimental values, hence dipole moment is a reliable tool for identification.
Q4. Explain the types of molecular vibrations with diagrams. 6 Marks
A. Stretching Vibrations — Change in bond length:
  • Symmetric stretching: Both atoms move away from central atom simultaneously
  • Asymmetric stretching: One moves in, other moves out
B. Bending Vibrations — Change in bond angle:
  • In-plane:
    • Scissoring: Two atoms approach each other in same plane
    • Rocking: Group oscillates within plane
  • Out-of-plane:
    • Wagging: Both atoms move in & out of plane in same direction
    • Twisting: Both atoms move in & out of plane in opposite directions
Degrees of Freedom: For non-linear molecule with N atoms = 3N-6 vibrational modes; for linear = 3N-5.
Q5. What is the fingerprint region in IR spectroscopy? What is its significance? 3 Marks
The region 900–1400 cm⁻¹ in an IR spectrum is called the fingerprint region. It contains a large number of absorption bands due to complex vibrations of the entire molecule.

Significance:
  • Unique for every compound — like a fingerprint
  • No two different compounds show the same absorption pattern
  • Used for identification and comparison of compounds
  • Slight change in molecular structure causes a remarkable change in this region
Q6. Explain Raman Effect. What are Stokes and Anti-Stokes lines? 6 Marks
Raman Effect (discovered by C.V. Raman, 1928): When monochromatic light is incident on a substance, most scattered light has the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering). A small fraction (~1%) has different frequency — this is called the Raman Effect.

Stokes Lines: Scattered light has lower frequency than incident light (ν_s < ν₀). Molecule gains energy. These lines appear on the low-frequency side.

Anti-Stokes Lines: Scattered light has higher frequency than incident light (ν_as > ν₀). Molecule loses energy. These are weaker and appear on the high-frequency side.

Raman Shift (Δν): Δν = ν₀ − ν_s = ν_as − ν₀ (same magnitude, opposite sides)
Q7. Explain the isotopic effect in IR spectroscopy with an example. 4 Marks
Replacing a lighter atom with its heavier isotope increases the reduced mass (μ) of the bond. Since stretching frequency is inversely proportional to √μ, the frequency decreases.
ν̃ = (1/2πc) √(k/μ)
Example: O–H bond absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹. When H is replaced by D (deuterium):
ν̃(H–X) / ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX / μ_HX) ≈ √2 ≈ 1.414
So O–D absorbs at ~2630 cm⁻¹ (lower frequency). This isotopic shift is used to confirm presence of O–H or N–H groups.
Q8. State the condition for a molecule to show (a) IR spectrum (b) Raman spectrum. 3 Marks
  • (a) IR Active: A vibration is IR active if it involves a change in the electric dipole moment of the molecule. Only polar molecules or molecules with polar bonds that undergo asymmetric vibrations show IR absorption.
  • (b) Raman Active: A vibration is Raman active if it involves a change in the polarisability of the molecule.
Mutual Exclusion Rule: For centrosymmetric molecules (e.g., CO₂), vibrations that are IR active are Raman inactive and vice versa.
Q9. Explain the Debye equation. How is it used to determine dipole moment experimentally? 5 Marks
Debye Equation: Debye extended the Clausius-Mosotti equation to include orientation polarisation for polar molecules:
PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA / 3kT
This can be written as: PT = A + B/T, where:
  • A = (4/3)πNAα (temperature-independent, from induced polarisation)
  • B = μ²NA/3k (orientation contribution)
Experimental Determination of μ:
  • Measure total molar polarisation PT at several different temperatures
  • Plot PT vs 1/T → straight line
  • Slope = μ²NA/3k → rearrange to find μ
  • Intercept = (4/3)πNAα → gives polarisability α
μ = √(3k × Slope / NA)
Q10. What are the different types of molecular polarisation? Explain each briefly. 4 Marks
Total molar polarisation: PT = Pe + Pa + Po

(i) Electronic Polarisation (Pe): When an electric field is applied, the electron cloud of the atom is displaced relative to the nucleus. Present in ALL molecules. Temperature-independent.

(ii) Atomic/Ionic Polarisation (Pa): Displacement of atoms or ions relative to each other within a molecule under an electric field. Temperature-independent.

(iii) Orientation Polarisation (Po): Polar molecules have permanent dipoles. In an electric field, these dipoles align along the field direction. This is temperature-dependent — at higher temperatures, thermal energy opposes alignment → Po decreases.

Non-polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa only. Polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa + Po.
🌡️ Chapter 2 – Chemical Thermodynamics: Colligative Properties

What are Colligative Properties?

Colligative properties are properties of solutions that depend only on the number of solute particles dissolved, not on the nature or identity of the solute.

Four Colligative Properties:

  • 1. Lowering of vapour pressure
  • 2. Elevation of boiling point
  • 3. Depression of freezing point
  • 4. Osmotic pressure
💡 All four depend only on the number of moles of solute, not the type of solute.
2.1 Lowering of Vapour Pressure – Raoult's Law

Vapour Pressure of a Liquid

The pressure exerted by vapour above a liquid in equilibrium with the liquid at a given temperature is called vapour pressure.

Factors affecting VP:

  • Nature of liquid: Weaker intermolecular forces → higher VP (more volatile)
  • Temperature: Increases with temperature

Raoult's Law

For a solution of a non-volatile solute in a solvent:

p = p° × x₁   (where x₁ = mole fraction of solvent)

Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure:
(p° – p) / p° = x₂   (where x₂ = mole fraction of solute)

This is called Raoult's Law — the relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the mole fraction of the solute.

Mole Fraction & Molecular Weight Determination

x₂ = n₂ / (n₁ + n₂)
For dilute solution: (p° – p) / p° ≈ n₂/n₁ = (W₂/M₂) / (W₁/M₁)

∴ M₂ = (W₂ × M₁ × p°) / (W₁ × (p° – p))

This formula is used to determine molecular weight of solute from VP measurements.

2.2 Elevation of Boiling Point & Depression of Freezing Point

Elevation of Boiling Point (ΔT_b)

Addition of non-volatile solute lowers VP → solution needs higher temperature to boil → boiling point is elevated.

ΔT_b = K_b × m
where K_b = ebullioscopic / boiling point elevation constant
m = molality of solution

K_b = (RT_b² × M₁) / (1000 × l_v)
where l_v = latent heat of vaporization per gram

Depression of Freezing Point (ΔT_f)

Solute lowers the vapour pressure → solid and liquid phases cannot be in equilibrium at the normal freezing point → freezing point is depressed.

ΔT_f = K_f × m
where K_f = cryoscopic constant (molal depression constant)
m = molality of solution

K_f = (RT_f² × M₁) / (1000 × l_f)
where l_f = latent heat of fusion per gram of solvent

Determination of Molecular Weight (Rast Method)

The Rast method uses camphor as solvent (K_f = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹ — very high). Steps:

  • Known mass of solute (W₂) is dissolved in known mass of camphor (W₁)
  • Melting point depression (ΔT_f) is measured
  • Molecular weight is calculated:
M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)

Beckmann Method uses a thermometer that can be set at any temperature range (not just fixed points).

2.3 Osmotic Pressure & Van't Hoff's Equation

Osmosis & Osmotic Pressure

Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane (SPM) from dilute solution to concentrated solution (or from pure solvent to solution).

Osmotic Pressure (π): The minimum pressure that must be applied to the solution side to stop the flow of osmosis.

💡 Example: NaCl solution separated from pure water by SPM — water flows towards NaCl solution. To stop this, pressure equal to osmotic pressure must be applied on the NaCl side.

Van't Hoff's Laws of Osmotic Pressure

Boyle's Law: At constant temperature, osmotic pressure varies inversely with volume:

π ∝ 1/V   →   πV = constant

Charles' Law: At constant volume, osmotic pressure varies directly with temperature:

π ∝ T   →   π/T = constant

Van't Hoff's Equation: (analogous to ideal gas law)

πV = nRT
π = (n/V)RT = CRT
πV = (W/M)RT   →   M = WRT/πV

Used to determine molecular weight of solute from osmotic pressure measurements.

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

If pressure applied on solution side is greater than osmotic pressure, solvent molecules flow from solution to pure solvent — this is Reverse Osmosis.

  • Feed water: The solution from which pure water is to be extracted
  • Permeate: Purified/desalinated water obtained
  • Reject stream: Concentrated salt-containing water that doesn't pass through RO membrane
💡 RO is the basis of water purifiers used in homes!

Van't Hoff Factor (i) – For Electrolytes

Electrolytes dissociate in solution → more particles → greater colligative effect. The Van't Hoff factor (i) accounts for dissociation:

i = (Observed colligative property) / (Calculated colligative property assuming no dissociation)

Modified equations:
ΔT_b = i × K_b × m
ΔT_f = i × K_f × m
π = i × CRT

For NaCl → KCl → i ≈ 2; For BaCl₂ → i ≈ 3; For non-electrolytes → i = 1

⭐ Chapter 2 – Important Exam Q&A

Q1. State and explain Raoult's Law. Derive an expression for relative lowering of vapour pressure. 6 Marks
Raoult's Law: The vapour pressure of a solvent above a solution is equal to the product of the vapour pressure of the pure solvent and the mole fraction of the solvent in the solution.
p = p° × x₁
Derivation of Relative Lowering:
Since x₁ + x₂ = 1 → x₁ = 1 − x₂
p = p°(1 − x₂) = p° − p°x₂
∴ p° − p = p°x₂
∴ (p° − p)/p° = x₂ = relative lowering of VP
This shows that relative lowering of VP = mole fraction of solute (x₂), which depends only on the number of solute particles — hence it is a colligative property.
Q2. Define osmosis and osmotic pressure. Give Van't Hoff's equation for osmotic pressure and its derivation. 6 Marks
Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution (or from pure solvent to solution).

Osmotic Pressure (π): Minimum pressure applied on the solution to stop osmosis when separated from pure solvent by a SPM.

Van't Hoff's Equation Derivation:
  • Van't Hoff Boyle's Law: πV = constant (at constant T)
  • Van't Hoff Charles' Law: π/T = constant (at constant V)
  • Combining: πV = RT per mole
  • For n moles in volume V: πV = nRT
πV = nRT   →   π = CRT   →   M = WRT/πV
This equation is similar to the ideal gas equation PV = nRT, hence osmotic pressure behaves like gas pressure.
Q3. What is the depression of freezing point? Derive the expression for K_f (cryoscopic constant). 6 Marks
Depression of Freezing Point: When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, the freezing point of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent. The decrease is called depression of freezing point (ΔT_f).
ΔT_f = K_f × m
Derivation of K_f:
From thermodynamic derivation using Clausius-Clapeyron equation:
K_f = RT_f² / (1000 × l_f) × M₁
where:
  • T_f = freezing point of pure solvent (in Kelvin)
  • l_f = latent heat of fusion per gram of solvent
  • M₁ = molecular weight of solvent
  • R = gas constant
Molar depression constant: K_f is defined as the depression in freezing point when 1 mole of solute is dissolved in 1 kg of solvent.
Q4. Describe the Beckmann method for determination of molecular weight by depression of freezing point. 5 Marks
Apparatus: Consists of a freezing tube with a Beckmann thermometer and stirrer, placed inside an outer vessel (cooling bath) containing a freezing mixture.

Procedure:
  • Known weight (W₁) of pure solvent is taken in the freezing tube
  • Cooled to freezing point (T_f°) of pure solvent — noted
  • Known weight (W₂) of solute is added and dissolved
  • Solution is cooled to new freezing point (T_f) — noted
  • ΔT_f = T_f° − T_f is calculated
Calculation:
M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)
Rast Method: Uses camphor as solvent (K_f = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹). Very large K_f allows small depression to be accurately measured even for small amounts of solute.
Q5. Explain Reverse Osmosis. What are its applications? 4 Marks
Reverse Osmosis (RO): When pressure greater than osmotic pressure is applied on the solution side, solvent molecules are forced to flow from the solution through the SPM to the pure solvent side. This reversal of normal osmosis is called Reverse Osmosis.

Key Terms:
  • Feed water = the solution from which pure water is extracted
  • Permeate = purified/desalinated water obtained
  • Reject stream = concentrated brine/salt water left behind
Applications:
  • Desalination of seawater
  • Purification of drinking water (household RO purifiers)
  • Removal of heavy metals, bacteria, salts from water
  • Industrial wastewater treatment
Q6. Explain Van't Hoff factor. How does it modify colligative property equations for electrolytes? 4 Marks
Van't Hoff Factor (i): Electrolytes dissociate in solution, producing more particles than expected. The Van't Hoff factor accounts for this:
i = Total moles of particles in solution / Moles of solute dissolved
Modified colligative equations:
ΔT_b = i × K_b × m
ΔT_f = i × K_f × m
Δp/p° = i × x₂
π = i × CRT
Examples:
  • Non-electrolyte (glucose): i = 1
  • NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻: i = 2
  • BaCl₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻: i = 3
  • K₂SO₄ → 2K⁺ + SO₄²⁻: i = 3
Q7. What is vapour pressure of a liquid? Explain the factors affecting vapour pressure. 4 Marks
Vapour Pressure: The pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid above its surface when liquid and vapour are in dynamic equilibrium at a given temperature.

Factors affecting VP:
  • Nature of liquid: Liquids with weak intermolecular forces (e.g., diethyl ether) have high VP; those with strong forces (e.g., water, glycerol) have low VP.
  • Temperature: VP increases with temperature as more molecules gain enough kinetic energy to escape into the vapour phase.
  • Presence of solute: Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the VP of the solvent (Raoult's Law).
Q8. A solution of 0.033g of solute in 0.450g of camphor depresses freezing point by 17.7 K. Calculate molecular weight. (K_f of camphor = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹) Numerical
Given:
  • K_f = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹
  • W₂ (solute) = 0.033 g
  • W₁ (camphor) = 0.450 g
  • ΔT_f = 17.7 K
Formula:
M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)
Calculation:
M₂ = (1000 × 37.7 × 0.033) / (17.7 × 0.450) = 1244.1 / 7.965 ≈ 156 g·mol⁻¹
Answer: M₂ = 156 g·mol⁻¹
