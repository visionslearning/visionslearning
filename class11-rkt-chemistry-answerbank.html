Q1.1
The maximum number of electrons in the M shell (n = 3) is ________. (Ch4)
Answer: (c) 18 — Max electrons = 2n² = 2 × 3² = 18.
Q1.2
The geometry of CH₄ molecule according to VSEPR theory is ________. (Ch4/Ch14)
Answer: (b) Tetrahedral — Carbon has 4 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs → regular tetrahedral, bond angle = 109.5°.
Q1.3
The empirical formula of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is ________. (Ch1)
Answer: (b) CH₂O — Divide each subscript by 6 (HCF): C:H:O = 1:2:1 → CH₂O.
Q1.4
The general formula of alkanes is ________. (Ch15)
Answer: (a) CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ — Saturated hydrocarbons (all single bonds). E.g., n=1: CH₄, n=2: C₂H₆.

Q2 — Very Short Answer (4 × 1 = 4 Marks)
Q2.1
State Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle. (Ch4 — 4.5.2)
It is impossible to simultaneously and precisely determine both the position and momentum of a subatomic particle. Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π where Δx = uncertainty in position, Δp = uncertainty in momentum, h = Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s).
Q2.2
Define molar mass and give its unit. (Ch1)
The mass of one mole (6.022 × 10²³ particles) of a substance expressed in grams is called molar mass. Unit: g/mol (or g mol⁻¹). It is numerically equal to the molecular/atomic mass in u.
Q2.3
What is a functional group? Give one example. (Ch14 — 14.5.1)
A functional group is an atom or group of atoms present in an organic molecule that is responsible for its characteristic chemical properties and reactions. Example: –OH (hydroxyl group) in alcohols; –COOH (carboxyl) in acids.
Q2.4
Write the molecular formula of butane (n = 4 for alkane). (Ch15)
General formula of alkane: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
n = 4: C₄H₂(4)+2 = C₄H₁₀
Butane = C₄H₁₀
Structure: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (n-butane)

Q3
Calculate the percentage composition of H₂SO₄. (H=1, S=32, O=16) (3M)
Molar mass of H₂SO₄ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98 g/mol
% H = (2/98) × 100 = 2.04%
% S = (32/98) × 100 = 32.65%
% O = (64/98) × 100 = 65.31%
Total = 2.04 + 32.65 + 65.31 = 100% ✓
Q4
A compound contains 40% C, 6.67% H and 53.33% O. Find its empirical formula. (C=12, H=1, O=16) (3M)
Mole ratio — C: 40/12 = 3.33 | H: 6.67/1 = 6.67 | O: 53.33/16 = 3.33
Divide by smallest (3.33): C:1 H:2 O:1
Empirical formula = CH₂O
(This is the empirical formula of glucose, acetic acid, formaldehyde etc.)

Q6
Calculate the molarity of a solution prepared by dissolving 4 g of NaOH in 500 mL water. (Na=23, O=16, H=1) (3M)
M(NaOH) = 23+16+1 = 40 g/mol
n = 4/40 = 0.1 mol
V = 500 mL = 0.5 L
M = n/V = 0.1/0.5
Molarity = 0.2 mol/L = 0.2 M
Q7
Define: (i) Molarity (ii) Normality (iii) Molality. Give the formula for each. (3M)
  • Molarity (M): Moles of solute per litre of solution. M = n/V(L) Unit: mol/L
  • Normality (N): Gram equivalents of solute per litre of solution. N = gram equivalents/V(L) = M × n-factor Unit: N (eq/L)
  • Molality (m): Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. m = n/W_solvent(kg) Unit: mol/kg
Key difference: Molarity depends on volume (temperature-dependent); molality depends on mass (temperature-independent).
Q8
For the reaction 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, if 4 g H₂ reacts with 32 g O₂, identify the limiting reagent and find the mass of water formed. (H=1, O=16) (3M)
Moles H₂ = 4/2 = 2 mol; Moles O₂ = 32/32 = 1 mol
Stoichiometry: 2 mol H₂ needs 1 mol O₂ ✓ (exactly stoichiometric)
Both are completely consumed — neither is in excess.
H₂O formed = 2 mol × 18 g/mol
Mass of H₂O = 36 g; Both are limiting reagents

Chapter 4 — Structure of Atom (Sec B) — Topics 4.1.1 to 4.7.6
Q9
State and explain Bohr's postulates for the hydrogen atom. (4.1.1, 4.1.2) (3M)
  • Postulate 1: Electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular paths (orbits/shells) without losing energy. These are called stationary states or allowed orbits.
  • Postulate 2: Only orbits in which angular momentum (mvr) is an integral multiple of h/2π are allowed: mvr = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3… = principal quantum number)
  • Postulate 3: Energy is absorbed or emitted only when an electron jumps from one orbit to another: ΔE = E₂ – E₁ = hν
  • The orbit with n=1 is the ground state; higher n = higher energy = larger orbit.
Q10
State Aufbau principle, Pauli's exclusion principle and Hund's rule with examples. (4.5.1, 4.7.2, 4.7.3) (3M)
  • Aufbau Principle (4.7.2): Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy. (n+l) rule: lower n+l fills first; equal n+l → lower n first. Order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p...
  • Pauli's Exclusion Principle (4.5.1): No two electrons in the same atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. Each orbital accommodates max 2 electrons with opposite spins (↑↓).
  • Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity (4.7.3): In degenerate orbitals (same energy), electrons first occupy each orbital singly with parallel spin (↑↑) before pairing. Example: Nitrogen (Z=7): 1s² 2s² 2p³ → three 2p orbitals each have one ↑ electron before any pairing.

Chapter 4 — Structure of Atom (Section C)
Q14
Explain de Broglie's hypothesis. Write the formula and give its significance. (4.5.2) (3M)
de Broglie's Hypothesis (1924):
  • All moving particles (not just light) exhibit dual nature — both particle and wave characteristics.
  • Every moving particle of mass m moving with velocity v has an associated wavelength: λ = h/mv = h/p where h = Planck's constant, p = mv = momentum.
  • Larger the mass → smaller the wavelength (negligible for macroscopic objects). Electrons have significant wave character.
Significance:
  • Explains why electrons occupy only specific orbits in Bohr's model (standing wave condition: 2πr = nλ).
  • Foundation of quantum mechanics and wave mechanical model.
  • Verified experimentally by Davisson and Germer (electron diffraction, 1927).
Q15
What are the drawbacks of Rutherford's model? How did Bohr's model overcome them? (4.1.3) (3M)
Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model:
  • 1. Instability: Accelerating electron should radiate energy continuously → spiral into nucleus → atom should collapse. But atoms are stable.
  • 2. Line spectra: Cannot explain why atoms emit discrete spectral lines instead of a continuous spectrum.
  • 3. No electron arrangement: Did not specify how electrons are distributed around nucleus.
Bohr's Model overcomes:
  • 1. Proposed stationary orbits — electrons do not radiate energy while in fixed orbits → atom is stable.
  • 2. Explained hydrogen spectrum — emission occurs only during electron transitions between orbits → discrete lines (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen series).
  • 3. Defined allowed orbits by quantisation condition: mvr = nh/2π.

Q17
Explain homolytic and heterolytic bond cleavage with one example each. (14.6.1) (3M)
Homolytic Cleavage:
  • The bond breaks so that each fragment gets one electron from the shared pair → forms free radicals (neutral species with unpaired electron).
  • A:B → A• + B•
  • Example: Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl• (under UV light)
  • Occurs in non-polar conditions; leads to free radical reactions.
Heterolytic Cleavage:
  • The bond breaks so that both electrons go to ONE fragment → forms ions (carbocation or carbanion).
  • A:B → A⁺ + :B⁻ (or A:⁻ + B⁺)
  • Example: CH₃–Cl → CH₃⁺ (carbocation) + Cl⁻
  • Occurs in polar conditions; leads to ionic reactions (SN1, SN2, etc.).
Q18
What are electrophiles and nucleophiles? Explain inductive effect briefly. (14.6.2, 14.6.4) (3M)
Electrophile: Electron-deficient species that accepts an electron pair. Examples: H⁺, Cl⁺, NO₂⁺, BF₃, AlCl₃, carbocations (R⁺).
Nucleophile: Electron-rich species that donates an electron pair. Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O, carbanions (R⁻).

Inductive Effect:
  • Permanent displacement of electron density along a σ-bond chain due to electronegativity difference between atoms.
  • –I effect (electron withdrawing): electronegative groups (–Cl, –NO₂, –CN) pull electrons towards them.
  • +I effect (electron donating): alkyl groups (–CH₃, –C₂H₅) push electrons away.
  • The effect decreases with distance (significant only up to 2–3 carbons).
  • Example: In CH₃–CH₂–Cl, Cl pulls electron density → C1 is δ⁺, C2 is slightly δ⁺.

Chapter 15 — Alkanes (Section C) — 15.1.1 to 15.1.6
Q19
Describe the preparation of alkanes by: (i) Catalytic hydrogenation (ii) Wurtz reaction. Give equations. (15.1.3) (3M)
(i) Catalytic Hydrogenation of Alkenes/Alkynes (15.1.3):
  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons react with H₂ in presence of Ni/Pt/Pd catalyst under heat/pressure → saturated alkane.
  • CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → (Ni, Δ) → CH₃–CH₃ (ethene → ethane)
  • CH≡CH + 2H₂ → (Ni, Δ) → CH₃–CH₃ (ethyne → ethane)
(ii) Wurtz Reaction (15.1.3):
  • Two moles of alkyl halide react with sodium metal in dry ether to give a higher alkane.
  • 2R–X + 2Na → (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX
  • Example: 2CH₃Br + 2Na → (dry ether) → CH₃–CH₃ + 2NaBr
  • Limitation: Only useful for symmetric alkanes (both halides same); mixed gives mixture.

Section D — Long Answer Questions (4 marks each)
Q20
Describe Bohr's atomic model in detail. Derive the expression for radius of nth orbit and energy of electron in nth orbit for hydrogen. (4.1.1–4.1.3, 4.2) (4M)
Bohr's Model — Postulates:
  • 1. Fixed circular orbits (stationary states); electrons don't radiate energy.
  • 2. mvr = nh/2π (angular momentum quantisation).
  • 3. ΔE = hν (energy emitted/absorbed on orbit change).
Radius of nth orbit:
  • Centripetal force = Electrostatic force: mv²/r = Ze²/4πε₀r²
  • From angular momentum condition: v = nh/2πmr
  • Substituting and solving: rₙ = n²a₀/Z where a₀ = 0.529 Å (Bohr radius for H, n=1, Z=1)
  • r₁ (H) = 0.529 Å; r₂ = 4×0.529 = 2.116 Å
Energy of electron in nth orbit:
  • Total energy = KE + PE = –13.6 Z²/n² eV (for hydrogen-like atoms)
  • Eₙ = –13.6/n² eV (for H, Z=1)
  • E₁ = –13.6 eV (ground state); E₂ = –3.4 eV; E₃ = –1.51 eV
  • Negative energy = electron is bound to nucleus; energy increases (becomes less negative) with n.
Limitations: Cannot explain multi-electron spectra, Zeeman effect, dual nature.
Q21
Explain VSEPR theory with examples. Predict shapes of NH₃, H₂O, BF₃ and CO₂. (Ch 4 / Ch 14 bonding) (4M)
VSEPR Theory:
  • Electron pairs (bond pairs + lone pairs) around the central atom repel each other and adopt positions that minimise repulsion → determines molecular shape.
  • Repulsion order: LP–LP > LP–BP > BP–BP

Q22
Explain isomerism in organic compounds. Define chain, position and functional group isomerism with examples. (14.6.5) (4M)
Isomerism: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae are called isomers; the phenomenon is isomerism.

1. Chain Isomerism (Skeletal):
  • Different arrangement of carbon skeleton (branching).
  • Example (C₅H₁₂): n-pentane (straight) vs isopentane (one branch) vs neopentane (two branches)
2. Position Isomerism:
  • Same carbon skeleton and functional group, but the group is at different positions.
  • Example (C₃H₇OH): 1-propanol (OH at C1) vs 2-propanol (OH at C2)
3. Functional Group Isomerism:
  • Same molecular formula but different functional groups.
  • Example (C₂H₆O): Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH — alcohol) vs dimethyl ether (CH₃–O–CH₃ — ether)
4. Metamerism:
  • Same functional group but different alkyl groups on either side.
  • Example: CH₃–O–C₂H₅ vs C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ (ethers with same formula)
Q23
Explain the physical and chemical properties of alkanes. (15.1.4, 15.1.5, 15.1.6) (4M)
Physical Properties of Alkanes (15.1.4):
  • State: C₁–C₄ = gases; C₅–C₁₇ = liquids; C₁₈+ = solids (at room temperature).
  • Boiling/Melting point: Increases with increasing molecular mass (more London dispersion forces). Branched isomers have lower bp than straight chain (less surface area).
  • Solubility: Insoluble in water (non-polar); soluble in organic solvents (like dissolves like).
  • Density: Less dense than water (float on water).
Chemical Properties of Alkanes (15.1.5, 15.1.6):
  • Combustion (complete): CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat Alkanes are excellent fuels (high calorific value).
  • Halogenation (free radical substitution): In presence of UV light, H is replaced by halogen. CH₄ + Cl₂ → (hν) → CH₃Cl + HCl Mechanism: initiation → propagation → termination (free radical chain reaction).
  • Cracking (pyrolysis): Higher alkanes break down to smaller alkenes and alkanes at high temperature. C₁₆H₃₄ → (high T) → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆
  • Isomerisation: Straight chain alkanes convert to branched isomers using AlCl₃/HCl catalyst (used in petroleum refining to improve octane rating).
Uses of Alkanes: Fuels (LPG, petrol, diesel), solvents (hexane), wax (paraffin), petrochemical raw materials.
