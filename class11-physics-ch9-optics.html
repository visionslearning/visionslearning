9.1 & 9.2

🌟 Nature of Light & Introduction

🔑 Key Points
  • Optics = science of light (like acoustics is science of sound)
  • Speed of light in vacuum: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s (exact: 299792458 m/s) — maximum possible speed (Einstein's relativity)
  • Speed in medium: v = c/n; where n = absolute refractive index of medium
  • Dual nature of light: light is energy-carrier photons guided by EM wave rules — established by experiment
  • Three categories of light phenomena:
  • → Ray (Geometrical) Optics: reflection, refraction, TIR, double refraction — uses ray model
  • → Wave (Physical) Optics: interference, diffraction, polarization, Doppler effect — uses EM wave model
  • → Particle (Quantum) Optics: photoelectric effect, Compton effect, spectral lines — uses photon/quantum model
9.3

🔭 Ray Optics — Fundamental Laws

🔑 Four Fundamental Principles
  • (i) Light travels in a straight line in a homogeneous and isotropic medium
  • (ii) Law of Reflection: ∠i = ∠r; incident ray, reflected ray, and normal are coplanar
  • (iii) Law of Refraction (Snell's Law): n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r; incident ray, refracted ray and normal are coplanar
  • (iv) Principle of reversibility — if direction of light ray is reversed, it retraces original path
  • Homogeneous medium: same properties everywhere in the medium
  • Isotropic medium: same properties in all directions
  • Refractive index n = c/v = sin i / sin r (Snell's law form)
9.4

🪞 Reflection at Curved Surfaces (Mirrors)

🔑 Key Terms
  • Pole (P): centre of reflecting surface of mirror
  • Centre of curvature (C): centre of sphere of which mirror is a part
  • Radius of curvature (R): radius of that sphere; R = 2f
  • Principal focus (F): point where parallel rays meet (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) after reflection
  • Focal length (f): distance PF; f = R/2
  • Concave mirror: converging; centre of curvature in front of mirror
  • Convex mirror: diverging; centre of curvature behind mirror; always forms virtual, erect, diminished image

📋 Cartesian Sign Convention
  • All distances measured from pole P
  • Distances in direction of incident light (usually left → right) are positive

9.5

🌊 Refraction of Light

🔑 Key Points
  • Refraction: bending of light when it passes from one medium to another due to change in speed
  • Snell's Law: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r
  • Denser medium (larger n): light bends towards normal; speed decreases
  • Rarer medium (smaller n): light bends away from normal; speed increases
  • Lateral displacement: a ray through a glass slab emerges parallel to incident ray but displaced sideways; d = t sin(i − r) / cos r
  • Apparent depth: object in denser medium appears closer; n = real depth / apparent depth
  • Fish underwater sees objects outside at apparent depth; monkey sees fish at apparent depth = real depth / n

9.6

💎 Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

🔑 Key Points
  • TIR: when light travels from denser to rarer medium and angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle — light is totally reflected back
  • Conditions for TIR: (i) light must go from denser → rarer medium; (ii) angle of incidence must be greater than critical angle (i > θ_c)
  • Critical angle (θ_c): angle of incidence for which angle of refraction = 90°
  • Formula: sin θ_c = n₂/n₁ = 1/n (for glass-air: sin θ_c = 1/n_glass)
  • Higher refractive index → smaller critical angle → TIR easier
⭐ Applications of TIR
  • Optical fibre: light undergoes repeated TIR inside glass fibre; used in internet cables, endoscopy, communication
  • Diamond: high n (≈2.42) → very small θ_c (≈24°) → brilliant sparkle due to TIR from many facets
  • Mirage: in hot desert, air near ground has lower n (less dense) → TIR of sky light → appears like water pool
  • Rainbow: TIR + dispersion inside water droplets
  • Prism as reflector: totally reflecting prisms (45°-45°-90°) used in periscopes, binoculars instead of mirrors

9.7

🔵 Refraction at Spherical Surface & Lenses

🔑 Refraction at Spherical Surface Formula
  • For refraction at single spherical surface separating media n₁ and n₂:
  • Formula: n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R
  • R is positive if centre of curvature is on the transmission side (right)
🔑 Lenses
  • Convex (converging) lens: thicker at centre; converges parallel rays to real focus; f > 0
  • Concave (diverging) lens: thinner at centre; diverges parallel rays; virtual focus; f < 0
  • Lens Maker's Formula: 1/f = (n−1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂]; n = refractive index of lens material
  • Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u
  • Magnification: m = v/u = h'/h
  • Power of lens: P = 1/f (in metres); unit = dioptre (D)
  • Lenses in contact: P = P₁ + P₂; 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂

9.8

🔺 Prism — Refraction & Dispersion

🔑 Key Points
  • Prism: triangular transparent block; angle of prism = A (apex angle)
  • Angle of deviation (d): angle between original incident ray and final emergent ray
  • d = i + e − A (where i = angle of incidence, e = angle of emergence)
  • Minimum deviation (D_m): when i = e (ray passes symmetrically); r = A/2; i = (A + D_m)/2
  • Refractive index formula: n = sin[(A + D_m)/2] / sin(A/2)
  • Dispersion: splitting of white light into its component colours (VIBGYOR) by prism due to different n for different λ
  • Angular dispersion: δ_v − δ_r (difference in deviation of violet and red)
  • Dispersive power: ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1); n_y = refractive index for yellow (mean)
  • Violet deviates most (highest n, shortest λ); Red deviates least (lowest n, longest λ)

🔭 Optical Instruments

👁️ The Eye
  • Least distance of distinct vision (D): 25 cm for normal eye
  • Far point: infinity (normal eye); near point: 25 cm
  • Myopia (short-sightedness): cannot see far; image forms in front of retina; corrected by concave lens
  • Hypermetropia (long-sightedness): cannot see near; image behind retina; corrected by convex lens
  • Presbyopia: age-related, loss of near vision; corrected by bifocal lenses
  • Astigmatism: unequal curvature of eye lens; corrected by cylindrical lenses
🔍 Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)
  • Single convex lens; object placed between F and lens
  • Magnifying power (image at D): M = 1 + D/f
  • Magnifying power (image at infinity): M = D/f
  • Shorter focal length → greater magnification
🔬 Compound Microscope
  • Two convex lenses: objective (short f, small) + eyepiece (longer f, acts as magnifier)
  • Object placed just beyond F_o; objective forms real, magnified intermediate image
  • Eyepiece magnifies that intermediate image further
  • Magnifying power: M = m_o × M_e = (L/f_o) × (D/f_e) [image at infinity]
  • L = tube length (distance between F_o and F_e); shorter f_o and f_e → more magnification
🔭 Astronomical Telescope
  • Two convex lenses: objective (large f, large aperture) + eyepiece (small f)
  • Objective collects light from distant object; eyepiece magnifies it
  • Magnifying power (normal adjustment): M = f_o / f_e
  • Length of telescope (normal adjustment): L = f_o + f_e
  • Large objective aperture → more light gathered → brighter, clearer image of faint objects
  • Large f_o, small f_e → higher magnification
🔭 Reflecting Telescope (Cassegrain)
  • Uses concave mirror as objective instead of lens
  • Advantages: no chromatic aberration; can be made very large; lighter than refracting telescope
  • Largest telescopes in world are all reflecting type

🪞 Reflection & Mirrors
Q1
State the mirror formula. A concave mirror of focal length 20 cm forms an image at 60 cm. Find the object distance and magnification.
🔴 Very IMP4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Mirror Formula: The relation between object distance (u), image distance (v) and focal length (f) for a spherical mirror is:

1/f = 1/v + 1/u

Solution: f = −20 cm (concave), v = −60 cm (real image, in front)

1/u = 1/f − 1/v = 1/(−20) − 1/(−60) = −1/20 + 1/60 1/u = −3/60 + 1/60 = −2/60 = −1/30 ∴ u = −30 cm

Magnification: m = −v/u = −(−60)/(−30) = −2

Image is real, inverted and twice the size of object (magnified).

🔴 Very IMP3 Marks

Concave Mirror: Converging mirror. Reflects parallel rays to meet at focus F in front of the mirror.

Uses of Concave Mirror:

1. Shaving/makeup mirror — object (face) placed between F and P; forms virtual, erect, magnified image

2. Headlights of vehicles / searchlights — bulb placed at focus; reflects parallel beam of light

💎 Total Internal Reflection
Q3
What is total internal reflection? State the conditions for TIR. Derive the relation between critical angle and refractive index.
🔴 Every Year4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Total Internal Reflection: When a ray of light travels from an optically denser medium to a rarer medium, and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle, the ray is completely reflected back into the denser medium. This is called total internal reflection.

Conditions for TIR:

1. Light must travel from a denser medium to a rarer medium (e.g., glass to air)

2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle (i > θ_c)

Derivation of sin θ_c = 1/n:

At critical angle θ_c, the refracted ray grazes along the interface (r = 90°).

Applying Snell's Law at the denser (n) to rarer (air, n=1) interface:

n sin θ_c = 1 × sin 90° n sin θ_c = 1
∴ sin θ_c = 1/n OR θ_c = sin⁻¹(1/n)

For glass (n = 1.5): sin θ_c = 1/1.5 = 2/3 → θ_c = 41.8°

Q4
Explain the working of an optical fibre. State its applications.
🔴 Very IMP3 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Optical Fibre: A thin, flexible glass or plastic fibre consisting of a dense core surrounded by a less dense cladding material.

Working: When light enters the core at one end at an angle greater than the critical angle (of core-cladding interface), it undergoes repeated total internal reflections along the length of the fibre. No light escapes through the sides — all light travels through to the other end.

  ══════════════════════════════════════
   core (dense) surrounded by cladding (rarer)
   TIR at each reflection — no energy loss

Applications:

1. Internet/communication cables — carry data as light pulses; much faster than copper cables

2. Medical endoscopy — examine internal organs without surgery

3. Decorative lighting, illuminated signs

4. Sensors in aircraft and automobiles

🔵 Lenses
Q5
Derive the lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n−1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)
🔴 Very IMP4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Consider a thin convex lens of refractive index n, radii R₁ and R₂, in air (n₁ = 1).

Step 1: Refraction at first surface (centre of curvature C₁, radius R₁):

n/v₁ − 1/u = (n−1)/R₁ ... (i)

Step 2: Refraction at second surface (the image I₁ from step 1 acts as object):

1/v − n/v₁ = (1−n)/R₂ = −(n−1)/R₂ ... (ii)

Step 3: Add equations (i) and (ii):

1/v − 1/u = (n−1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂]

For object at infinity (u → ∞), v = f (focal length):

∴ 1/f = (n−1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂] — Lens Maker's Formula

This gives the focal length of a lens in terms of its geometry and refractive index.

Q6
What is power of a lens? Two lenses of power +3D and −1D are placed in contact. Find combined focal length.
🔴 IMP3 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Power of a Lens: The power of a lens is defined as the reciprocal of its focal length in metres. It represents the ability of the lens to converge or diverge light.

P = 1/f (metres); Unit: dioptre (D)

Positive power → converging (convex) lens

Negative power → diverging (concave) lens

Solution:

P₁ = +3D, P₂ = −1D P = P₁ + P₂ = 3 + (−1) = +2D f = 1/P = 1/2 = 0.5 m = 50 cm

Combined focal length = 50 cm (converging lens)

🔺 Prism & Dispersion
Q7
Derive the formula for refractive index of a prism: n = sin[(A+Dm)/2] / sin(A/2)
🔴 Every Year4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

For a prism of apex angle A with angle of incidence i and angle of emergence e:

Relation 1: r₁ + r₂ = A (sum of angles of refraction inside prism = apex angle)

Relation 2: d = i + e − A (angle of deviation)

At minimum deviation D_m: i = e, so r₁ = r₂ = r

From relation 1: r + r = A → r = A/2 From relation 2: D_m = 2i − A → i = (A + D_m)/2

Applying Snell's law at first surface (air to prism):

n = sin i / sin r
n = sin[(A + D_m)/2] / sin(A/2)

This is the standard formula to find refractive index of prism material by measuring apex angle A and minimum deviation D_m.

Q8
What is dispersion of light? Why does a prism produce a spectrum? Name the colours of the spectrum in order.
🔴 IMP3 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Dispersion of Light: The phenomenon of splitting of white light into its component colours on passing through a prism (or any refracting medium) is called dispersion.

Reason: The refractive index of a medium is different for different wavelengths (colours) of light. Violet light has the highest refractive index (shortest λ) and red has the lowest (longest λ). Therefore, each colour bends through a different angle in the prism, causing them to separate.

Spectrum colours (VIBGYOR) in increasing order of wavelength:

Violet → Indigo → Blue → Green → Yellow → Orange → Red

Violet deviates most (highest n), Red deviates least (lowest n).

Rainbow: formed by dispersion + TIR inside water droplets in atmosphere after rain.

🔭 Optical Instruments
Q9
With a neat diagram, explain the construction and working of a compound microscope. Derive the formula for magnifying power.
🔴 Very IMP4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Construction: Two convex lenses — objective lens (O) of short focal length f_o and small diameter, and eyepiece (E) of slightly longer focal length f_e, separated by a tube of length L.

  Object → [Objective O] → intermediate image → [Eyepiece E] → Final image (at D)
  |___f_o__|____L (tube length)___|____f_e___|

Working:

1. Object AB placed just beyond F_o of objective

2. Objective forms real, inverted, magnified intermediate image A'B' just inside F_e of eyepiece

3. Eyepiece acts as a simple microscope; magnifies A'B' to give final virtual, magnified image at D (25 cm)

Magnifying Power:

M = magnification by objective × magnification by eyepiece m_o = L/f_o (for image at tube length L from objective) M_e = D/f_e (eyepiece, image at infinity)
M = (L/f_o) × (D/f_e) where D = 25 cm
Q10
Explain the construction and working of an astronomical telescope. Derive the formula for magnifying power under normal adjustment.
🔴 Very IMP4 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Construction: Two convex lenses — objective (large aperture, long focal length f_o) and eyepiece (small aperture, short focal length f_e).

  Distant object → [Objective f_o large] → image at F_o = F_e → [Eyepiece f_e small] → Final image at ∞
  |___________f_o__________|__f_e__|
  Total length L = f_o + f_e (normal adjustment)

Working:

1. Objective collects light from distant object; forms real, inverted, diminished image at its focal plane

2. Eyepiece is adjusted so its focal plane coincides with objective's focal plane (normal adjustment)

3. Eyepiece acts as magnifier; final image is at infinity — eye is relaxed

Magnifying Power (normal adjustment):

M = angle subtended by final image / angle subtended by object at unaided eye M = β/α = (h/f_e) / (h/f_o)
M = f_o / f_e

Length of telescope: L = f_o + f_e

To increase M: use large f_o (large objective) and small f_e (short eyepiece).

Q12
Why is the sky blue? Why does the sun appear reddish at sunrise and sunset?
🟠 IMP2 Marks
✅ Model Answer

Blue Sky: Sunlight (white light) passes through the atmosphere. Air molecules scatter light. Shorter wavelengths (blue, violet) scatter much more than longer wavelengths (Rayleigh scattering: intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴). Blue light is scattered in all directions reaching our eyes → sky appears blue.

Reddish Sun at sunrise/sunset: When the Sun is near the horizon, its light travels through a greater thickness of atmosphere. Blue light is scattered away. Only the longer wavelengths — red and orange — reach the observer's eye → Sun appears red/orange.

N2. A ray of light passes from glass (n = 1.5) to water (n = 1.33). Find the critical angle.
n_glass = 1.5, n_water = 1.33
