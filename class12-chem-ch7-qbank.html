Q1
Write the preparation, properties and uses of sulphur dioxide (SO₂). Write the chemical equations.
20242023202220212020 3–4 MarksGroup 16
✅ Model Answer

Preparation of SO₂:

1. By burning sulphur in air:

S + O₂ → SO₂

2. By action of dilute H₂SO₄ on sodium sulphite (laboratory method):

Na₂SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + SO₂↑ + H₂O

3. By roasting metal sulphides:

4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂ (industrial)

Properties of SO₂:

1. Acidic oxide: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulphurous acid — weak acid)

2. Reducing agent: Decolourises KMnO₄ and K₂Cr₂O₇ solutions

2KMnO₄ + 5SO₂ + 2H₂O → K₂SO₄ + 2MnSO₄ + 2H₂SO₄

3. Oxidising agent: Oxidises H₂S to sulphur

2H₂S + SO₂ → 3S + 2H₂O

4. SO₂ is a colourless gas with pungent smell; heavier than air; soluble in water

Uses of SO₂:

1. Used as a preservative and disinfectant 2. Manufacturing H₂SO₄ (Contact process) 3. Bleaching agent (wool, silk) 4. Refrigerant 5. Prevents fermentation of sugar

Q3
Explain the allotropes of sulphur. Write the structure and stability of rhombic and monoclinic sulphur.
2024202320222020 3 MarksGroup 16
✅ Model Answer

Allotropes of Sulphur: Sulphur exists in several allotropic forms, all containing S₈ rings with a crown shape (puckered ring).

PropertyRhombic (α)Monoclinic (β)
Crystal shapeRhombic (octahedral)Needle-like
ColourYellowPale yellow
Stable temperatureBelow 369 K (96°C)Above 369 K
Density2.08 g/cm³1.98 g/cm³
Solubility in CS₂SolubleSoluble

Transition temperature: 369 K (96°C) — below this, rhombic is stable; above this, monoclinic is stable. Both convert to each other at 369 K.

Structure: Both allotropes contain crown-shaped S₈ rings — 8 sulphur atoms in a puckered ring with S-S single bonds (bond angle ≈ 108°, bond length = 206 pm).

Plastic sulphur: Formed by pouring molten sulphur into cold water — amorphous, elastic, brown/yellow colour. Unstable, slowly converts to crystalline rhombic.

🟠 Group 17 — Halogens
Q6
What are interhalogen compounds? Give the general formula. Write the preparation and properties of ClF₃.
20242023202220212020 3–4 MarksGroup 17
✅ Model Answer

Interhalogen compounds: Compounds formed by combination of two different halogens. The less electronegative (larger) halogen occupies central position.

General formula: XX'ₙ (n = 1, 3, 5, 7) where X is heavier halogen

TypeExamplesShape
XX' (n=1)ClF, BrF, BrCl, ICl, IBrLinear
XX'₃ (n=3)ClF₃, BrF₃, IF₃T-shaped
XX'₅ (n=5)BrF₅, IF₅, ClF₅Square pyramidal
XX'₇ (n=7)IF₇Pentagonal bipyramidal

Preparation of ClF₃:

Cl₂ + 3F₂ → 2ClF₃ (at 250°C, excess F₂)

Properties of ClF₃:

1. Colourless gas, poisonous 2. T-shaped structure (3 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs on Cl) 3. Very strong fluorinating agent — reacts with many metals and non-metals 4. Reacts with water: ClF₃ + 2H₂O → HClO₂ + 3HF

Why n is always odd? Central halogen uses sp³d, sp³d², sp³d³ hybridisation — only odd number of bond pairs possible along with lone pairs.

Q9
Why does fluorine show anomalous behaviour compared to other halogens? Give 4 reasons.
2024202320222021 3 MarksGroup 17
✅ Model Answer

Fluorine shows anomalous behaviour due to:

1. No d-orbitals in valence shell → Cannot form oxoacids (HClO, HBrO exist but HFO does not). Cannot expand octet.

2. Highest electronegativity (4.0) → Strongest oxidising agent. Cannot be oxidised to positive oxidation state. Always shows oxidation state = −1.

3. Smallest atomic size → HF forms strong hydrogen bonds (HF is liquid; HCl, HBr, HI are gases at room temperature). Boiling point anomalously high.

4. Strongest fluorinating agent → Reacts explosively with H₂ even in dark; attacks glass (SiO₂ + 2F₂ → SiF₄ + O₂). Most reactive non-metal.

5. Highest bond dissociation energy issue: F-F bond is surprisingly weak (159 kJ/mol) due to repulsion between lone pairs on adjacent small F atoms.

6. HF is a weak acid (forms stable H-bonded (HF)₂ dimer) — unlike HCl, HBr, HI which are strong acids.

Q10
Write the preparation and properties of chlorine (Cl₂). Why is Cl₂ a better oxidising agent than Br₂?
2023202220212020 3 MarksGroup 17
✅ Model Answer

Preparation of Cl₂:

Laboratory: MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂↑

Industrial (Deacon's process): 4HCl + O₂ → 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O (CuCl₂ catalyst, 400°C)

Electrolysis of NaCl(aq) — Chlor-alkali process: Cl₂ at anode

Properties of Cl₂:

1. Greenish-yellow, poisonous gas with pungent smell; heavier than air

2. Reacts with water: Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl (bleaching by HOCl)

3. Reacts with NaOH: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O

4. Displacement: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (Cl₂ displaces Br⁻, being more reactive)

Why Cl₂ > Br₂ as oxidising agent?

Cl is smaller than Br → higher electronegativity → greater electron affinity → stronger tendency to gain electrons → stronger oxidising agent. Standard reduction potential E°(Cl₂/Cl⁻) = +1.36V > E°(Br₂/Br⁻) = +1.07V

Q14
Why do noble gases have the highest ionisation energies in their respective periods? Why do their boiling points increase down the group?
2023202220212020 2 MarksGroup 18
✅ Model Answer

Highest ionisation energy: Noble gases have completely filled outermost shells (ns²np⁶ = 8 electrons — octet). This configuration is extremely stable. Very high nuclear charge acts on a compact, fully filled shell → very difficult to remove any electron → highest IE in that period.

Example: IE of Ar = 1521 kJ/mol (highest in period 3), compared to Na = 496 kJ/mol and Cl = 1251 kJ/mol.

Increasing boiling points (He → Rn):

Noble gases exist as monatomic molecules. Only intermolecular forces are London dispersion (van der Waals) forces. These forces increase with:

(i) Increasing atomic size (ii) Increasing number of electrons (more polarisable)

As we go down the group: He(2e) → Rn(86e) → more electrons → larger induced dipoles → stronger van der Waals forces → higher boiling point.

He: −269°C → Ne: −246°C → Ar: −186°C → Kr: −153°C → Xe: −108°C → Rn: −62°C
