📗 Unit 1 – Diversity in Living World
Q1. What is chemotaxonomy? Give one suitable example. ▶

💡 Explanation: Traditional taxonomy used shape, size, colour to classify organisms. But chemotaxonomy uses chemicals inside the organism — like proteins and DNA — which are more reliable because chemicals don't change with the environment. Two organisms with similar chemical makeup are closely related even if they look different. This is now the most accurate form of classification.

Chemotaxonomy is the method of classifying organisms based on their chemical constituents — especially proteins, DNA sequences, and secondary metabolites.

  • It compares biochemical similarities and differences among organisms.
  • More accurate than morphological classification alone.

Example: Plants producing reserpine (Rauwolfia serpentina) are grouped together based on this chemical compound.

✍️ Define + example = full marks
Q2. Write a note on useful and harmful bacteria. ▶

💡 Explanation: Bacteria are the most abundant organisms on earth. They are prokaryotes with no nucleus. Most bacteria are helpful to us — they help digest food, fix nitrogen, produce medicines. Some however cause serious diseases by producing toxins or directly invading tissues.

Useful Bacteria:

  • Lactobacillus – converts milk to curd (fermentation)
  • Rhizobium – fixes atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules of legumes
  • Bacillus subtilis – produces antibiotics
  • Used in production of cheese, vinegar, and vitamins

Harmful Bacteria:

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis – causes tuberculosis (TB)
  • Vibrio cholerae – causes cholera
  • Clostridium tetani – causes tetanus
  • Cause food spoilage and plant diseases
Q3. The systematic study of organisms is essential. Give a suitable reason. ▶

💡 Explanation: Without systematic study, we would have no order in the living world. Imagine millions of species with no names or categories — finding a cure for a disease or understanding an ecosystem would be impossible. Taxonomy gives every organism a unique identity and helps scientists communicate globally using the same names.

Systematic study (taxonomy) of organisms is essential because:

  • It helps in identification and naming of organisms
  • Establishes evolutionary relationships among organisms
  • Helps in understanding biodiversity
  • Useful in agriculture, medicine, ecology, and conservation
  • Prevents confusion — every organism gets a unique scientific name
✍️ Give at least 4 reasons for full marks
Q4. Describe the different types of viruses. ▶

💡 Explanation: Viruses are non-cellular, acellular particles — they are on the border of living and non-living. They cannot reproduce on their own — they need a host cell. They inject their DNA/RNA into the host and use the host's machinery to make copies of themselves. This is why viruses are hard to kill — antiviral drugs must target the virus without harming the host cell.

Viruses are classified based on:

A. Based on Host:

  • Plant viruses – TMV (Tobacco Mosaic Virus)
  • Animal viruses – Influenza, HIV, Polio virus
  • Bacteriophages – infect bacteria (e.g., T4 phage)

B. Based on Nucleic Acid:

  • DNA viruses – Adenovirus, Herpes virus
  • RNA viruses – TMV, HIV, Influenza

C. Based on Shape:

  • Spherical (e.g., Poliovirus)
  • Rod-shaped (e.g., TMV)
  • Complex/tadpole-shaped (e.g., Bacteriophage)
Q5. What is a herbarium? ▶

💡 Explanation: A herbarium is like a library of plants. Just as a library stores books for reference, a herbarium stores dried plant specimens for future study. Scientists can compare a newly found plant with herbarium specimens to identify it, even if the plant is extinct in the wild. India's largest herbarium is at BSI (Botanical Survey of India), Kolkata.

A herbarium is a storehouse (repository) of collected plant specimens that are dried, pressed, and preserved on sheets. These sheets are arranged in a systematic order (according to a universally accepted classification system) and stored for reference.

  • Each specimen carries a label with name, date, location of collection
  • Serves as a quick referral system for plant taxonomists
  • Famous herbaria: Kew (London), BSI Kolkata (India)
Q7. Write a note on a museum. ▶

💡 Explanation: A museum is a permanent record of biodiversity. Unlike herbaria (only plants), museums have animals too. Specimens preserved in formalin stop decaying because formalin kills all bacteria and fungi that would otherwise break down the tissue. Taxidermy (stuffing animals) is used for larger animals like birds and mammals to preserve their natural appearance.

A museum is a place where preserved specimens of plants and animals are stored for study and reference.

  • Specimens preserved in formalin, alcohol, or as dry specimens
  • Insects are pinned and kept in insect boxes
  • Skeletons and casts of large animals are displayed
  • Used for teaching, research, and public education
  • Example: Natural History Museum, London
Q26. Write the meaning of taxonomy. ▶

💡 Explanation: Taxonomy is the science of order in biology. Without it, a biologist in India and a biologist in USA could call the same organism by completely different names and never realise they are studying the same species. Linnaeus solved this with binomial nomenclature — a two-part universal Latin name. The hierarchy (Kingdom to Species) organises all life into a logical tree.

Taxonomy (Greek: taxis = arrangement; nomos = law) is the branch of biology that deals with identification, naming, and classification of organisms.

  • Based on observable characteristics
  • Father of taxonomy: Carolus Linnaeus
  • Uses binomial nomenclature — two-part Latin names (Genus + species)
  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species
Q27. What is DNA barcoding? Mention the steps in this process. ▶

💡 Explanation: Traditional taxonomy required physical specimens and expert knowledge. DNA barcoding revolutionised this — just like a barcode on a product identifies it in seconds, a short DNA sequence can identify any species from a tiny tissue sample. It's used in customs to check for smuggled animals, in food industry to verify fish species, and in conservation to monitor endangered species.

DNA barcoding is a technique that uses a short standardized region of DNA (like a barcode) to identify species quickly and accurately.

Gene used: CO1 gene (cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) for animals; rbcL gene for plants.

Steps:

  • 1. Collect tissue sample from organism
  • 2. Extract DNA from the tissue
  • 3. Amplify the target gene by PCR
  • 4. Sequence the DNA
  • 5. Compare with database (BOLD system) for identification
Q30. What are the two subkingdoms of Kingdom Plantae? ▶

💡 Explanation: Kingdom Plantae is divided based on whether plants produce seeds or not. Cryptogamae (hidden reproduction) include simpler plants like algae, mosses, and ferns that reproduce by spores — no flowers, no seeds. Phanerogamae (visible reproduction) include seed-bearing plants — gymnosperms have naked seeds (like pine cones) and angiosperms have seeds enclosed in fruits (like mango, wheat).

  • Cryptogamae – plants without seeds; reproduce by spores. Includes Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta.
  • Phanerogamae – plants with seeds. Includes Gymnosperms and Angiosperms.
📗 Unit 2 – Plant Kingdom
Q11. What are the important features of Bryophytes? ▶

💡 Explanation: Bryophytes are called amphibians of the plant kingdom because like amphibians (frogs), they live in moist areas and need water for reproduction (the sperm must swim to reach the egg). They are the first plants to have moved from water to land but they never fully adapted to dry conditions. Examples like moss (Funaria) are commonly seen on moist walls and rocks.

  • Called amphibians of plant kingdom — need water for reproduction
  • Body is thallus-like, differentiated into stem and leaf-like structures
  • No vascular tissue (xylem/phloem) — no true roots, stems, leaves
  • Attached to soil by rhizoids
  • Dominant phase is gametophyte (haploid)
  • Sporophyte is dependent on gametophyte
  • Examples: Marchantia (liverwort), Funaria (moss), Anthoceros (hornwort)
Q13. Write the characteristics of Thallophyta. ▶

💡 Explanation: Thallophyta are the simplest plants — their body is not divided into root, stem, or leaf. The whole body is called a thallus. Algae like Spirogyra are found in ponds and ditches. They are important because they produce oxygen through photosynthesis and are the base of aquatic food chains.

  • Plant body is a thallus — not differentiated into root, stem, leaf
  • No vascular tissue
  • Mostly aquatic (algae)
  • Reproduce by vegetative, asexual (spores) and sexual methods
  • Chlorophyll present — autotrophic (algae)
  • Examples: Spirogyra, Ulva, Chara, Volvox
  • Also includes fungi (heterotrophic thallophytes)
Q15. What are the important features of Kingdom Fungi? ▶

💡 Explanation: Fungi are unique organisms — they are neither plants nor animals. They don't make their own food (no chlorophyll) — instead they break down dead organic matter (saprophytes) or live on other organisms (parasites). Their cell wall is made of chitin, not cellulose. The mushroom we eat is actually the reproductive part (fruiting body) of a fungus. Fungi are also used to make antibiotics like penicillin.

  • Heterotrophic — saprophytic, parasitic, or symbiotic
  • Cell wall made of chitin
  • Body made of hyphae → collectively called mycelium
  • Reproduce by spores (asexual) and sexual methods
  • Store food as glycogen (not starch)
  • Lack chlorophyll — non-photosynthetic
  • Examples: Rhizopus, Penicillium, Agaricus, Aspergillus
📗 Unit 3 – Cell Biology & Division
Q8. Write the significance of mitosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Mitosis is a copying process. The cell makes an exact copy of all its chromosomes and divides into two identical daughter cells. This is how our body grows — a single fertilised egg divides billions of times by mitosis to form all our organs. It also replaces damaged cells, like when skin heals after a cut.

  • Helps in growth of multicellular organisms
  • Maintains the chromosome number (diploid) in daughter cells
  • Repairs and replaces damaged or worn-out tissues
  • Responsible for asexual reproduction (in lower organisms)
  • Maintains genetic stability — daughter cells are genetically identical to parent
Q12. Define meiosis. Name the person who coined the term "meiosis". ▶

💡 Explanation: Unlike mitosis which makes identical copies, meiosis is a reduction division. It reduces the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n) so that when two gametes (sperm + egg) fuse during fertilisation, the original chromosome number is restored. Without meiosis, chromosome number would double every generation! Crossing over during meiosis also creates new genetic combinations — this is why children are not identical to parents.

Meiosis is a type of cell division in which the chromosome number is reduced to half (haploid). It occurs in reproductive organs to produce gametes.

  • Involves two successive divisions: Meiosis I and Meiosis II
  • Results in 4 haploid daughter cells from 1 diploid parent cell
  • Promotes genetic variation through crossing over

Term coined by: J.B. Farmer and J.E.S. Moore (1905)

Q19. Describe the cell cycle with a neat, labelled diagram. ▶

💡 Explanation: The cell cycle is a carefully controlled sequence. During Interphase the cell doubles its contents (DNA replication in S phase), grows (G1, G2), then divides during M phase. This control is important — if a cell skips steps or divides uncontrollably, it leads to cancer. G0 is a resting state where cells like neurons permanently stop dividing.

The cell cycle is the sequence of events from the formation of a cell to its division into two daughter cells.

Two main phases:

1. Interphase (I-phase): Longest phase — cell prepares for division

  • G₁ phase – cell growth; RNA and protein synthesis
  • S phase – DNA replication (chromosome number doubles)
  • G₂ phase – further growth; organelle duplication

2. M-phase (Mitotic phase):

  • Karyokinesis – division of nucleus (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase)
  • Cytokinesis – division of cytoplasm
G₁ → S → G₂ → M → (repeat)
[Draw a circle divided into G₁, S, G₂, M phases for diagram]
Q28. Describe prophase of mitosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Prophase is the preparation stage. The cell packages its DNA into tight, visible chromosomes (like coiling a rope to carry it). The nuclear envelope (which protects DNA) breaks down so spindle fibres can reach the chromosomes. Centrioles help in forming the spindle — that is why animal cells (which have centrioles) divide more efficiently than plant cells.

Prophase is the first and longest stage of mitosis. Key events:

  • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes
  • Each chromosome consists of 2 chromatids joined at centromere
  • Nuclear envelope starts to disappear
  • Nucleolus disappears
  • Spindle fibres begin to form (from centrioles in animal cells)
  • Centrioles move to opposite poles
✍️ Draw and label: chromosomes, spindle fibres, disappearing nuclear envelope
Q29. Distinguish between mitosis and meiosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: The key difference is the outcome — mitosis produces 2 identical cells (for growth/repair), while meiosis produces 4 genetically different cells (for reproduction). Meiosis also has crossing over in Prophase I, which shuffles genetic material — this is why every person is unique. This table is very commonly asked in exams — memorise all 7 rows.

Q31. Write a note on prophase I of meiosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Prophase I is the most important and complex phase of meiosis. The most critical event here is crossing over (in Pachytene stage) — where homologous chromosomes exchange segments of DNA. This is the main source of genetic variation in sexually reproducing organisms. Without crossing over, all offspring of the same parents would be genetically identical.

Prophase I is the most complex and longest phase of meiosis. It is divided into 5 substages:

  • Leptotene: Chromosomes become visible; start to condense
  • Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis); bivalents form
  • Pachytene: Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids; chiasmata form
  • Diplotene: Homologs start to separate; chiasmata visible
  • Diakinesis: Chromosomes fully condensed; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms
✍️ Remember: L-Z-P-D-D (Leptotene-Zygotene-Pachytene-Diplotene-Diakinesis)
📗 Unit 4 – Human Physiology
Q10. Explain the structure of a V.S. kidney. ▶

💡 Explanation: The kidney has an outer cortex where filtration happens and an inner medulla where concentration of urine happens. The pelvis collects all the urine from the tubules and drains it into the ureter. The hilum is the entry/exit point — like the "door" of the kidney. Always draw and label a clean diagram for this question.

The kidney is a bean-shaped organ. Its vertical section shows:

  • Cortex (outer region): Contains Malpighian corpuscles (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) and convoluted tubules
  • Medulla (inner region): Contains renal pyramids and loops of Henle
  • Pelvis: Funnel-shaped cavity that collects urine; leads into ureter
  • Hilum: Notch on inner side where renal artery enters and renal vein + ureter exit
  • Renal capsule: Tough outer covering
✍️ Draw and label: cortex, medulla, pelvis, hilum, renal artery, vein, ureter
Q17. Explain the structure of a nephron with diagram. ▶

💡 Explanation: The nephron is like a tiny purification plant. Blood enters the glomerulus at high pressure and gets filtered — water, glucose, urea, salts all pass into the Bowman's capsule (this is filtration). As the filtrate passes through PCT, Loop of Henle and DCT, useful substances like glucose and water are reabsorbed back into blood. What remains is urine — mainly urea, excess salts and water.

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each kidney has ~1 million nephrons.

Parts of a nephron:

  • Bowman's capsule: Cup-shaped structure enclosing the glomerulus
  • Glomerulus: Tuft of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule — filtration occurs here
  • Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Selective reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, water
  • Loop of Henle: Descending (permeable to water) + Ascending limb (permeable to salts) — concentrates urine
  • Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Further reabsorption and secretion
  • Collecting Duct: Collects urine from many nephrons → passes to pelvis
Q14. Write a note on ureotelism. ▶

💡 Explanation: When proteins are broken down in our body, the nitrogen must be removed (since only C, H, O go into energy pathways). This nitrogen becomes ammonia first. In ureotelic organisms like us, ammonia is converted to urea in the liver — a less toxic compound — and excreted in urine. This is more efficient in water conservation compared to ammonia (which needs lots of water to dilute).

Ureotelism is the mode of excretion in which urea is the main nitrogenous waste product.

  • Occurs in: humans, most mammals, frogs, aquatic turtles
  • Urea is formed in the liver by the ornithine cycle (urea cycle)
  • Urea is less toxic and less soluble than ammonia → can be stored before excretion
  • Requires less water for excretion compared to ammonotelism
  • Excreted through kidneys as urine
Q23. Define excretion and osmoregulation. ▶

💡 Explanation: Excretion removes harmful waste products (especially nitrogenous wastes from protein breakdown). Osmoregulation is about balance — maintaining the right amount of water and salts. These two processes are closely linked in the kidney — the same nephron that filters out waste also reabsorbs the right amount of water. ADH (antidiuretic hormone) from the pituitary gland controls how much water is reabsorbed.

Excretion: The biological process by which metabolic waste products (especially nitrogenous wastes like urea, uric acid) are eliminated from the body to maintain internal homeostasis.

Osmoregulation: The process by which an organism maintains the concentration of water and dissolved solutes (osmolarity) in its body fluids within a narrow range, despite external changes.

  • Kidneys are the main organs of both excretion and osmoregulation
  • ADH (antidiuretic hormone) controls water reabsorption in kidneys
Q24. What are the different types of nutrition? ▶

💡 Explanation: All living organisms need energy. Autotrophic organisms (like plants) make their own food using sunlight or chemicals. Heterotrophic organisms (like us) must eat other organisms for energy. The type of heterotrophic nutrition depends on the food source — holozoic organisms eat solid food, saprophytes feed on dead matter, parasites feed on living hosts without immediately killing them.

Two main types:

A. Autotrophic Nutrition: Organism prepares its own food

  • Photosynthesis (plants, algae) — uses sunlight
  • Chemosynthesis (some bacteria) — uses chemical energy

B. Heterotrophic Nutrition: Organism depends on others for food

  • Holozoic – ingest solid food (animals, humans)
  • Saprophytic – feed on dead organic matter (fungi, bacteria)
  • Parasitic – live on/inside host (tapeworm, Plasmodium)
  • Symbiotic – mutual benefit (Rhizobium in root nodules)
Q20. Describe the three parts of the small intestine. ▶

💡 Explanation: The small intestine is the main site of digestion and absorption. The duodenum receives digestive juices (bile from liver, pancreatic juice from pancreas) and starts breaking down food. The jejunum has millions of villi (finger-like projections) that massively increase surface area for absorption. The ileum absorbs special nutrients like Vitamin B12. Food spends 2–4 hours in the small intestine.

The small intestine (~6–7 m long) has three parts:

  • Duodenum (25 cm): C-shaped; receives bile from liver and pancreatic juice from pancreas; partial digestion of proteins, fats, and carbohydrates begins here
  • Jejunum (~2.5 m): Main site of digestion and absorption; has many villi and microvilli for maximum absorption
  • Ileum (~3.5 m): Final part; absorbs vitamin B₁₂ and bile salts; connects to large intestine at ileocaecal junction
Q25. Write down the dental formula of a human being. ▶

💡 Explanation: The dental formula shows the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper and lower jaws. Incisors cut food, canines tear it, premolars and molars grind it. Humans are diphyodont (two sets: milk teeth + permanent teeth) and heterodont (different types of teeth for different functions). Humans are the only primates with 32 permanent teeth including wisdom teeth.

Dental formula shows the number of each type of tooth in one half of each jaw (upper/lower):

I 2/2 · C 1/1 · PM 2/2 · M 3/3 = 16 per side × 2 = 32 total teeth
  • I = Incisors (cutting), C = Canines (tearing), PM = Premolars (grinding), M = Molars (grinding)
  • Humans have 32 permanent teeth (including 4 wisdom teeth)
  • Diphyodont: two sets of teeth (milk + permanent)
Q32. Sketch and label the human digestive system. ▶

💡 Explanation: The human digestive system is basically a long tube (~9 metres total) with specialised sections. Each section has a specific job — mouth breaks food mechanically, stomach uses acid to kill bacteria and begin protein digestion, small intestine is the main site of chemical digestion and absorption, large intestine absorbs water and forms faeces. The liver and pancreas are accessory glands that produce digestive juices but are not part of the tube itself.

Parts of the Human Digestive System (in order):

  • Mouth (oral cavity) → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → Large Intestine (caecum, colon, rectum) → Anus

Accessory glands:

  • Salivary glands – secrete saliva (amylase)
  • Liver – secretes bile (stored in gall bladder)
  • Pancreas – secretes pancreatic juice (lipase, amylase, trypsin)
✍️ In exam: draw a simple diagram from mouth to anus and label all parts + accessory organs
📗 Unit 5 – Locomotion & Support (Skeletal System)
Q6. Explain the structure of a typical synovial joint. ▶

💡 Explanation: A synovial joint is the most common type of freely movable joint. The synovial fluid acts like engine oil — it lubricates the joint so bones don't grind against each other. Without this fluid, movements like walking or lifting would be painful. Arthritis is a disease where this fluid reduces and joints become painful.

A synovial joint is a freely movable joint found where two bones meet and movement is required.

Structure:

  • Articular cartilage: Covers the ends of bones — reduces friction
  • Synovial cavity: Space between bones filled with synovial fluid
  • Synovial membrane: Lines the capsule; secretes synovial fluid
  • Joint capsule: Tough fibrous outer covering holding the joint together
  • Ligaments: Connect bone to bone; provide stability
  • Synovial fluid: Acts as lubricant; reduces friction during movement

Examples: Knee joint, shoulder joint, hip joint, elbow joint

Q18. Write a note on the sternum. ▶

💡 Explanation: The sternum (breastbone) plays a critical protective role — it forms the front wall of the chest (thoracic cage) along with the ribs, protecting the heart and lungs. The xiphoid process is important in CPR — proper hand placement avoids pressing on it to prevent injury. Remember: Manubrium (top) → Body (middle) → Xiphoid (bottom).

The sternum (breastbone) is a flat bone located in the centre of the chest. It forms the anterior (front) wall of the thoracic cage.

Three parts:

  • Manubrium: Upper part; articulates with clavicle and 1st rib
  • Body (gladiolus): Middle and longest part; articulates with ribs 2–7
  • Xiphoid process: Small lower part; made of cartilage; no rib attachment

The sternum protects the heart and lungs. It joins with ribs to form the ribcage.

Q21. What are joints? Explain their different types. ▶

💡 Explanation: A joint is where two bones meet. Fibrous joints (skull) are fixed because we don't need skull bones to move — they protect the brain. Cartilaginous joints (vertebrae) allow slight movement — needed for bending the spine. Synovial joints (knee, shoulder) are freely movable — essential for locomotion. The type of joint at each location matches its function perfectly.

A joint is the point of contact between two bones or between a bone and cartilage.

Types of Joints:

TypeMovementExample
Fibrous (Fixed)No movementSkull sutures
Cartilaginous (Slightly movable)Slight movementVertebrae, pubic symphysis
Synovial (Freely movable)Full range movementKnee, shoulder, elbow

Types of Synovial Joints:

  • Ball and socket – shoulder, hip (all directions)
  • Hinge – knee, elbow (one plane)
  • Pivot – atlas-axis (rotation)
  • Gliding – wrist bones
  • Saddle – thumb
Q22. Write notes on: (a) Atlas vertebra (b) Axis vertebra. ▶

💡 Explanation: Atlas and Axis are the top two vertebrae of the spine. Atlas supports the skull (like the Greek god Atlas holding the world) — it allows the nodding "yes" movement. Axis has the dens (odontoid process) which acts as a pivot — it allows the rotation "no" movement of the head. These two vertebrae together give us the ability to turn and nod our head freely.

(a) Atlas vertebra (C1):

  • First cervical vertebra; ring-shaped
  • No body or spinous process
  • Has two large lateral masses with articular facets
  • Articulates with skull (occipital condyles) → allows nodding (yes movement)

(b) Axis vertebra (C2):

  • Second cervical vertebra
  • Has a bony projection called odontoid process (dens) pointing upward
  • Dens fits into the ring of atlas → forms a pivot joint → allows rotation of head (no movement)
📗 Organisms & Sketches
Q9. Sketch and label: (a) Paramecium (b) Mucor (c) Spirogyra ▶

💡 Explanation: For sketch questions — always draw a neat, large, clear diagram with all labels pointed by straight lines (not arrows). Paramecium uses its cilia for both movement and feeding. Mucor is a common bread mould — its sporangiophores are like tiny mushrooms carrying spore bags. Spirogyra's spiral chloroplasts are unique — they run the entire length of the cell and contain pyrenoids for starch synthesis. Practice drawing all three!

(a) Paramecium (Protozoa – Ciliata):

  • Slipper-shaped, unicellular, covered with cilia
  • Label: cilia, oral groove, macronucleus, micronucleus, contractile vacuole, food vacuole

(b) Mucor (Fungi):

  • Filamentous fungus; aseptate hyphae
  • Label: sporangiophore, sporangium, spores, mycelium (rhizoids)

(c) Spirogyra (Green algae):

  • Filamentous green algae with spiral chloroplasts
  • Label: spiral chloroplast, pyrenoid, nucleus, cell wall, vacuole
✍️ These are standard 3-mark sketch questions — practice drawing all three
Q16. Describe the different types of viruses based on their host. ▶

💡 Explanation: Viruses are classified by their host because a virus that infects tobacco plants cannot infect a human — it is specifically adapted to enter plant cells. Bacteriophages are important in research and medicine — scientists use them to fight antibiotic-resistant bacteria (phage therapy). HIV is a retrovirus — it uses RNA as its genetic material and reverse transcribes it into DNA, which is why it is so difficult to treat.

  • Plant viruses: Infect plants. Usually RNA viruses. E.g., TMV (Tobacco Mosaic Virus) — causes mosaic disease in tobacco and tomato. Rod-shaped.
  • Animal viruses: Infect animals and humans. DNA or RNA type. E.g., Poliovirus (RNA), Adenovirus (DNA), HIV (RNA-retrovirus), Influenza virus.
  • Bacteriophages: Infect bacteria. Usually DNA viruses with complex structure (head, tail, tail fibres). E.g., T4 bacteriophage — infects E. coli.

🌍 Chapter 1 – The Living World
1.1 Characteristics of Living Organisms ▶

💡 Explanation: Metabolism is the most important characteristic of life — even organisms that don't reproduce (like mules or sterile bees) are still alive because they metabolise. The moment metabolism completely stops, the organism is dead. Growth happens internally (from within) — a sand mound may grow by addition of sand from outside but it does not grow from within like a living cell does.

Planet Earth has abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living) components.

FeatureKey Points
MetabolismCatabolism (breaking molecules) + Anabolism (building molecules). Most all-inclusive characteristic of life.
GrowthOrganisms grow from within. Non-living things like sand mounds grow but NOT from within.
AgeingMolecules, organs and systems gradually lose effective working.
ReproductionAsexual or sexual. NOT all-inclusive — mules & sterile worker bees don't reproduce yet are living.
DeathBody loses capacity to perform metabolism → organism dies.
Responsiveness / IrritabilityLiving beings respond to thermal, chemical or biological changes. Unique property of living organisms.
✍️ Metabolism = most all-inclusive characteristic | Reproduction = NOT all-inclusive
1.2 Herbarium ▶

💡 Explanation: A herbarium is like a reference library of plants. Dried specimens last hundreds of years — herbaria from the 1700s are still used by scientists today! The label on each sheet is critical — it tells when and where the plant was found, who collected it, and its classification. This data helps track how species distribution has changed over time. India's largest herbarium is at BSI (Botanical Survey of India), Kolkata with over 2 million specimens.

Herbarium = a dried plant specimen pressed, treated and mounted on a standard size sheet to preserve it.

  • Word coined by Pitton de Tournefort
  • Art initiated by Italian taxonomist Luca Ghini (1490–1556)
  • Effective tools in taxonomic studies

Label (lower right corner) contains:

  • Date and place of collection
  • Detailed classification of the plant
  • Ecological peculiarities and characters
  • Local names and name of the collector
✍️ Herbarium = Ex-situ conservation tool
1.3 Botanical Gardens ▶

💡 Explanation: Botanical gardens are living museums of plants. Unlike herbaria (dried specimens), plants here are alive and growing. Scientists can study growth, flowering, fruiting, and behaviour of plants from all over the world in one place. They also serve as gene banks — preserving rare and endangered plant species. The first botanical garden was created in 1543 in Italy and India's famous one is in Howrah which has the largest banyan tree in the world.

  • Places where plants from different parts of world are grown scientifically in in vivo manner
  • Plants labelled with scientific and common names
  • First botanical garden established in 1543 by Prof. Luca Ghini at Pisa, Italy
  • Famous Indian example: Indian Botanical Garden, Howrah (Kolkata)
1.4 Museum ▶

💡 Explanation: Museums preserve biodiversity for future generations — including extinct species that can no longer be found in nature. Formalin (formaldehyde solution) works as a preservative because it cross-links proteins and kills bacteria, preventing decomposition. Taxidermy is used for birds and mammals because stuffing preserves their natural shape and colour better than formalin jars. School and college museums are important for hands-on learning in biology.

  • Collections of preserved plant and animal specimens
  • Specimens preserved in formalin (10%–40% formaldehyde) in transparent jars
  • Larger animals (birds, mammals) are stuffed and preserved — science called Taxidermy
  • Collections include shells, skeletons, insect boxes
  • Reference hubs for biodiversity studies
1.5 Zoological Parks & 1.6 Biodiversity Parks ▶

💡 Explanation: In-situ conservation is always better because organisms are conserved in their natural environment where they can interact with other species and maintain ecological balance. Ex-situ conservation (zoos, herbaria) is a backup when in-situ is not possible. A biodiversity park restores degraded land by planting native species — it brings back natural ecosystems on barren land.

Zoo (Zoological Park):

  • Wild animals kept in captivity; conditions similar to natural habitat
  • This is ex-situ conservation
  • Naturalists study food habits and behaviour here

Biodiversity Park:

  • Ecological assemblage of species forming self-sustaining communities on degraded/barren landscapes
  • This is in-situ conservation
  • Example: Late Uttamrao Patil Biodiversity Park, Gureghar, Mahabaleshwar
TypeMeaningExample
In-situConservation in natural habitatBiodiversity parks, sacred groves
Ex-situConservation outside natural habitatZoos, botanical gardens, herbaria
1.7 Key & Biodiversity Tools ▶

💡 Explanation: A key works like a decision tree — at every step you choose between two opposite characteristics (e.g. "has wings / does not have wings"). Each choice leads you closer to the correct identification. Biological keys are essential tools for field scientists who discover new species. Dichotomous keys (two choices at each step) are most commonly used. The statement at each step is called a "lead" — each lead narrows down the possibilities until you reach the organism's identity.

Key: A taxonomical aid used for classification of plants and animals.

  • Based on contrasting characters
  • One contrasting character accepted, other rejected
  • Statement in a key = lead
  • Keys are normally analytical in nature

Other Biodiversity Tools:

ToolDescription
FloraPlant life occurring in a particular area at a particular time
MonographDescribes any one selected biological group in detail
ManualKeys for identification of species in a particular area

Notable Scientist – Dr. S. P. Agharkar ▶

💡 Explanation: Dr. Agharkar is an inspiring example of Indian scientific contribution. His discovery of freshwater jellyfish (previously known only in Africa) in India was groundbreaking — it proved that Indian biodiversity was vastly unexplored. His work on Western Ghats is especially important because Western Ghats is one of the world's 8 biodiversity hotspots. ARI (Agharkar Research Institute) in Pune continues his legacy of biodiversity research.

  • One of India's leading botanists; born November 1884 in Malvan, Maharashtra
  • Explored biodiversity of Western Ghats
  • Discovered a species of freshwater jellyfish previously known only in Africa
  • Findings published in scientific journal Nature in 1912
  • Institute ARI, Pune has been named after him
🔬 Chapter 5 – Cell Structure & Organization
5.1 Cell Basics, Microscopy & Cell Sizes ▶

💡 Explanation: Cell size and microscopy are basic but frequently tested facts. The smallest cell (Mycoplasma) is a bacterium with no cell wall — it is also the cause of a type of pneumonia. The largest cell (ostrich egg) is visible to the naked eye — the entire egg yolk is one single cell! The electron microscope uses beams of electrons instead of light — electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, allowing much greater magnification and resolution.

Definition: Cell is the structural and functional unit of life capable of independent existence and can perform all functions of life.

MicroscopeMagnificationUse
Simple50–100×Larger cells
Compound (Light)1000×+General cell study
Electron MicroscopeUp to 500,000×Interior of cell ★

Cell Size Facts:

  • Smallest cell → Mycoplasma (0.3 µm)
  • Bacterial cell → 3–5 µm
  • Largest cell → Ostrich egg (~15 cm)
  • Longest cell → Nerve cell (Neuron)
5.2 Cell Theory & Modern Cell Theory ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The cell is the basic unit of life — just as a brick is the basic unit of a building. Schleiden and Schwann proposed the cell theory but they could not explain where new cells came from. Virchow filled this gap with his statement "Omnis cellula-e-cellulla" — all cells come from pre-existing cells. This became the foundation of modern cell biology and also explained why cancer cells keep dividing uncontrollably.

  • Matthias Schleiden (1838) — German botanist; plant tissues composed of cells
  • Theodore Schwann (1839) — British zoologist; animals & plants made of cells
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855) — new cells arise from pre-existing cells: Omnis cellula-e-cellulla

Postulates of Modern Cell Theory:

  • All living organisms are made up of cells
  • Living cells arise from pre-existing cells
  • Cell is the structural and functional unit of life
  • Total activities of cells = activity of organism
  • Cells show transformation of energy
  • Cells contain DNA & RNA in nucleus and cytoplasm
✍️ Trick: Schleiden=Plants | Schwann=Animals | Virchow=New cells from old
5.3 Totipotency & Stem Cells ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Totipotency is the remarkable ability of a single cell to grow into an entire organism. Stem cells in embryos are totipotent — they haven't yet been told what to become. As the embryo develops, cells become specialised and lose this ability (differentiation). Scientists are trying to use stem cells to regenerate damaged organs — like growing a new heart or kidney from a patient's own cells.

Totipotency: Capacity of a living nucleated cell to differentiate and divide to form any other type of cell and thereby a complete new organism.

  • Cell is totipotent because it has the entire genetic information in its nucleus
  • Embryonic animal cells are totipotent → called Stem Cells
  • Stem cells have great medical applications including cure for diseases

Singer & Nicholson's Fluid Mosaic Model (1972) is the most tested topic in cell biology. Venkatraman Ramakrishnan is the most recent Nobel laureate related to cell biology — important for current affairs marks too.

5.5 Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Prokaryotic cells are ancient and simple — no compartments, no nucleus, DNA just floating in cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells evolved later and are far more complex — the nucleus protects DNA, membrane-bound organelles allow specialised functions in separate compartments. The 70S vs 80S ribosome difference is medically important — antibiotics like streptomycin target 70S bacterial ribosomes without affecting our 80S ribosomes!

5.6 Prokaryotic Cell – Detailed Structure ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Prokaryotic cell structure is the most primitive and simplest form. The glycocalyx (capsule) protects bacteria from the host's immune system — this is why capsulated bacteria like Streptococcus pneumoniae cause more severe disease. Mesosomes are important because bacteria have no mitochondria — mesosomes take over the functions of respiration and DNA attachment. Plasmids are used in genetic engineering to insert useful genes into bacteria (e.g. insulin production).

Cell Envelope (3-layered):

  • Outer → Glycocalyx (slime layer = loose / capsule = tough)
  • Middle → Cell Wall (Gram +ve: Peptidoglycan | Gram –ve: Murein)
  • Inner → Plasma Membrane (phospholipid bilayer)

Gram Staining — developed by Danish bacteriologist Hans Christian Gram

Mesosomes — infoldings of cell membrane; help in:

  • Cell wall formation
  • Cellular respiration
  • DNA replication

Genetic Material:

  • Single circular coiled DNA attached to mesosome
  • Replication pattern: Theta model
  • DNA NOT with histone proteins → NOT called chromatin
  • Plasmids — extrachromosomal self-replicating DNA: F-plasmid (reproduction) | R-plasmid (antibiotic resistance)
✍️ Basal body of bacterial flagella = smallest motor in the world!
5.7 Plasma Membrane & Fluid Mosaic Model ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The Fluid Mosaic Model explains why the membrane is called "fluid" — the phospholipid molecules and proteins can move sideways (laterally) within the membrane, like icebergs floating in a sea of lipids. This fluidity is essential for functions like vesicle formation, cell signalling, and receptor movement. The hydrophobic tails always face inward — away from water — which is why the bilayer is so stable.

  • Thin, quasifluid structure; thickness → 75 Å
  • Under electron microscope → trilamellate (3 layers)
  • Contains lipids (mostly phospholipids) arranged in bilayer
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic — one hydrophilic polar head + two hydrophobic non-polar tails

Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicholson, 1972):

  • Phospholipid bilayer + proteins; proteins like "icebergs in sea of lipids"
  • Intrinsic/Transmembrane proteins — span entire membrane; form channels
  • Extrinsic/Peripheral proteins — found on two surfaces of membrane

Transport:

  • Passive Transport — no energy; along concentration gradient; water by osmosis
  • Active Transport — against concentration gradient; requires ATP; e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump
✍️ Passive = no Power | Active = ATP needed
5.8 Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: RER is the cell's protein factory — ribosomes on its surface make proteins that are then packaged and sent out. Pancreatic cells that make digestive enzymes have lots of RER. SER handles lipids and detoxification — liver cells that neutralise alcohol have abundant SER. The ER is the largest organelle in a cell and forms a continuous network from nucleus to cell membrane.

Present in all eukaryotic cells except ova and mature RBCs. Forms more than 50% of total membrane of a eukaryotic cell.

FeatureRER (Rough)SER (Smooth)
RibosomesPresent (studded)Absent
Main functionProtein synthesisLipid/steroid synthesis
Also doesSecretory proteinsDetoxification; Ca²⁺ storage
ExamplePancreatic cells (insulin)Liver cells (detox), Muscle cells
✍️ RER=Rough=Ribosomes(protein) | SER=Smooth=Steroids+Storage
5.9 Golgi Complex ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The Golgi complex is the cell's post office and courier service. Products made in the ER arrive at the cis face, get modified (like adding sugar labels), packaged into vesicles, and shipped from the trans face to their destination — secretion outside, lysosomes, or other organelles. The "address label" on each vesicle tells it exactly where to go.

Golgi Complex = assembly + manufacturing + packaging + transport unit of cell ("Post Office of the cell")

  • Consists of stacks of membranous sacs = cisternae
  • Cis face — forming/receiving side (from ER)
  • Trans face — maturing/secreting side (products leave)
  • Cisternae in Golgi are NOT physically connected to each other

Functions:

  • Modification of ER secretions (glycolipids & glycoproteins)
  • Production of own secretions (e.g. pectin in plant cells)
  • Markers imprinted on vesicle membrane to identify target cell/organelle

Cisternal Maturation Model — cisternae themselves mature moving from cis to trans face

✍️ CIS = Come IN (receiving) | TRANS = Transfer OUT (shipping)
5.10 Lysosomes ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Lysosomes are the cell's recycling system. They digest worn-out organelles, bacteria that entered the cell, and foreign particles. The acidic pH inside lysosomes activates the digestive enzymes — if they were active at normal pH they would digest the cell itself. When a cell is damaged or old, autophagic vesicles burst and digest the entire cell — this is called autophagy (self-eating), which is why lysosomes are called "suicide bags."

Lysosomes = dismantling and restructuring units of cell; membrane-bound vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes; enzymes work at acidic pH; arise from Golgi-associated ER.

Types of Lysosomes:

  • Primary — membrane-bound vesicles; enzymes in inactive state
  • Secondary/Heterophagic — formed by fusion with endocytic vesicle
  • Residual Body — vesicle with undigested remains
  • Autophagic Vesicle (Suicide Bags) — digest cell's own damaged organelles ★★

Important facts:

  • Acrosome in sperm = modified lysosome; contains hyaluronidase
  • Tay-Sach's disease — lipase deficiency → fat accumulation → brain damage
  • Help in destruction of malignant cells (T-lymphocytes)
✍️ Lysosome = Suicide Bag / Digestive bag of the cell
5.11 Mitochondria ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Mitochondria produce ATP (the energy currency of cells) through aerobic respiration. The inner membrane folds (cristae) increase surface area to pack more ATP-producing oxysomes. Mitochondria have their own DNA and 70S ribosomes — evidence that they were once free-living bacteria engulfed by a primitive eukaryote (Endosymbiont Theory). This is why mitochondria are called semi-autonomous organelles.

Mitochondria = Powerhouse of the cell; involved in aerobic respiration. Absent in: Prokaryotic cells and RBCs.

  • Double membrane bound organelle
  • Outer membrane — permeable; protein Porin/Parson's particles
  • Inner membrane — selectively permeable; finger-like folds → Cristae
  • Inner chamber → Matrix (circular DNA + RNA + 70S ribosomes + Krebs cycle enzymes)

Oxysomes (F₀-F₁ / Elementary Particles):

  • Head (F₁) = lollipop head → faces matrix → enzyme ATP synthase
  • Foot (F₀) = embedded in inner membrane → proton channel
  • Involved in proton pumping and ATP synthesis

Mitochondria has own DNA + 70S ribosomes → semi-autonomous! (Endosymbiont Theory)

5.12 Plastids & Chloroplast ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Plastids are found only in plant cells and some algae. Chloroplasts (green) do photosynthesis — grana are the stacked thylakoids where light reactions occur. Chromoplasts (coloured) attract insects for pollination with their bright reds, yellows, and oranges. Leucoplasts store nutrients in non-green parts like roots and seeds. Endosymbiont Theory: chloroplasts were once free-living photosynthetic cyanobacteria engulfed by a cell.

Double membraned; contain DNA, RNA and 70S ribosomes. Found in plant cells, algae, some protists.

TypePigmentFunction / Example
LeucoplastsNone (white)Storage: Amyloplast(starch), Elaioplast(oil), Aleuroplast(protein)
ChromoplastsCarotene & XanthophyllRed/yellow/orange colour of fruits & flowers
ChloroplastsChlorophyll (green)Photosynthesis

Chloroplast Structure:

  • Inside inner membrane → membranous sacs = Thylakoids
  • Thylakoids stacked as Grana (singular: granum)
  • Grana connected by membranous tubules = Stroma lamellae
  • Space outside thylakoids = Stroma
  • Stroma also contains DNA and ribosomes

Endosymbiont Theory ★★: Primitive eukaryote engulfed aerobic prokaryote → became mitochondrion; another engulfed photosynthetic prokaryote → became chloroplast. Both are semi-autonomous.

✍️ Leuco=White | Chromo=Colour | Chloro=Green
5.13 Nucleus ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The nucleus is the control centre of the cell — like the brain. It stores all genetic information (DNA) that directs every activity of the cell. Nucleopores are like security gates — they control what enters and exits the nucleus. The nucleolus is the factory within the factory — it makes ribosomal RNA needed to build ribosomes, which then make proteins. Without a nucleus, a cell cannot survive long (like mature RBCs which have no nucleus and only live 120 days).

Nucleus = Master cell organelle; contains entire genetic information.

Interphase nucleus = nuclear envelope + nucleoplasm + nucleolus + chromatin network

  • Nuclear Envelope — double-layered; two membranes separated by perinuclear space (10–50 nm)
  • Nuclear lamina — network of protein fibres; maintains shape of nucleus
  • Nucleopores — small openings guarded by pore complexes; regulate flow of substances

Nucleolus:

  • Made of rRNA + ribosomal proteins
  • Site of ribosome biogenesis
  • NOT bound by membrane
  • Oocyte → large nucleolus | Sperm → small nucleolus

Chromatin:

  • Chromatin = DNA + histone proteins + non-histone proteins + RNA
  • Euchromatin — more DNA; genetically active
  • Heterochromatin — more proteins, less DNA; genetically inert
  • Normal human cell → 46 chromosomes
5.14 Ribosomes ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Ribosomes are universal — found in every living cell, from bacteria to humans. They translate mRNA (the genetic message) into proteins. The size difference (70S prokaryotic vs 80S eukaryotic) is medically important — antibiotics like streptomycin and erythromycin specifically block 70S ribosomes, killing bacteria without harming our 80S ribosomes. Free ribosomes make proteins used inside the cell; bound ribosomes (on RER) make proteins to be exported.

Ribosomes = Protein factories of the cell. First observed by Pallade (1953). Made of rRNA + Proteins; no membranous covering.

Small subunit30S (16S rRNA + 21 proteins)40S (18S rRNA + 33 proteins)

  • Free ribosomes → produce enzymatic proteins used in cytoplasm
  • Free ribosomes form chains = Polyribosomes

5.15 Cilia & Flagella – Axoneme (9+2) ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Cilia and flagella are both used for movement, but differ in size and number. Cilia beat in coordinated waves — like oars of a rowing team — to move the cell or move fluid over the cell surface (e.g. mucus in respiratory tract). Flagella work like a propeller — few but powerful. The 9+2 axoneme arrangement is universal in all eukaryotic cilia and flagella — the peripheral doublets slide against each other powered by dynein motors to produce the bending motion.

  • Fine hair-like membrane-bound protoplasmic outgrowths
  • Cilia — small, many in number; act as oars
  • Flagella — longer, few in number

Structure: Basal body + Basal plate + Shaft

  • Basal body — derived from centriole; 9 peripheral triplets
  • Axoneme — core; shows 9+2 arrangement (9 peripheral doublets + 2 single central fibrils) ★★
  • Central sheath connected to peripheral doublets by radial spokes
✍️ AXONEME = 9+2 — MOST asked in exams!
5.16 Centrioles & Cytoskeleton ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Centrioles are only in animal cells — plant cells manage cell division without them (they use other proteins instead). During cell division, centrioles duplicate and move to opposite poles to form the spindle apparatus that pulls chromosomes apart. The cytoskeleton is the cell's internal skeleton — microtubules are like rigid bones giving shape, microfilaments are like muscles allowing movement, and intermediate filaments provide mechanical strength.

Centrosome — found near nucleus of animal cell; contains a pair of cylindrical structures → centrioles.

  • Each cylinder = nine sets of triplet microtubules made of tubulin
  • Proximal end looks like a cartwheel
  • Functions: assembly of spindle apparatus during cell division; form basal body of cilia & flagella

Cytoskeleton:
