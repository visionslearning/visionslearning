3.1 Detection of Radioactive Radiations

Methods of Detection

Radioactive radiations (α, β, γ) are invisible but can be detected by their interactions with matter. Main detection methods:

  • Ionisation methods (GM Counter)
  • Photographic methods
  • Scintillation methods (Electrometer, PMT)
  • Fluorescence methods

Photomultiplier Tube (P.M.T.)

  • Also called an electron multiplier tube
  • Surrounded by a photosensitive cathode and anode
  • Dynodes are arranged in increasing order of positive potential
  • Incident electrons hit first dynode → secondary electrons produced
  • Each dynode multiplies electrons → amplified current
  • Aluminum foil acts as light reflector of P.M.T.

Electrometer

  • Connected to anode of PMT
  • Measures amplified current from photomultiplier tube
  • Secondary electrons from cesium/antimony metal and silver oxide are multiplied in multiples of incident electrons

Phosphor / Scintillator

  • Solid organic phosphors: naphthalene, anthracene
  • Liquid: BaPt(CN)₄ can also be used
  • Thin aluminum foil reflects light to photocathode
3.2 Applications of Radioisotopes as Tracers

What are Radioactive Tracers?

Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) are used as tracers because they behave chemically identical to normal isotopes but can be detected by their radioactive emissions. They help track the path of atoms/molecules in chemical and biological processes.

💡 Tracers are like "labels" — same chemistry, but detectable!

(i) Photosynthesis Mechanism

Plants use CO₂ and H₂O to synthesize carbohydrates. Using isotopes ¹⁴C and ¹⁸O:

6 C¹⁶O₂ + 6 H₂¹⁸O → C₆H₁₂¹⁸O₆ + 6 ¹⁶O₂
6 C¹⁸O₂ + 6 H₂¹⁶O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 ¹⁸O₂

Result: The O₂ released in photosynthesis comes from water (H₂O), not from CO₂.

(ii) Hydrolysis of Esters

Using labelled water H₂¹⁸O to determine which bond breaks during ester hydrolysis:

  • Path I: Acyl-oxygen bond breaks (acid + alcohol)
  • Path II: Alkyl-oxygen bond breaks

Experiment with ¹⁸O water showed the acid contains ¹⁸O, not the alcohol → acyl-oxygen bond breaks (Path I is correct).

(iii) Friedel-Crafts Reaction Mechanism

AlCl₃ is the catalyst. Using labelled chlorine in HCl gas:

C₆H₆ + CH₃Cl → (AlCl₃ catalyst) → C₆H₅CH₃ + HCl↑

The labelled Cl from CH₃Cl (not from AlCl₃) appeared in the HCl evolved → confirms AlCl₃ forms an intermediate complex (CH₃–AlCl₃) which then donates the CH₃⁺ carbocation to benzene ring.

3.3 Nuclear Reactor

Fissile vs Fertile Materials

Fissile Materials:
  • Undergo fission directly with slow neutrons
  • Examples: ²³⁵U, ²³⁹Pu, ²³³U
Fertile Materials:
  • Non-fissile but converted to fissile by neutron bombardment
  • Examples: ²³⁸U (→ ²³⁹Pu), ²³²Th (→ ²³³U)
💡 ²³⁵U and ²³⁹Pu are called fertile elements. ²³²Th is fertile material (India — monazite sands in Kerala).

Essential Components of a Nuclear Reactor

ComponentMaterial UsedFunction
(i) Core / Fuel²³⁵U or ²³⁹PuFissionable material — provides nuclear fuel
(ii) ModeratorHeavy water (D₂O), graphite, ordinary waterSlows down fast neutrons to thermal (slow) neutrons
(iii) Control RodsCadmium or boron steelAbsorb excess neutrons; maintain multiplication factor k = 1
(iv) CoolantHeavy water (D₂O) or graphiteRemoves heat generated in core by fission
(v) ShieldingThick lead sheetsPrevents leakage of radiation from reactor
(vi) Neutron ReflectorsHelium, CO₂Prevent neutrons from escaping from core surface

Power Reactor

A power reactor is used to generate electric power. The fission energy is converted to heat → steam → turbine → electricity.

  • Construction: Nuclear reactor core surrounded by thick blocks of carbon (graphite) as moderator + shield
  • Aluminium boxes contain ²³⁵U in long pins placed in alternate rows in graphite blocks
  • D₂O (coolant) is circulated through pipes around the fuel core
  • Cadmium rods act as neutron absorbers; control neutron density and temperature
💡 India has one of the largest deposits of thorium (²³²Th) as monazite sand in Kerala!

Neutron Flux & Multiplication Factor

Neutron flux = number of neutrons emitted per second from surface area of fuel
For Ra–Be alloy: neutron flux ~ 10⁶ neutrons/cm²/s

Multiplication Factor (k):
k = 1 → critical (sustained chain reaction)
k > 1 → supercritical (explosion – bomb)
k < 1 → subcritical (reaction dies out)

Q1. Describe the construction and working of a Photomultiplier Tube (PMT). 5 Marks
Photomultiplier Tube (PMT): Also known as electron multiplier tube. Used to detect and amplify scintillations from radioactive decay.

Construction:
  • A crystal of phosphor (e.g., NaI activated by Tl) surrounded by a photosensitive cathode
  • Aluminum foil acts as a light reflector
  • Dynodes (multiple electrodes) arranged in increasing order of positive potential
  • Connected to an electrometer to measure current
Working:
  • Nuclear radiation strikes the phosphor → produces a flash of light (scintillation)
  • Light falls on photocathode → ejects photoelectrons
  • Photoelectrons accelerated to first dynode → secondary electrons produced
  • Secondary electrons multiplied at each subsequent dynode
  • Large amplified current measured by electrometer — this is the detected signal
Advantages: Simple to operate, high sensitivity, can distinguish between different types of radiation, efficient for counting γ-rays.
Q2. What are radioactive tracers? Explain their use in the study of photosynthesis. 5 Marks
Radioactive Tracers: Radioisotopes are atoms with radioactive nuclei that behave chemically identical to stable isotopes but can be detected by their radioactive emissions. When incorporated into compounds, they "trace" or follow the path of atoms through chemical/biological reactions.

Application in Photosynthesis:
The overall reaction of photosynthesis is:
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
The question was: does the O₂ released come from CO₂ or H₂O?

Using ¹⁸O labelled water (H₂¹⁸O):
6C¹⁶O₂ + 6H₂¹⁸O → C₆H₁₂¹⁸O₆ + 6¹⁶O₂ — No, this gives O₂ without ¹⁸O
Using ¹⁸O labelled CO₂ (C¹⁸O₂):
6C¹⁸O₂ + 6H₂¹⁶O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6¹⁸O₂ — O₂ released has ¹⁸O
Conclusion: The oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water (H₂O), not from CO₂. This was confirmed by detecting ¹⁸O in evolved O₂ only when ¹⁸O-labelled CO₂ was used.
Q3. Explain the use of radioactive tracers in the hydrolysis of esters. 4 Marks
Problem: In acid/base hydrolysis of esters (R–CO–O–R'), the question was whether the acyl-oxygen bond or the alkyl-oxygen bond breaks.

Experiment: Labelled water (H₂¹⁸O) was used in the hydrolysis:
R–CO–O–R' + H₂¹⁸O → ?
Path I (acyl-oxygen bond breaks): ¹⁸O appears in the acid → R–C(=O)–¹⁸OH + R'OH
Path II (alkyl-oxygen bond breaks): ¹⁸O would appear in the alcohol → RCOOH + R'¹⁸OH

Result: The ¹⁸O was found in the acid (not the alcohol). Hence, Path I is correct — the acyl-oxygen bond breaks during ester hydrolysis.

Q5. Describe the essential components of a nuclear reactor with their functions. 8 Marks
A nuclear reactor is a device in which controlled fission chain reaction is maintained to produce energy.

(i) Core / Nuclear Fuel: The fissionable material (²³⁵U or ²³⁹Pu) placed in the core. Energy is produced here by fission reaction.

(ii) Moderator: Heavy water (D₂O), graphite, or ordinary water. Slows down fast neutrons to thermal (slow) neutrons required for sustained fission of ²³⁵U.

(iii) Control Rods: Made of cadmium or boron steel. These absorb excess neutrons to maintain multiplication factor k = 1 (critical). If k > 1, rods are inserted more; if k < 1, they are withdrawn.

(iv) Coolant: Heavy water (D₂O) or graphite circulated through pipes around the fuel core. Removes heat generated and transfers it to a heat exchanger to produce steam for power generation.

(v) Shielding: Thick layers of lead sheets surround the reactor. Prevents leakage of harmful radiation (α, β, γ) to the environment.

(vi) Neutron Reflectors: Substances like helium or CO₂ around the core surface. Reflect neutrons back into the core to prevent their escape.
Q6. Explain the Friedel-Crafts reaction mechanism using radioactive tracers. 4 Marks
Reaction:
C₆H₆ + CH₃Cl → (AlCl₃) → C₆H₅CH₃ + HCl
Question: Does the AlCl₃ catalyst donate Cl to form HCl, or does the Cl come from CH₃Cl?

Tracer Experiment: Labelled chlorine was used only in the HCl gas (not in AlCl₃ or CH₃Cl). Results showed that labelled Cl appeared in the HCl product, not from AlCl₃.

Mechanism Confirmed:
  • AlCl₃ interacts with CH₃Cl to form intermediate complex: CH₃⁺...AlCl₄⁻
  • CH₃⁺ (carbocation) attacks benzene ring (electrophilic substitution)
  • HCl is released and AlCl₃ is regenerated as catalyst
This confirms AlCl₃ forms an intermediate but is not consumed, acting as a true catalyst.

4.1 Adsorption

Surface Chemistry — Introduction

Surface chemistry is a branch of physical chemistry dealing with properties of surfaces and phase boundaries. The surface phenomenon involving accumulation of one substance on the surface of another is called adsorption.

  • Adsorbent: The substance on whose surface adsorption occurs (e.g., activated charcoal, silica gel, Pd, Ni)
  • Adsorbate: The substance that gets adsorbed on the surface (e.g., H₂ on Ni, NH₃ on activated charcoal)
  • Desorption: Removal of adsorbed substance from adsorbent surface
  • Sorption: Both adsorption and absorption occurring simultaneously
💡 Adsorption = surface phenomenon; Absorption = bulk phenomenon

Physical vs Chemical Adsorption

PropertyPhysisorptionChemisorption
ForceWeak van der Waals forcesChemical bond formation (strong)
SpecificityNon-specificHighly specific
ReversibilityReversibleIrreversible
LayersMultilayerUnimolecular layer only
TemperatureOccurs at low temperature; decreases with increase in TOccurs at high temperature; increases initially then decreases
Heat evolvedLow: <40 kJ/molHigh: 40–400 kJ/mol
Activation energyLowHigh
ExampleN₂ on Fe surfaceH₂ on Ni surface

Factors Affecting Adsorption

  • Surface area: Greater surface area → greater adsorption. Activated charcoal has large surface area.
  • Temperature: Physisorption decreases with increase in temperature; chemisorption increases initially then decreases.
  • Pressure: Adsorption increases with pressure (for gases).
  • Nature of adsorbate: Gases with higher critical temperature are more easily adsorbed (e.g., SO₂ > CO₂ > H₂).
  • Nature of adsorbent: Activated charcoal, silica gel, alumina are good adsorbents due to high surface area.
4.2 Adsorption Isotherms

Adsorption Isotherm

A graphical curve showing the relationship between the amount of substance adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent and the equilibrium pressure at constant temperature is called an adsorption isotherm.

Five types of adsorption isotherms (Brunauer classification) have been reported for physical adsorption.

Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

An empirical equation given by Freundlich (valid only at low pressures):

x/m = k·P^(1/n)    (n > 1)

Taking log: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)·log P

where x = mass adsorbed, m = mass of adsorbent, P = pressure, k and n are constants.

  • Plotting log(x/m) vs log P gives a straight line
  • Slope = 1/n, Intercept = log k
  • Limitation: Valid only at low pressures; entirely empirical (no theoretical background)

Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm (1916)

Langmuir derived a theoretical equation based on kinetic theory of gases.

Postulates:

  • All gases behave ideally
  • Adsorbed layer is unimolecular (monolayer)
  • Gas molecules adsorbed on selective sites (one site = one molecule)
  • Bond between gas molecule and adsorbent site is sufficiently strong (chemical)
  • Heat of adsorption is same for all sites and independent of surface coverage

Derivation:

Let θ = fraction of surface covered. Then (1 − θ) = fraction uncovered.

Rate of adsorption = k_a × (1 − θ) × P
Rate of desorption = k_d × θ

At equilibrium: k_a(1−θ)P = k_d·θ
Solving: θ = (k_a/k_d)·P / [1 + (k_a/k_d)·P]

Let b = k_a/k_d (proportionality constant):
θ = bP / (1 + bP) ← Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm

Special cases:

  • At low pressure: bP << 1 → θ = bP (linear — follows Freundlich with 1/n = 1)
  • At high pressure: bP >> 1 → θ = 1 (saturation — monolayer complete)

Tyndall Effect

When a beam of light is passed through a colloidal solution, the path of light becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles. This is called the Tyndall Effect.

  • Observed in colloids — NOT in true solutions (particles too small to scatter)
  • The illuminated path is called the Tyndall cone
  • Used to distinguish true solutions from colloids
💡 Examples: Blue colour of sky, visibility of headlight beams in fog, beam of light in dusty room — all Tyndall Effect!

Brownian Motion

The continuous random zigzag motion of colloidal particles in a dispersion medium is called Brownian motion. It was first observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains.

  • Caused by unequal bombardment of colloidal particles by molecules of dispersion medium
  • More pronounced for smaller particles and less viscous medium
  • Brownian motion prevents settling of colloidal particles → responsible for kinetic stability of colloids
💡 Brownian motion is direct evidence of the kinetic molecular theory of matter.

Coagulation (Flocculation) of Colloids

The process of settling of colloidal particles by adding an electrolyte or by other means is called coagulation or flocculation. Colloidal particles carry a charge — adding opposite ions neutralises the charge → particles aggregate and settle.

Methods of coagulation:

  • By adding electrolyte
  • By electrophoresis (particles migrate to electrode and get discharged)
  • By mixing oppositely charged colloids
  • By boiling (increases collision between particles)

Hardy-Schulze Rule

The coagulating power of an electrolyte depends on the valency of the active ion (ion of opposite charge to the colloid).

Hardy-Schulze Rule:
Higher the valency of the coagulating ion → greater the coagulating power

For a negative colloid (e.g., As₂S₃ sol):
Al³⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺    (coagulating power)

For a positive colloid (e.g., Fe(OH)₃ sol):
[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻    (coagulating power)
💡 Hardy-Schulze Rule: Coagulating power ∝ (valency of active ion)⁶ approximately (Schulze rule of six).

Gold Number (Protective Colloids)

A protective colloid is a lyophilic colloid that, when added to a lyophobic colloid, prevents its coagulation by forming a protective layer around the particles.

Gold Number (introduced by Zsigmondy): The minimum mass (in mg) of a protective colloid required to just prevent the coagulation of 10 mL of a standard gold sol (red → blue colour change) on adding 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution.

Smaller the gold number → Better the protective power

Gold numbers of some colloids:
Gelatin: 0.005–0.01 (best protective colloid)
Haemoglobin: 0.03–0.07
Gum arabic: 0.1–0.2
Starch: 25
💡 Gelatin has the smallest gold number → most effective protective colloid. Used in ice cream, medicines, photographic films.

Zeta Potential

The electric potential at the slipping/shear plane between the colloidal particle (with its bound ions layer) and the bulk of the dispersion medium is called zeta potential (ζ).

  • Higher zeta potential → greater electrostatic repulsion between particles → more stable colloid
  • Coagulation occurs when zeta potential is reduced to zero (by adding electrolyte)
  • Zeta potential is related to electrophoretic mobility of colloidal particles
4.3 Colloids, Emulsions & Gels

Colloids — Key Terms

TermDefinition
ColloidHeterogeneous system with particle size 1–1000 nm (between true solution and suspension)
Dispersed phaseThe substance distributed as particles
Dispersion mediumThe medium in which particles are distributed
CMCCritical Micelle Concentration — concentration at which micelles begin to form
MicelleSpherical aggregate of surfactant molecules with hydrophilic heads outward and hydrophobic tails inward
ElectrophoresisMigration of colloidal particles under applied electric field
Electro-osmosisMovement of dispersion medium through a porous membrane under applied electric field

Electrophoresis & Electro-osmosis

Electrophoresis: Migration of dispersed colloidal particles towards oppositely charged electrode under electric field. If electrophoresis is prevented, the dispersion medium moves instead — this is electro-osmosis.

Streaming Potential (Quincke's Equation):

ζ = Pη² / (2DE)     [ζ = zeta potential]

Dorn Effect / Sedimentation Potential: Difference in potential created between electrodes when small particles fall in a liquid under gravity.

ζ = 4πηS / (Dk)    where S = streaming liquid, k = specific conductance, D = dielectric constant

Emulsions

Emulsions are colloidal systems of two immiscible liquids dispersed in each other with the help of an emulsifier.

Types:
  • O/W (Oil in Water): Oil drops dispersed in water (e.g., milk, cold cream)
  • W/O (Water in Oil): Water drops dispersed in oil (e.g., butter, vanishing cream)
Identification Rule:
  • If γ_a > γ_b → water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion is stable
  • If γ_b > γ_a → oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion is stable

Types of Emulsifiers

  • (i) Surfactants: Break up grease/oil on surfaces by emulsifying action — long chain alkyl sulphonates, soaps
  • (ii) Proteins: e.g., casein in milk — lyophilic (water-loving) emulsifier
  • (iii) Carbon black emulsions: Emulsify and stabilise water-in-oil and oil-in-water emulsions
💡 Emulsifier reduces interfacial tension between oil and water → stable emulsion forms

Demulsification

Breaking of an emulsion into two separate liquid layers is called demulsification. Methods:

  • Physical: Heating, freezing, or centrifugation (e.g., breaking of milk emulsion to get cream)
  • Chemical: Adding electrolytes, surfactants, or detergents to destroy the emulsifier film

Example: Splitting of milk by adding lime juice → fat separates.

Gels

A gel is a colloidal system in which a liquid is dispersed in a solid medium (semi-solid jelly-like form).

  • Xerogels: Contain more solid and less liquid — rigid structure (e.g., sheet of gelatin, cellophane)
  • Other gels: Silicic acid, ferric hydroxide, gum arabic, gelatin, agar

Classification by mechanical properties:

  • On basis of mechanical properties (rigidity) — varies from substance to substance
4.4 Surfactants — Properties & Applications

What are Surfactants?

Surfactants (surface-active agents) are substances that decrease surface tension when added to a liquid. They concentrate at the interface and have a dual structure:

  • Hydrophilic head (polar/water-loving)
  • Hydrophobic tail (non-polar/water-hating, usually a long hydrocarbon chain)
Gibbs Adsorption Equation:
S = −(c/RT) × (dγ/dc)
where S = excess concentration at surface, γ = surface tension, c = bulk concentration

If dγ/dc is negative (surfactant) → S is positive → substance concentrates at surface = positive adsorption

Classification of Surfactants

TypeExampleActive Ion
AnionicSodium cetyl sulphate (C₁₆H₃₃SO₄Na⁺)Surface activity in anion
CationicCetyl trimethyl ammonium bromideSurface activity in cation
Non-ionicPolyglycol ether (C₁₆H₃₃(OCH₂CH₂)ₙOH)No charge
AmphotericAmino acids, protein moleculesBoth ionic forms possible

Micelle Structure & CMC

At low concentrations, surfactants exist as individual molecules. Above a critical concentration called CMC (Critical Micelle Concentration), surfactant molecules aggregate into spherical structures called micelles:

  • Hydrophilic heads face the water (outward)
  • Hydrophobic tails point inward (away from water)
  • CMC is a definite value for each surfactant
  • Above CMC, conductance of ionic surfactant drops — micelles form
💡 Ionic micelles behave like macromolecular electrolytes — conducts less than expected

Detergents vs Soaps

Soaps: Sodium/potassium salts of higher fatty acids (e.g., sodium stearate). Ineffective in hard water — precipitate with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions.

Detergents: Cleaning agents with sulphonate group. Work even in hard water because sulphonates of Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ are soluble.

Example: Linear alkyl benzene sulphonate (LABS)

Structure: Linear alkyl chain + Benzene ring + –SO₃⁻Na⁺ (sulphonate group)

Applications of Surfactants

ApplicationHow it Works
Detergent & cleaningMicelles trap dirt/grease in hydrophobic core; rinsed away with water
PesticidesImprove spray coverage — oil-in-water emulsions; reduce pesticide amount needed
Food IndustryStabilise emulsions in cream, ice cream, salad dressings (silicone surfactants at low concentration)
Wetting agentsSurfactants help liquids spread over surfaces (e.g., water on waxy leaves)

Donnan Membrane Equilibrium

When a protein (non-diffusible macromolecular ion) is separated from a salt solution by a semipermeable membrane, the distribution of diffusible ions across the membrane is unequal — this is the Donnan Membrane Equilibrium.

  • The non-diffusible ions prevent small ions from escaping through the membrane
  • At equilibrium: [Na⁺]inside × [Cl⁻]inside = [Na⁺]outside × [Cl⁻]outside
  • Creates osmotic pressure difference and electrical potential difference

Q2. State and explain the Freundlich adsorption isotherm. What are its limitations? 5 Marks
Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm: An empirical relationship between the amount of gas adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent and the pressure at constant temperature.
x/m = k·P^(1/n)    (n > 1)
Taking logarithm:
log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)·log P
This is a straight line equation: plotting log(x/m) vs log P gives slope = 1/n and intercept = log k.

Limitations:
  • Valid only at low pressures — fails at high pressures
  • Purely empirical — no theoretical basis
  • k and n are temperature-dependent constants
  • Cannot predict saturation adsorption (x/m → ∞ as P → ∞ according to this equation, which is unrealistic)
Q3. Derive the Langmuir adsorption isotherm. State its postulates and limitations. 8 Marks
Postulates of Langmuir Theory:
  • All gases behave ideally
  • Adsorbed layer is monomolecular (monolayer)
  • Gas molecules adsorb on selective sites; one molecule per site
  • Bond between molecule and surface is strong enough
  • Heat of adsorption is same for all sites and independent of coverage
Derivation:
Let θ = fraction of surface covered; (1−θ) = uncovered fraction; P = pressure
Rate of adsorption ∝ (1−θ)·P = k_a(1−θ)P
Rate of desorption ∝ θ = k_d·θ
At equilibrium:
k_a(1−θ)P = k_d·θ
k_aP − k_aθP = k_dθ
k_aP = θ(k_d + k_aP)
θ = k_aP / (k_d + k_aP) = bP / (1 + bP)   [where b = k_a/k_d]
Langmuir Isotherm: θ = bP / (1 + bP)

Limitations:
  • Assumes monolayer — fails at high pressures where multilayer forms
  • Assumes all sites are identical — not always true
  • Ignores interaction between adsorbed molecules
  • Does not explain Type II–V isotherms
Q4. What is an emulsion? Explain types of emulsions and role of emulsifiers. 5 Marks
Emulsion: A colloidal system in which two immiscible liquids (usually oil and water) are dispersed in each other with the help of a third substance called an emulsifier.

Types:
  • O/W (Oil-in-Water): Oil is dispersed phase, water is dispersion medium. Examples: milk, cold cream, vanishing cream.
  • W/O (Water-in-Oil): Water is dispersed phase, oil is medium. Examples: butter, margarine.
Role of Emulsifier:
  • Reduces interfacial tension between oil and water
  • Forms a film around dispersed droplets preventing coalescence
  • Common emulsifiers: soaps, detergents (anionic), long-chain alkyl sulphonates, proteins (casein in milk)
Stability rule: If interfacial tension of dispersion medium side > that of dispersed phase side → stable emulsion forms with the medium surrounding the dispersed phase.
Q5. Explain Gibbs adsorption equation. What are surface-active agents (surfactants)? 5 Marks
Gibbs Adsorption Equation:
For a dilute solution, Gibbs showed that the excess concentration of solute per unit area at the surface is:
S = −(c/RT) × (dγ/dc)
where S = surface excess, γ = surface tension, c = bulk concentration, R = gas constant, T = temperature.

Interpretation:
  • If dγ/dc is negative → S is positive → solute concentrates at surface → called positive adsorption → substance is a surfactant
  • If dγ/dc is positive → S is negative → solute depleted at surface → negative adsorption
Surfactants: Substances that decrease surface tension when dissolved in a solvent. They have dual nature — hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tail (e.g., soaps, detergents, LABS).

Water has high surface tension — when surfactants dissolve, they concentrate at the surface and bring about a large decrease in surface tension.
Q6. Explain electrophoresis and electro-osmosis with diagrams. 6 Marks
Electrophoresis: The migration of colloidal particles (dispersed phase) under the influence of an applied electric field.
  • Negatively charged colloids migrate towards anode (positive electrode)
  • Positively charged colloids migrate towards cathode (negative electrode)
  • Used to determine charge on colloidal particles
Electro-osmosis: When electrophoresis is prevented (particles cannot move), the dispersion medium (liquid) moves in the opposite direction under the electric field.
  • Direction of flow depends on charge of colloidal particles
  • For negatively charged particles: medium flows towards cathode
  • Steady state reached when electro-osmotic pressure equals counter-back pressure
Streaming Potential (Quincke): When a liquid is forced through a porous material, a potential difference arises. Related to zeta potential by:
ζ = Pη² / (2DE)
where P = driving pressure, η = viscosity, D = dielectric constant, E = streaming potential.
Q7. Write a note on applications of surfactants in detergency and pesticides. 5 Marks
A. Detergency:
  • Detergents are surfactants with a sulphonate group (–SO₃⁻) that work even in hard water
  • Cleaning mechanism: Hydrophobic tail of detergent embeds into grease/oil; hydrophilic head faces water
  • Agitation causes grease to be emulsified into small micelles and suspended in water
  • Rinsing removes the dirt-laden micelles
  • Advantage over soaps: Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ sulphonates are soluble → work in hard water
B. Pesticides:
  • Surfactants improve spraying, dispersing, and spreading of pesticides on plant foliage
  • Oil-in-water emulsions of pesticides allow smaller amounts to be used — same effectiveness
  • Wetting property: surfactants reduce contact angle → pesticide spreads over waxy leaves
  • Silicone surfactants work at very low concentrations and are added to spray mixtures to decrease surface tension → larger proportion of each droplet reaches plant surface
Q8. Explain Donnan Membrane Equilibrium. 4 Marks
Donnan Membrane Equilibrium (demonstrated by Gibbs and Donnan):
When a protein (non-diffusible macromolecular ion) in solution is separated from a simple salt solution (e.g., NaCl) by a semipermeable membrane:
  • Protein ions (large) cannot pass through the membrane
  • Small ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) can pass through (diffusible ions)
  • But the non-diffusible ions prevent small ions from distributing equally
At Equilibrium:
[Na⁺]_inside × [Cl⁻]_inside = [Na⁺]_outside × [Cl⁻]_outside
This unequal distribution of ions creates:
  • An osmotic pressure difference across the membrane
  • An electrical potential difference (Donnan potential)
Importance: Explains behaviour of biological membranes, osmotic pressure of protein solutions, and ion transport in living cells.
Q9. State and explain the Hardy-Schulze Rule with examples. 4 Marks
Hardy-Schulze Rule: The coagulating power of an electrolyte depends upon the valency of the active ion (ion bearing opposite charge to the colloidal particles). Higher the valency, greater the coagulating power.

For negatively charged colloid (e.g., As₂S₃ sol):
Al³⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺    (order of coagulating power)
For positively charged colloid (e.g., Fe(OH)₃ sol):
[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻    (order of coagulating power)
Coagulation value: The minimum concentration of electrolyte (in millimoles per litre) required to cause coagulation of a colloid is called its coagulation value. Lower coagulation value = higher coagulating power.
Q10. What is the Tyndall Effect? What is Brownian motion? State their significance. 4 Marks
Tyndall Effect: When a beam of light passes through a colloidal solution, the path of the beam becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles. This phenomenon is called the Tyndall Effect.
  • Not observed in true solutions (particles too small to scatter light)
  • Used to distinguish colloids from true solutions
  • Illuminated path = Tyndall cone
  • Examples: blue colour of sky, headlights in fog, beam of light in dusty room
Brownian Motion: The continuous, random zigzag motion of colloidal particles in a dispersion medium, caused by unequal bombardment by medium molecules.
  • First observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains
  • More pronounced for smaller particles in less viscous medium
  • Responsible for kinetic stability of colloids — prevents settling
  • Provides direct evidence for kinetic molecular theory
Q11. What is gold number? How does it measure protective power of a colloid? 4 Marks
Gold Number (introduced by Zsigmondy): The minimum mass in milligrams of a protective colloid that just prevents the coagulation of 10 mL of a standard red gold sol when 1 mL of 10% NaCl is added to it.

Smaller gold number → Greater protective power
Protective ColloidGold Number
Gelatin0.005–0.01 (best)
Haemoglobin0.03–0.07
Gum arabic0.1–0.2
Starch25 (poor)

Mechanism: The protective colloid forms an adsorbed layer around the lyophobic colloidal particles, preventing the electrolyte ions from reaching and neutralising their charge. This prevents coagulation.

Applications: Gelatin used in photographic films, ice cream, medicines; proteins in blood protect iron oxide particles from coagulating.
Q12. Explain the applications of adsorption in daily life and industry. 5 Marks
  • Gas masks: Activated charcoal in gas masks adsorbs poisonous gases (CO, SO₂, Cl₂) protecting soldiers/workers from toxic environments.
  • Dehumidification: Silica gel (SiO₂) adsorbs moisture from air. Used in packaging of electronics, medicines, and food products.
  • Chromatography: Separation of mixtures by differential adsorption on a stationary adsorbent phase. Alumina and silica gel are common adsorbents.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis: Reactants are adsorbed on catalyst surface (e.g., N₂ and H₂ on Fe surface in Haber process). Adsorption weakens bonds and lowers activation energy.
  • Clarification of sugar: Animal charcoal (bone char) adsorbs coloured impurities from raw sugar solution to produce white refined sugar.
  • Froth flotation: Pine oil selectively adsorbs on sulphide ore particles → they become hydrophobic → float as froth, separated from gangue. Used in metallurgy.
  • Water purification: Activated carbon removes taste, odour, and organic contaminants from drinking water by adsorption.
