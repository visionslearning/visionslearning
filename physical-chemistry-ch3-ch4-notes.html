⚛️ CHAPTER 3 – NUCLEAR CHEMISTRY

3.1 Photomultiplier Tube (PMT)

What is it? A PMT (also called electron multiplier tube) is used to detect radioactive radiations by converting them into measurable electric current. It works on the principle of photoelectric effect + electron multiplication.

Construction

  • A crystal of phosphor (e.g., NaI activated by Tl) placed at the front — converts radiation to light
  • Photosensitive cathode (photocathode) emits electrons when light hits it
  • Dynodes — multiple electrodes arranged in increasing order of positive potential
  • Aluminum foil acts as light reflector to direct light towards photocathode
  • Electrometer connected at anode to measure the final amplified current

Working (Step by Step)

  1. Nuclear radiation (α/β/γ) strikes the phosphor crystal → produces a flash of light (scintillation)
  2. Light falls on photocathode → photoelectrons are ejected
  3. Photoelectrons are accelerated towards the first dynode → hit dynode → produce more secondary electrons
  4. Secondary electrons hit the next dynode (at higher potential) → more electrons produced (multiplication)
  5. This continues through all dynodes → huge amplified pulse of electrons
  6. Electrometer at anode measures the amplified current → this signal counts the radiation
💡 Each dynode multiplies electrons → PMT gives very high sensitivity. Secondary electrons come from cesium/antimony metal or silver oxide.
📝 Marks tip: For 5-mark answer — write construction (3 points) + working (5 steps) + one advantage. Draw a simple box diagram showing phosphor → photocathode → dynodes → anode → electrometer.

3.2 Radioactive Tracers

What are tracers? Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) that are chemically identical to normal atoms but can be detected by their radioactive emissions. They act as "labels" — you can follow exactly where a particular atom goes in a reaction.
💡 Same chemistry, but detectable! Tracer atoms go exactly where normal atoms would go.

(i) Photosynthesis — Where does O₂ come from?

The question: In photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂), does the released O₂ come from CO₂ or from H₂O?

Experiment 1: Used ¹⁸O-labelled water (H₂18O) with normal CO₂:
6C¹⁶O₂ + 6H₂¹⁸O → C₆H₁₂¹⁸O₆ + 6¹⁶O₂ (O₂ has no ¹⁸O)

Experiment 2: Used ¹⁸O-labelled CO₂ with normal water:
6C¹⁸O₂ + 6H₂¹⁶O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6¹⁸O₂ (O₂ has ¹⁸O!)

Conclusion: The O₂ released in photosynthesis comes from water (H₂O), NOT from CO₂.
📝 Marks tip: Write both experiments with equations. State the conclusion clearly. 5-mark answer = definition + both equations + conclusion.

(ii) Hydrolysis of Esters — Which bond breaks?

Question: In ester hydrolysis (R–CO–O–R'), does the acyl-oxygen bond break OR the alkyl-oxygen bond break?

Experiment: Used ¹⁸O-labelled water (H₂18O):
R–CO–O–R' + H₂18O → ?

Path I (acyl-oxygen breaks): ¹⁸O appears in the acid → R–C(=O)–18OH + R'OH
Path II (alkyl-oxygen breaks): ¹⁸O appears in the alcohol → RCOOH + R'–18OH

Result: ¹⁸O found in the acid → Path I (acyl-oxygen bond breaks) is correct.

(iii) Friedel-Crafts Reaction Mechanism

Reaction: C₆H₆ + CH₃Cl → (AlCl₃) → C₆H₅CH₃ + HCl

Question: Does the Cl in HCl come from AlCl₃ or from CH₃Cl?

Experiment: Labelled Cl used only in HCl gas. Result — labelled Cl appeared in evolved HCl.

Mechanism confirmed:
  • AlCl₃ + CH₃Cl → CH₃⁺...AlCl₄⁻ (intermediate complex)
  • CH₃⁺ (carbocation) attacks the benzene ring (electrophilic substitution)
  • HCl is released; AlCl₃ is regenerated (true catalyst, not consumed)

🧪 CHAPTER 4 – SURFACE CHEMISTRY

4.1 Adsorption — Basics

Adsorption: Accumulation of a substance on the surface of another substance at higher concentration compared to the bulk.
Adsorbent: Substance on whose surface adsorption occurs (e.g., activated charcoal, silica gel, Ni, Pd)
Adsorbate: Substance that gets adsorbed (e.g., H₂ on Ni, NH₃ on charcoal)
Desorption: Removal of adsorbed substance from adsorbent surface
Sorption: Simultaneous adsorption + absorption
💡 Adsorption = surface phenomenon | Absorption = bulk phenomenon

4.2 Adsorption Isotherms

Adsorption isotherm: A graph showing amount of substance adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent vs equilibrium pressure at constant temperature.

Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

An empirical equation (no theoretical basis) given by Freundlich. Valid only at low pressures:
x/m = k · P^(1/n)    (n > 1)
Taking logarithm of both sides:
log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) · log P
This is a straight line: Plot log(x/m) vs log P
→ Slope = 1/n    → Intercept = log k
💡 Limitations: Valid only at low P | Purely empirical | k and n change with temperature | Cannot predict saturation (x/m → ∞ as P → ∞, which is unrealistic)

Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm (1916) — Theoretical

Postulates:
  • All gases behave ideally
  • Adsorbed layer is monomolecular (monolayer)
  • Gas molecules adsorb on selective sites; one molecule per site
  • Bond between molecule and surface is strong
  • Heat of adsorption is same for all sites and independent of coverage

Derivation of Langmuir Isotherm

Let θ = fraction of surface covered → (1−θ) = uncovered fraction, P = pressure

Rate of adsorption = k_a × (1−θ) × P
Rate of desorption = k_d × θ

At equilibrium (adsorption = desorption):
k_a(1−θ)P = k_d·θ
k_aP − k_aθP = k_dθ
k_aP = θ(k_d + k_aP)
θ = k_aP / (k_d + k_aP)

Let b = k_a/k_d (equilibrium constant):
θ = bP / (1 + bP)   ← Langmuir Isotherm
Special cases:
• Low pressure (bP << 1): θ ≈ bP → linear relationship (like Freundlich with 1/n = 1)
• High pressure (bP >> 1): θ ≈ 1 → surface fully covered (saturation, monolayer complete)
💡 Limitations: Only monolayer — fails at high P | Assumes identical sites | Ignores interaction between adsorbed molecules | Can't explain Type II–V isotherms
📝 Marks tip (8M): Postulates = 2M | Derivation steps (rate equations → equilibrium → θ formula) = 4M | Special cases = 1M | Limitations = 1M. Write every algebra step clearly!

4.3 Colloids — Properties

Tyndall Effect

When a beam of light is passed through a colloidal solution, the path of light becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles. This is the Tyndall Effect. The illuminated path is called the Tyndall cone.

NOT seen in true solutions (particles too small to scatter light).
Examples: Blue sky, headlights in fog, beam of light in dusty room.

Brownian Motion

Continuous random zigzag motion of colloidal particles caused by unequal bombardment by molecules of the dispersion medium. First observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains.
More pronounced for smaller particles and less viscous medium.
Significance: Prevents settling of colloid → kinetic stability. Direct evidence for kinetic molecular theory.

Coagulation & Hardy-Schulze Rule

Coagulation: Settling of colloidal particles by adding electrolyte → opposite ions neutralise charge → particles aggregate and settle.

Hardy-Schulze Rule: Higher the valency of the active ion (ion of opposite charge to colloid) → greater the coagulating power.

For negative colloid (As₂S₃): Al³⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺
For positive colloid (Fe(OH)₃): [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻

Coagulation value: Minimum concentration (mmol/L) of electrolyte to coagulate a colloid. Lower value = higher coagulating power.

Gold Number (Protective Colloids)

Gold Number (Zsigmondy): Minimum mass (in mg) of protective colloid that just prevents coagulation of 10 mL standard red gold sol when 1 mL of 10% NaCl is added.

Smaller gold number → Better protective power
Protective ColloidGold NumberProtective Power
Gelatin0.005–0.01Best
Haemoglobin0.03–0.07Good
Gum arabic0.1–0.2Moderate
Starch25Poor
Mechanism: Protective colloid forms an adsorbed layer around lyophobic particles → prevents electrolyte ions from neutralising their charge → coagulation prevented.
Applications: Gelatin in ice cream, photographic films, medicines.

Zeta Potential

Electric potential at the slipping/shear plane between colloidal particle and bulk dispersion medium.
Higher zeta potential → greater repulsion → more stable colloid.
When zeta potential = 0 (neutralised by electrolyte) → coagulation occurs.

4.4 Emulsions, Gels & Electrophoresis

Emulsions

Colloidal systems of two immiscible liquids dispersed in each other with help of an emulsifier.
TypeWhat's dispersedMediumExamples
O/W (Oil-in-Water)Oil dropsWaterMilk, cold cream, vanishing cream
W/O (Water-in-Oil)Water dropsOilButter, margarine
Emulsifier: Reduces interfacial tension between oil and water. Forms protective film around dispersed droplets to prevent them merging.
Types: Surfactants (soaps, LABS), proteins (casein in milk), carbon black emulsions.

Demulsification: Breaking an emulsion into two layers. Methods: heating, freezing, centrifugation (physical) | adding electrolytes or detergents (chemical).

Electrophoresis & Electro-osmosis

Electrophoresis: Migration of colloidal particles (dispersed phase) towards oppositely charged electrode under electric field.
Negative colloid → migrates to anode | Positive colloid → migrates to cathode

Electro-osmosis: When colloidal particles are prevented from moving, the dispersion medium (liquid) itself moves under the electric field in opposite direction.

Streaming Potential (Quincke's equation):
ζ = Pη² / (2DE)    [ζ = zeta potential, P = pressure, η = viscosity, D = dielectric constant, E = potential]

Gels

Colloidal system where liquid is dispersed in solid medium — semi-solid jelly-like form.
Xerogels: More solid, less liquid — rigid (gelatin sheets, cellophane).
Other gels: Silicic acid, ferric hydroxide, gum arabic, agar.

4.5 Surfactants

Surfactants (surface-active agents): Substances that decrease surface tension of a liquid. Concentrate at the interface. Have dual structure:
• Hydrophilic head (polar, water-loving)
• Hydrophobic tail (non-polar, water-hating — long hydrocarbon chain)
Gibbs Adsorption Equation: S = −(c/RT) × (dγ/dc)
If dγ/dc is negative → S is positive → solute concentrates at surface → positive adsorption → surfactant behaviour.

Classification of Surfactants

TypeExampleActive Part
AnionicSodium cetyl sulphate (C₁₆H₃₃SO₄Na⁺)Anion (–SO₄⁻)
CationicCetyl trimethyl ammonium bromideCation (N⁺)
Non-ionicPolyglycol etherNo charge
AmphotericAmino acids, proteinsBoth ionic forms

Micelle & CMC

Above CMC (Critical Micelle Concentration), surfactant molecules aggregate into spherical micelles:
• Hydrophilic heads face outward (towards water)
• Hydrophobic tails point inward (away from water)
Above CMC, conductance of ionic surfactant drops — micelles behave as macromolecular electrolytes.

Soaps vs Detergents

Soaps: Na/K salts of higher fatty acids. Fail in hard water (precipitate with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺).
Detergents: Sulphonate group (–SO₃⁻Na⁺). Work in hard water — Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ sulphonates are soluble.
Example: Linear alkyl benzene sulphonate (LABS).

4.7 Donnan Membrane Equilibrium

When a protein (non-diffusible macromolecular ion) is separated from NaCl solution by a semipermeable membrane:
• Protein ions (large) cannot pass through membrane
• Small ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) can pass through
• But non-diffusible protein ions prevent equal distribution of small ions

At equilibrium:
[Na⁺]_inside × [Cl⁻]_inside = [Na⁺]_outside × [Cl⁻]_outside
This creates:
• Osmotic pressure difference across membrane
• Electrical potential difference (Donnan potential)

Importance: Explains biological membrane behaviour, osmotic pressure of protein solutions, ion transport in living cells.
