Extract — From: 1.2 On Saying Please (Adapted)

There is no law that compels a man to say "please" when he asks a favour, or "thank you" when he gets it. But it is one of those social graces that make life pleasant and smooth. The man who says "please" and "thank you" does not merely observe a ritual; he is showing a spirit of goodwill. He is saying, in effect, "I recognise that you are not my servant, but my equal. I am asking, not commanding."

The bus conductor who is treated with respect will give you his best service. The shopkeeper who is greeted pleasantly will go out of his way to help you. Civility costs nothing, but buys everything.

We live together in society and it is society that makes life bearable. The roots of society are manners. Without manners, society cannot survive. When we are rude, we weaken those roots. When we are polite, we water them.

Q.2A — Read the following passage and do all activities.[10 Marks]
Unseen Passage

Every morning, Sunita woke up before sunrise. She lived alone in a small house at the edge of a village and had done so for thirty years. People in the village called her 'the silent woman' because she rarely spoke unless necessary. But her silence was not sadness — it was contentment.

She spent her mornings tending to her garden. She grew vegetables, herbs and flowers. Her garden was the most colourful thing in the village. Children from the school nearby would press their faces to the fence to look at the marigolds and sunflowers. Some days, she left small bundles of vegetables at neighbours' doors without knocking.

One summer, the rains failed. The wells dried up. The farmers lost their crops. But Sunita's garden survived because she had dug a small underground tank years ago to store rainwater. When the worst days came, she opened her garden to the village. She gave out vegetables every morning and let children sit in the shade of her mango trees.

The village headman came to thank her. Sunita simply said, "The garden does not belong to me alone. The sun, the rain, the soil — they all share it with me. I am just the one who remembered to prepare."

(A) she prayed every day(B) she used chemical fertilisers (C) she had dug an underground rainwater tank(D) the rains never failed there

Poem: Song of the Open Road — Walt Whitman (Textbook 2.1, Adapted stanza)
Afoot and light-hearted I take to the open road,
Healthy, free, the world before me,
The long brown path before me leading wherever I choose.

Henceforth I ask not good-fortune, I myself am good-fortune,
Henceforth I whimper no more, postpone no more, need nothing,
Done with indoor complaints, libraries, querulous criticisms,
Strong and content I travel the open road.

The earth, that is sufficient,
I do not want the constellations any nearer,
I know they are very well where they are,
I know they suffice for those who belong to them.
