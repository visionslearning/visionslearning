Extract A — From: 1.5 His First Flight — Liam O'Flaherty (Adapted)

The young seagull was alone on the ledge. His brothers and sister had already flown away with his mother and father. But he was afraid. Every time he tried to move his wings and step to the edge, his heart stood still with terror. The sea stretched down beneath, a great distance below, and it was such a long way across to the other side.

His parents had been watching him all morning. His father called to him sharply, scolding him. His mother had come near him with a piece of fish and had gone back without giving it to him. He was very hungry. He had not eaten since the day before.

Then, maddened by hunger, he dived suddenly. His heart stood still. Then a monstrous terror seized him. But the next moment he felt his wings spread outward. The wind rushed against his breast feathers. He was flying. He gave a loud, joyful cry and soared upward.

Extract B — From: 2.4 An Epitome of Courage — Stephen Hawking (Adapted)

Stephen Hawking was diagnosed with motor neurone disease at the age of twenty-one. Doctors gave him only two years to live. But Stephen Hawking lived for another fifty-five years — and in those years he became one of the greatest scientists the world has ever known.

He could not walk. He could not talk without a machine. Gradually, he lost control of every muscle in his body. Yet his mind remained extraordinary. He wrote books, gave lectures, and continued his research on black holes, the Big Bang, and the nature of time itself.

"However difficult life may seem, there is always something you can do and succeed at," he once said. He did not see his disability as the end. He saw it as a challenge — and he met that challenge with a smile.

(A) Read the following passage carefully and do all activities.[12 Marks]
Passage — Unseen (1)

Every child is born curious. Watch a small child in a garden — she touches everything, tastes everything, asks about everything. But something happens as children grow older. Schools teach them to sit still, memorise facts, and give correct answers. Slowly, curiosity is replaced by the fear of being wrong.

The greatest scientists, artists, and inventors of history were people who never lost their childhood curiosity. Albert Einstein once said that he had no special talent — he was just passionately curious. Thomas Edison failed over a thousand times before inventing the light bulb. When asked about his failures, he said, "I have not failed. I have just found a thousand ways that do not work."

Curiosity drives discovery. It is the engine of progress. A curious person asks "why?" and "what if?" — and those questions change the world. The best thing any school or parent can do for a child is to keep that curiosity alive. Let children ask questions. Let them explore. Let them be wrong, and then find their way to being right.

(B) Read the following passage and do the activities.[8 Marks]
Passage — Unseen (2)

World Heritage Sites are places of outstanding universal value — recognised and protected by UNESCO for the benefit of all humanity. India has 42 UNESCO World Heritage Sites, including the Taj Mahal, the Ajanta Caves, Hampi, the Sun Temple at Konark, and the hill forts of Rajasthan. These sites represent thousands of years of history, art, and culture.

Protecting heritage sites is not just a job for governments. Every visitor has a responsibility. Simple actions like not littering, not touching fragile carvings, and following the rules of the site help preserve these treasures for future generations. Many heritage sites are under threat from tourism itself — overcrowding, pollution, and illegal photography with flash damage delicate paintings and structures.

A heritage site is a window into our past. When we stand before the Ajanta cave paintings or the carvings of Khajuraho, we are connected to the hands and minds of people who lived centuries ago. Protecting these sites means protecting our identity. A nation that forgets its past has no foundation for its future.
