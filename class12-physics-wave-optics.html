7.1

🌊 Huygens' Principle & Wave Theory of Light

🔑 Key Points
  • Wave theory was proposed by Christiaan Huygens in 1678 — light travels as waves
  • Huygens' Principle (Statement): Every point on a wavefront acts as a secondary source of light and emits spherical secondary wavelets in all directions with the same speed as the original wave. The new wavefront at any later instant is the common tangent (envelope) to all these secondary wavelets.
  • Wavefront: locus of all points which are in the same phase of vibration. Types: spherical (point source), cylindrical (line source), plane (very far source)
  • Ray of light: perpendicular to the wavefront, represents direction of propagation
  • Corpuscular theory (Newton): predicted vglass > vair — WRONG; Huygens predicts vglass < vair — CORRECT
  • Speed of light in medium: v = c/n; n = refractive index
📐 Verification of Laws using Huygens' Principle
  • Law of Reflection (Huygens): incident wavefront AB hits mirror; Huygens construction shows angle of incidence = angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r)
  • Law of Refraction (Snell's Law from Huygens): incident wavefront from medium 1 hits surface; using secondary wavelets in medium 2 — sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁
  Incident wavefront        Refracted wavefront
         \  \  \                 /  /  /
          \  \  \               /  /  /
    medium 1 \ \ \             / / /
   ───────────A──────────────────────── interface
    medium 2            B
              secondary wavelets in medium 2

  sin i / sin r  =  v₁ / v₂  =  n₂ / n₁   (Snell's Law)
7.2

✨ Interference of Light

🔑 Key Points
  • Interference: superposition of two coherent light waves producing alternating bright and dark regions
  • Coherent sources: same frequency, same wavelength, constant phase difference — required for sustained interference
  • Constructive interference (bright fringe): path difference = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2 …); waves in phase; amplitude doubles, intensity becomes 4I₀
  • Destructive interference (dark fringe): path difference = (2n−1)λ/2; waves exactly out of phase; amplitude = 0, intensity = 0
  • Intensity formula: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)cosδ; for equal sources: I = 4I₀cos²(δ/2)
📌 Young's Double Slit Experiment (YDSE)
  • Setup: monochromatic light → single slit S → two slits S₁, S₂ (separation d) → screen at distance D
  • Path difference at point P (distance y from centre): Δ = yd/D
  • Bright fringe position: yn = nλD/d (n = 0, ±1, ±2 …)
  • Dark fringe position: yn = (2n−1)λD/2d
  • Fringe width: β = λD/d (distance between consecutive bright or dark fringes)
  • Fringe width is same for bright and dark fringes
  • Central fringe (n=0) is always bright
      S₁ ──────────────────────── P (y from centre)
       |          D               |
  S ───|                          | screen
       |                          |
      S₂ ─────────────────────── O (centre)

  Path diff: Δ = S₂P − S₁P = yd/D
  Bright: yd/D = nλ  →  y = nλD/d
  Fringe width: β = λD/d
📐 Effect on Fringe Width
  • β = λD/d — fringe width increases if λ increases, D increases, or d decreases
  • If d is doubled: β becomes half (fringes closer)
  • If D is doubled: β becomes double (fringes wider)
  • In a denser medium (water): λ → λ/n, so β → β/n (fringes become narrower)
  • If white light used: central fringe is white; coloured fringes on sides (violet closest to centre, red furthest)

7.3

🌀 Diffraction of Light

🔑 Key Points
  • Diffraction: bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or through a slit; occurs when slit width ≈ wavelength of light
  • Single slit diffraction: central bright maximum (widest & brightest); secondary maxima become progressively dimmer
  • Minima condition (single slit): a sinθ = nλ (n = ±1, ±2 …); a = slit width
  • Width of central maximum: 2λD/a (between first minima on both sides)
  • Angular half-width of central maximum: θ = λ/a
  • As slit width decreases, diffraction increases and central maximum becomes wider
  • Interference vs Diffraction: interference — two separate coherent sources; diffraction — single slit (secondary wavelets from same wavefront interfere)
              ──────── secondary max (dim)
     single   ──────── 1st minimum : a·sinθ = λ
     slit  →  ════════ CENTRAL MAX (brightest, widest)
      (a)     ──────── 1st minimum
              ──────── secondary max (dim)

  Width of central max = 2λD/a
  Position of minima: a sinθ = nλ
📌 Resolving Power
  • Resolving power: ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two nearby objects/points as separate
  • Rayleigh criterion: two images are just resolved when central maximum of one falls on first minimum of other
  • Limit of resolution (telescope): θ_min = 1.22λ/D (D = diameter of objective)
  • Resolving power of telescope: 1/θ_min = D/1.22λ — larger aperture → better resolution
  • Resolving power of microscope: RP = 2μ sinθ / λ (μ = refractive index of medium)
  • Use smaller λ (blue/UV light) for better resolving power
7.4

🔮 Polarisation of Light

🔑 Key Points
  • Polarisation: restricting the vibration of light to one plane; proves transverse nature of light
  • Unpolarised light: vibrations in all planes perpendicular to direction of propagation
  • Plane polarised light: vibrations confined to one plane only
  • Polariser: device that produces plane polarised light (e.g., Polaroid)
  • Analyser: device used to detect plane polarised light
  • When polariser and analyser are parallel (θ=0°): maximum transmission (I = I₀)
  • When polariser and analyser are perpendicular (θ=90°): zero transmission (I = 0) — "crossed polaroids"
📌 Malus' Law
  • Statement: When plane polarised light is incident on an analyser, the intensity of transmitted light is proportional to the square of the cosine of the angle (θ) between the plane of polarisation and the transmission axis of the analyser.
  • Formula: I = I₀ cos²θ
  • Discovered by Etienne Louis Malus in 1809
  • At θ=0°: I = I₀ (max); at θ=90°: I = 0 (min); at θ=45°: I = I₀/2
📌 Brewster's Law
  • Brewster's angle (i_B): specific angle of incidence at which reflected ray is completely plane polarised (vibrations parallel to surface only)
  • At Brewster's angle, reflected ray ⊥ refracted ray: i_B + r = 90°, so r = 90° − i_B
  • Derivation of Brewster's Law: n = sin i_B / sin r = sin i_B / sin(90°−i_B) = sin i_B / cos i_B
  • Formula: tan i_B = n (n = refractive index of second medium)
  • Refracted ray is partially polarised; reflected ray is fully polarised
  • Example: for glass (n=1.5), i_B = tan⁻¹(1.5) ≈ 56.3°
📌 Methods to Produce Polarised Light
  • Reflection: at Brewster's angle — reflected ray is plane polarised
  • Refraction: stack of glass plates at Brewster's angle — transmitted light becomes polarised after multiple refractions
  • Double refraction (Birefringence): in crystals like calcite, Iceland spar — splits into ordinary (o-ray) and extraordinary (e-ray)
  • Scattering: light scattered by small particles (Rayleigh scattering) is partially polarised — explains blue sky

🌊 Huygens' Principle
Q1
State Huygens' principle. Using it, derive the law of refraction (Snell's Law).
🔴 Every Year 4 Marks 2024, 2023, 2022, 2021, 2020
✅ Model Answer

Huygens' Principle:

(i) Every point on a given wavefront acts as a secondary source and emits spherical secondary wavelets in all directions with the same speed as the original wave.

(ii) The new wavefront at any later instant is the common tangent (envelope) to all these secondary wavelets.

Derivation of Snell's Law:

Let AB be the incident plane wavefront in medium 1 (speed v₁, refractive index n₁). The wavefront makes angle ∠i with the interface.

At t=0, wavefront touches interface at point A. By the time the wavefront reaches B (distance BC from A along interface), the secondary wavelet from A has travelled to distance AE = v₂τ in medium 2.

BC = v₁τ (remaining part of incident wavefront travels in medium 1) AE = v₂τ (secondary wavelet from A travels in medium 2)

From triangle ABC: sin i = BC/AC = v₁τ/AC

From triangle AEC: sin r = AE/AC = v₂τ/AC

sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂

Since v₁ = c/n₁ and v₂ = c/n₂:

sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ → n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r (Snell's Law) ✓

Conclusion: Since n₂ > n₁ for glass, v₂ < v₁ — light slows down in denser medium. This is consistent with experiment (unlike Newton's corpuscular theory).

Q2
State Huygens' principle. Using it, verify the law of reflection of light.
🔴 High Freq 3–4 Marks 2023, 2021
✅ Model Answer

Let AB be the incident plane wavefront hitting mirror surface MM'. The wavefront makes angle ∠i with the mirror surface.

At t=0, point A touches the mirror; at time t=τ, point B reaches the mirror.

BC = vτ — distance B travels to reach mirror AE = vτ — secondary wavelet from A reflects (same medium, same speed)

Since AE = BC = vτ, the reflected wavefront CE makes angle ∠r with the mirror.

From triangles ABC and AEC (right-angled, AE=BC, AC common):

∠i = ∠r (angle of incidence = angle of reflection) ✓

Both incident and reflected rays lie in the same plane as the normal — law of reflection verified.

Q3
In Young's Double Slit Experiment, derive the formula for fringe width. What is the effect on fringe width if: (i) wavelength is increased, (ii) distance between slits is doubled?
🔴 Every Year 4 Marks 2024, 2023, 2022, 2021, 2020
✅ Model Answer

Setup: Two slits S₁ and S₂ separated by distance d; screen at distance D; P is at height y from centre O.

Path difference:

S₂P² − S₁P² = (D² + (y + d/2)²) − (D² + (y − d/2)²) = 2yd (S₂P − S₁P)(S₂P + S₁P) = 2yd Since S₁P ≈ S₂P ≈ D: Δ = S₂P − S₁P = yd/D

Bright fringe (constructive): Δ = nλ → yn = nλD/d

Fringe width β = yn+1 − yn:

β = (n+1)λD/d − nλD/d = λD/d

Effects:

(i) If λ increases → β = λD/d increases — fringes become wider ✓

(ii) If d is doubled → β = λD/2d = β/2 — fringe width becomes half, fringes become narrower ✓

Q4
State the conditions for constructive and destructive interference. What is the ratio of maximum to minimum intensity if the two slits have intensity ratio 4:1?
🔴 High Freq 3 Marks 2024, 2022, 2020
✅ Model Answer

Constructive interference (bright fringe): Path difference Δ = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2 …); phase difference δ = 2nπ. Resultant amplitude = A₁ + A₂; Intensity is maximum.

Destructive interference (dark fringe): Path difference Δ = (2n−1)λ/2; phase difference δ = (2n−1)π. Resultant amplitude = |A₁ − A₂|; Intensity is minimum.

Intensity ratio calculation:

I₁/I₂ = 4/1 → A₁/A₂ = 2/1 I_max = (A₁ + A₂)² = (2+1)² = 9 I_min = (A₁ − A₂)² = (2−1)² = 1
I_max : I_min = 9 : 1

🔮 Polarisation
Q6
State Malus' Law. A plane polarised light of intensity I₀ is passed through an analyser. For what angle of the analyser will the intensity of transmitted light be (i) maximum (ii) zero (iii) half of I₀?
🔴 High Freq 3 Marks 2024, 2023, 2021, 2020
✅ Model Answer

Malus' Law: When a beam of plane polarised light of intensity I₀ is incident on an analyser, the intensity of the transmitted light is:

I = I₀ cos²θ

where θ is the angle between the plane of polarisation of the incident light and the transmission axis of the analyser.

(i) Maximum intensity: I = I₀ when cos²θ = 1 → θ = 0° (analyser parallel to polariser)

(ii) Zero intensity: I = 0 when cos²θ = 0 → θ = 90° (analyser perpendicular to polariser — "crossed polaroids")

(iii) Half of I₀: I = I₀/2 → cos²θ = 1/2 → cosθ = 1/√2 → θ = 45°

Q7
State Brewster's Law. Derive the relation tan i_B = n. What is the significance of Brewster's angle?
🔴 High Freq 3–4 Marks 2024, 2022, 2021
✅ Model Answer

Brewster's Law: When unpolarised light is incident on the boundary between two media at a particular angle called Brewster's angle (i_B), the reflected ray is completely plane polarised. The vibrations in the reflected ray are perpendicular to the plane of incidence.

Derivation of tan i_B = n:

At Brewster's angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular to each other:

∠(reflected) + ∠(refracted) = 90° i_B + r = 90° → r = 90° − i_B

Applying Snell's Law at the interface:

n = sin i_B / sin r = sin i_B / sin(90° − i_B) n = sin i_B / cos i_B
∴ tan i_B = n

Significance: At i_B, reflected light is 100% plane polarised. The refracted beam is partially polarised. Using a stack of glass plates at Brewster's angle, fully polarised transmitted light can be obtained. Used in photography (Polaroid filters), LCD screens, anti-glare glasses.

Q8
What is polarisation? How does it prove the transverse nature of light? Name any two methods of producing plane polarised light.
🔴 High Freq 3 Marks 2023, 2022, 2020
✅ Model Answer

Polarisation: The phenomenon of restricting the vibration of a light wave to a single plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation is called polarisation.

Proof of transverse nature: Polarisation is only possible in transverse waves (where displacement is perpendicular to propagation). In longitudinal waves (like sound), vibrations are along the direction of propagation, so polarisation is impossible. Since light can be polarised, it must be a transverse wave.

Two methods of producing plane polarised light:

1. Reflection — When unpolarised light is reflected from a surface at Brewster's angle (i_B = tan⁻¹n), the reflected light is completely plane polarised.

2. By using Polaroids — A Polaroid is a sheet containing aligned molecules (e.g., iodoquinine sulphate crystals). When unpolarised light passes through it, only vibrations in one direction are transmitted.

Q9
Explain the phenomenon of diffraction at a single slit. What are the conditions for minima?
🔴 High Freq 3 Marks 2023, 2022, 2021
✅ Model Answer

Diffraction at a single slit: When monochromatic light passes through a narrow slit of width 'a', Huygens' secondary wavelets from different parts of the same wavefront interfere with each other, producing alternating bright and dark bands on the screen.

Pattern: Central maximum — widest and brightest band. On either side — alternating dark (minima) and dim bright (secondary maxima).

Condition for minima: The slit of width 'a' is divided into pairs of strips. Each strip in one half has a corresponding strip in the other half that contributes a path difference of λ/2 → destructive interference.

a sin θ = nλ (n = ±1, ±2, ±3 …) — condition for minima

Width of central maximum = 2λD/a (between 1st minima on both sides)

As slit width 'a' decreases → central maximum becomes wider → more diffraction

N2. Two slits are 1 mm apart and screen is placed 1 m away. Find the fringe width if wavelength = 589 nm. How many fringes will be formed in 1 cm width? [2023 Board]
Given: d = 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³ m
D = 1 m
λ = 589 nm = 589 × 10⁻⁹ m
Width = 1 cm = 10⁻² m
β = λD/d = (589 × 10⁻⁹ × 1) / (1 × 10⁻³)

No. of fringes = total width / β

∴ β = 0.589 mm; Number of fringes ≈ 17 ✓
N3. The 10th bright fringe in a YDSE is at a distance of 2 cm from the central fringe. The slits are 1 mm apart and screen is 80 cm away. Find the wavelength of light. [2022 Board]

d = 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³ m
D = 80 cm = 0.80 m
y_n = nλD/d
λ = y_n × d / (n × D)
λ = (2 × 10⁻² × 1 × 10⁻³) / (10 × 0.80)

∴ λ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m = 2500 nm ✓
N4. In YDSE, slits are 0.2 mm apart, screen is 2 m away, wavelength = 600 nm. Find: (i) fringe width, (ii) position of 3rd bright fringe, (iii) position of 2nd dark fringe. [2021, 2020 type]

D = 2 m
λ = 600 nm = 6 × 10⁻⁷ m

N8. In YDSE, when light of wavelength 4500 Å is used, 20 fringes are observed in a certain region of screen. If wavelength is changed to 6000 Å, how many fringes will be observed in the same region? [2020 Board type]

λ₂ = 6000 Å, find n₂
Width of region = same = n₁β₁ = n₂β₂
Width = n₁ × λ₁D/d = n₂ × λ₂D/d
