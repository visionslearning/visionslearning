Class 11 Biology · Chapter 2

🔬 Systematics of Living Organisms

Notes · Key Points · Important Q&A · MCQs — Maharashtra Board

⭐ Key Points at a Glance

  • Systematics — study of kinds and diversity of organisms and their evolutionary relationships (G. Simpson, 1961).
  • Taxonomy — classification following rules; term coined by A.P. de Candolle (Swiss Botanist, 1778–1841).
  • Classification types: Artificial (Linnaeus), Natural (Bentham & Hooker), Phylogenetic (Engler & Prantl).
  • Three Domains proposed by Carl Woese (1990) — Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya.
  • Five Kingdoms proposed by R.H. Whittaker (1969) — Monera, Protista, Plantae, Fungi, Animalia.
  • Taxonomic hierarchy (highest → lowest): Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  • Binomial nomenclature introduced by Carl Linnaeus in "Species Plantarum" (1753).
  • DNA barcoding — identification of species based on DNA sequence from tiny tissue sample.
  • Viroids — discovered by T.O. Diener (1971); single-stranded RNA, no protein coat.
  • Numerical taxonomy — first proposed by Sokel and Sneath (1963).
2.1

Systematics & Taxonomy

Systematics"Systematics is the study of kinds and diversity of organisms and their comparative and evolutionary relationship." — G. Simpson, 1961
TaxonomyClassification following certain rules or principles. From Greek: taxis = arrangement + nomos = law/rule. Term first introduced by A.P. de Candolle (Swiss Botanist, 1778–1841).

Types of Classification:

TypeBasisExample
ArtificialFew easily observable non-evolutionary features (habit, colour) — irrespective of affinityLinnaeus system
NaturalObjectively significant features — considers relationshipsBentham & Hooker
PhylogeneticBased on common evolutionary descentEngler & Prantl
2.3

Three Domains of Life

Proposed by Carl Woese in 1990. Domain is a unit larger than Kingdom.

DomainCell TypeKey Feature
ArchaeaProkaryoticSurvive extreme conditions (extremophiles); cell wall without peptidoglycan; unique rRNA
BacteriaProkaryoticCell wall with peptidoglycan; differ from Archaea in cell wall structure
EukaryaEukaryoticIncludes Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia
All three domains haveVery unique ribosomal RNA (rRNA). Cladogram of three domains of life shows phylogenetic descent from a common ancestor.
2.4–2.8

Chemotaxonomy · Numerical Taxonomy · Cladogram · Phylogeny · DNA Barcoding

2.4 ChemotaxonomyClassification based on chemical constituents of organisms. E.g., Archaea — cell wall without peptidoglycan; Prokarya — with peptidoglycan; Fungi — chitinous cell wall; Plants — cellulosic cell wall.
2.5 Numerical TaxonomyClassification based on quantification of characters using algorithms like cluster analysis. Proposed by Sokel and Sneath (1963). Avoids subjective evaluation.
2.6 CladogramA typical branching pattern diagram showing hypothetical evolutionary relationship. Three domains of life diagram is a cladogram.
2.7 PhylogenyEvolutionary relationship of organisms. Considers morphological status AND relationship of groups with other life. Woese's three domains and Whittaker's five kingdoms are examples of phylogenetic relationship.
2.8 DNA BarcodingNew method for identification of species based on its DNA sequence from a tiny tissue sample. Steps: (a) Collecting DNA barcode data of known species (b) Matching barcode sequence of unknown sample against barcode library.
Applications: Protection of endangered species, preservation of natural resources, pest control, identifying disease vectors, authentication of health products, identification of medicinal plants.

Units of Classification — Key Points:

SpeciesBasic unit of classification. Group of organisms that can interbreed under natural conditions to produce fertile offspring. Ranked below genus. Denoted by Latin binomial.
GenusGroup of species bearing close resemblance in morphological characters but they do NOT interbreed. E.g., Tiger, Leopard, Lion — all genus Panthera. Brinjal, Potato — genus Solanum.
FamilyGroup of closely related genera. E.g., Hibiscus, Gossypium, Sida, Bombax — family Malvaceae. Cat, Leopard, Tiger, Lion — family Felidae. Dog — family Canidae.
Order (Cohort)Group of closely related families. E.g., Papavaraceae, Brassicaceae, Capparidaceae → Order Parietales. Dogs (Canidae) and Cats (Felidae) → Order Carnivora.
ClassAssemblage of closely allied orders. E.g., Orders Carnivora + Primates → Class Mammalia.
Division/PhylumCategory of related classes. E.g., Angiospermae includes classes Dicotyledonae + Monocotyledonae.
KingdomHighest taxonomic category. E.g., Sub-kingdoms Phanerogams + Cryptogams → Kingdom Plantae.
2.12

Nomenclature & Binomial System

Binomial nomenclature introduced by Carl Linnaeus in book "Species Plantarum" (1753).

Old code: ICBN (International Code of Botanical Nomenclature). Changed to ICNAFP (International Code of Nomenclature for Algae, Fungi and Plants) — "Shenzhen Code" at XIX IBC, Shenzhen, China, July 2017. Published 26 June 2018.

Rules of Binomial Nomenclature:

  • Name composed of two Latin/Greek words
  • Generic epithet (genus name) — simple noun; written FIRST; begins with CAPITAL letter
  • Specific epithet (species name) — descriptive adjective; written SECOND; begins with small letter
  • Scientific name must be underlined separately (handwritten) or printed in italics
  • Each epithet: minimum 3 letters, maximum 13 letters
  • Author's name written in full or abbreviated after scientific name. E.g., Mangifera indica L. (L = Linnaeus)
ExampleSunflower = Helianthus annuus L. | China Rose = Hibiscus rosa-sinensis | Mango = Mangifera indica L.
2.13

Salient Features of Five Kingdoms (R.H. Whittaker, 1969)

Carl Linnaeus classified organisms into 2 Kingdoms (Plantae and Animalia) — found inadequate for organisms like Bacteria, Fungi, Euglena. So R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed Five Kingdom classification showing phylogenetic relationship.

1. Kingdom Monera

  • Unicellular prokaryotic organisms — omnipresent
  • No well-defined nucleus; DNA = simple double-stranded circular chromosome called nucleoid
  • Extrachromosomal DNA = plasmids
  • Cell wall: peptidoglycan (murein)
  • No membrane-bound organelles; Ribosomes = 70S
  • Reproduction: asexual (binary fission, budding); rarely sexual by conjugation
  • Shapes: Coccus (spherical), Bacillus (rod), Vibrio (comma), Spirillum (spiral)
a. ArchaebacteriaExtremophiles — found in volcanic craters, salty lakes, hot springs. Halophiles (high salt), Thermophiles (high temp). E.g., methanogenic bacteria in gut of ruminants (cows, buffaloes) → produce methane in biogas plants. Cell wall WITHOUT peptidoglycan.
b. Eubacteria (True bacteria)Cell wall WITH peptidoglycan. Autotrophs (photosynthetic — Chlorobium, Chromatium; chemosynthetic — sulphur bacteria) and heterotrophs. Useful: Lactobacilli (curd), Azotobacter (N₂ fixation), Streptomyces (antibiotics). Harmful: typhoid, cholera, TB, tetanus.
Cyanobacteria (Blue-green algae)Multicellular filamentous; fresh water; covered by mucilagenous sheath. Photosynthetic pigments: Chl-a, Chl-b, carotenes, xanthophylls. Filaments show heterocyst — helps in nitrogen fixation. E.g., Nostoc.
MycoplasmaSmallest living forms. No cell wall. Many forms pathogenic. Resistant to common antibiotics due to absence of cell wall.

2. Kingdom Protista

  • Unicellular eukaryotic organisms
  • Show link with Plantae, Fungi and Animalia
a. Plant-like Protists (Chrysophytes / Phytoplanktons)Microscopic, mostly photosynthetic — major producers in oceans. Body wall = two soap-box like silica covers → called diatoms. Diatomaceous earth (accumulated shells) used in polishing and filtration.
DinoflagellatesAquatic (mostly marine), photosynthetic. Cell wall = cellulosic stiff plates. Pair of flagella. Wide range of pigments (yellow, green, brown, blue, red). Gonyaulax causes famous "red tide" — makes sea appear red.
EuglenoidsLack cell wall; have tough proteinaceous pellicle. Two flagella (one short, one long). Heterotrophs in dark; photosynthetic in light (similar pigments to higher plants).
b. Animal-like Protists (Protozoans)Lack cell wall; heterotrophs; primitive animal forms.
  • Amoeboid protozoans — pseudopodia. Amoeba (free living); Entamoeba (endoparasite → amoebic dysentery)
  • Flagellated protozoans — flagella. Trypanosoma → sleeping sickness
  • Ciliated protozoans — cilia. Paramoecium — has gullet (cytostome)
  • Sporozoans — spore forming. Plasmodium → malaria
c. Fungi-like Protists (Myxomycetes)Saprophytic; found on decaying leaves. Cells aggregate → large cell mass called plasmodium (NOT malaria parasite). Spores very tough — survive harsh conditions.

3. Kingdom Plantae

  • Dominated by autotrophs (photosynthetic)
  • Some semi-autotrophic insectivorous: Venus fly trap, pitcher plant, bladderwort
  • Some heterotrophic parasitic: Cuscuta
  • Multicellular, eukaryotic; chlorophyll-containing cells
  • Cell wall: cellulose
  • Show alternation of generations
  • Divided into: Cryptogamae (Cryptogams) and Phanerogamae (Phanerogams)

4. Kingdom Fungi

  • Eukaryotic heterotrophs — extracellular digestion
  • Found in warm, humid places
  • Body: unicellular or filamentous (hyphae); mass of hyphae = mycelium
  • Hyphae: septate or non-septate; uni or multinucleate
  • Non-septate multinucleate hyphae = coenocytic hyphae
  • Cell wall: chitin (fungal cellulose)
  • Mostly saprophytic; some parasitic, some symbiotic (lichens with algae; mycorrhiza with roots)

PhycomycetesAlgal fungiAseptate coenocytic hyphae; moist/damp habitatsMucor, Rhizopus (bread mould), Albugo

LichensSymbiosis of algae (phycobiont — cyanobacteria or green algae) + fungi (mycobiont). Algae provides food; fungi provides shelter, water and minerals. Excellent example of symbiosis. Found in extreme environments. Pollution indicators — not found in polluted areas. Help in soil formation. E.g., Parmelia.

5. Kingdom Animalia

  • Heterotrophs — holozoic nutrition
  • Most have locomotion
  • Multicellular eukaryotes; cells lack chlorophyll and cell wall
  • Growth is determinate (follow definite pattern)

2.14 Acellular Organisms (not in Whittaker's 5 Kingdoms)

VirusesNamed by Louis Pasteur. Name 'virus' given by M.J. Beijernek. Stanley showed viruses are inert outside host and can be crystallised. Have protein coat (capsid) made of capsomeres around nucleic acid. Genetic material: either DNA or RNA (never both). Bacteriophages (infect bacteria) — usually double-stranded DNA. Cause diseases: TMV in plants; smallpox, mumps, AIDS in humans.
ViroidsDiscovered by T.O. Diener (1971). Single-stranded RNA — no protein coat. Low molecular weight; smaller than viruses. Cause Potato Spindle Tuber disease.
PrionsAbnormally folded proteins causing infectious neurological diseases. Word prion = "proteinaceous infectious particle". E.g., Mad cow disease in cattle; Jacob's disease in humans.

❓ Important Questions & Answers

Q1
Define Systematics. Who defined it and when? ★★★
Show ▾
"Systematics is the study of kinds and diversity of organisms and their comparative and evolutionary relationship" — G. Simpson, 1961.
The methods of classification date back to ancient times when Indian, Greek and Roman philosophers contributed to systematise science. This field of biology includes taxonomy and the study of evolutionary relationships.
Q2
What is Taxonomy? What is the origin of the word? Who coined it? ★★★
Show ▾
Taxonomy means classification following certain rules or principles.
The word comes from two Greek words:
  • taxis = arrangement
  • nomos = law or rule
The term taxonomy was first introduced by A.P. de Candolle (Swiss Botanist, 1778–1841).
Q3
Distinguish between Artificial, Natural and Phylogenetic classification. ★★
Show ▾
  • Artificial classification: Based on few easily observable and non-evolutionary features like habit, colour — irrespective of their affinity with other organisms. E.g., Linnaeus system of classification.
  • Natural classification: Based on objectively significant characters rather than selected for convenience. Considers actual relationships. E.g., Bentham and Hooker's system.
  • Phylogenetic classification: Based on common evolutionary descent — reflects evolutionary history. E.g., Engler and Prantl classification.
Q4
What are the Three Domains of life? Who proposed them? Compare them. ★★★
Show ▾
Proposed by Carl Woese in 1990. Domain is a unit larger than Kingdom.
Three domains are Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya.
  • Archaea and Bacteria — both have prokaryotic cells
  • Eukarya — has eukaryotic cells
  • All three have very unique ribosomal RNA (rRNA)
  • Archaea survive in extreme environments (extremophiles) — volcanic craters, salty lakes, hot springs
  • Bacteria differ from Archaea in structure of cell wall — Bacteria have peptidoglycan; Archaea do NOT
The three domains are shown in a cladogram (phylogenetic descent diagram) branching from a common ancestor.
Q5
What is DNA barcoding? Explain its steps and applications. ★★★
Show ▾
DNA barcoding is a new method for the identification of any species based on its DNA sequence from a tiny tissue sample.
Steps:
  • (a) Collecting DNA barcode data of known species
  • (b) Matching the barcode sequence of the unknown sample against the barcode library for identification
Applications:
  • Protection of endangered species
  • Preservation of natural resources
  • Pest control in agriculture
  • Identifying disease vectors
  • Authentication of natural health products
  • Identification of medicinal plants
It also helps study newly identified species and understanding ecological and evolutionary relationships.
Q6
Write the taxonomic hierarchy from Kingdom to Species. Give one example. ★★★
Show ▾
Taxonomic hierarchy (highest → lowest):
Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

These are compulsory categories. There are also facultative categories like sub-order, sub-family, sub-class.

Example — China Rose: Kingdom Plantae → Division Angiospermae → Class Dicotyledonae → Sub-class Polypetalae → Order Malvales → Family Malvaceae → Genus Hibiscus → Species rosa-sinensis

As we go higher in taxonomic ladder, number of common characters decreases.
Q7
State the rules of Binomial Nomenclature. Who introduced this system? ★★★
Show ▾
Introduced by Carl Linnaeus in book "Species Plantarum" (1753).
Rules:
  • Name composed of two Latin/Greek words
  • Generic epithet (genus) — simple noun; written FIRST; begins with CAPITAL letter
  • Specific epithet (species) — descriptive adjective; written SECOND; begins with small letter
  • Scientific name — underlined separately (handwritten) or printed in italics
  • Each epithet: min 3 letters, max 13 letters
  • Author's name written after in full or abbreviated. E.g., Mangifera indica L. (L = Linnaeus)
Example: Sunflower = Helianthus annuus L.
Q8
Why did Whittaker propose Five Kingdom classification? Name the five kingdoms. ★★★
Show ▾
Carl Linnaeus' two kingdom classification (Plantae and Animalia) was found inadequate because it could not classify organisms that show characters of BOTH kingdoms, e.g., Bacteria, Fungi, Euglena.
Hence R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed Five Kingdom classification showing phylogenetic relationships.
Five Kingdoms:
  • 1. Kingdom Monera
  • 2. Kingdom Protista
  • 3. Kingdom Plantae
  • 4. Kingdom Fungi
  • 5. Kingdom Animalia
Q9
What are the salient features of Kingdom Monera? ★★★
Show ▾
Kingdom Monera features:
  • Unicellular prokaryotic organisms — omnipresent
  • No well-defined nucleus; genetic material = nucleoid (circular double-stranded DNA)
  • Extra chromosomal DNA = plasmids
  • Cell wall: peptidoglycan (murein)
  • No membrane-bound organelles (no mitochondria, chloroplast, ER)
  • Ribosomes: 70S (smaller than eukaryotic cells)
  • Reproduction: asexual (binary fission, budding); rarely sexual by conjugation
  • Shapes: Coccus, Bacillus, Vibrio, Spirillum
  • Classified as: Archaebacteria and Eubacteria
Q10
Write a note on useful and harmful bacteria. ★★
Show ▾
Useful bacteria:
  • Lactobacilli — curdling of milk (anaerobic)
  • Azotobacter — nitrogen fixation in soil
  • Streptomyces — antibiotic production
  • Bacteria used in composting and degrading oil
  • Methanogenic bacteria in ruminant gut → biogas production
Harmful bacteria:
  • Cause diseases: Typhoid (Salmonella typhi), Cholera (Vibrio cholerae), Tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis), Tetanus (Clostridium tetani)
Q11
What are the economic importance of fungi? ★★
Show ▾
Useful fungi:
  • Agaricus (mushroom) — consumed as food
  • Saccharomyces (yeast) — used in bakery and breweries (fermentation)
  • Penicillium — source of antibiotic Penicillin
  • Morels and truffles — consumed as delicacies
  • Neurospora — useful in genetic and biochemical assays
  • Fungi involved in decomposition — recycle nutrients
Harmful fungi:
  • Puccinia — causes rust disease in wheat (harmful to plants)
  • Various fungi cause diseases in animals and humans

Q13
What is Lichen? Explain its significance. ★★
Show ▾
Lichen is a co-existence (symbiosis) of algae and fungi for mutual benefit.
  • Algal member (phycobiont) — mostly cyanobacteria or green algae; provides food by photosynthesis
  • Fungal member (mycobiont) — provides shelter, absorbs water and minerals for the alga
Significance:
  • Excellent example of symbiosis
  • Found in extreme environments like snow-clad poles
  • Pollution indicators — NOT found in polluted regions
  • Important role in soil formation through specific acid production
E.g., Parmelia
Q14
Why is binomial nomenclature important? ★★
Show ▾
Common names (vernacular names) cause confusion — widely distributed organisms have many different local names in different languages and dialects. E.g., Mango has over 50 Sanskrit names in Ayurveda; Pansy has about 50 common English names.
Binomial nomenclature is important because:
  • Provides a unique, universal name for each organism
  • Names are internationally accepted — used worldwide
  • Names are systematic and in Latin/Greek — stable language
  • Avoids confusion caused by local/regional common names
  • Reflects genus-species relationship and sometimes evolutionary information
Q15
What is the difference between species and genus? ★★
Show ▾
  • Species: Basic unit of classification. Group of organisms that can interbreed under natural conditions to produce fertile offspring. E.g., Lion (Panthera leo), Tiger (Panthera tigris) are different species.
  • Genus: A group of species bearing close morphological resemblance but they do NOT interbreed. E.g., Tiger, Leopard, Lion all belong to same genus Panthera — same genus, different species.
