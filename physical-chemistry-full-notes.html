1.1 Dipole Moment

What is Dipole Moment?
When two atoms bonded together have different electronegativities, electrons are pulled more towards one atom — creating a partial negative (δ−) and partial positive (δ+) end. This separation of charge is called a dipole. The dipole moment (μ) measures how strong this polarity is.
μ = q × d    Unit: Debye (D)    1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m
q = magnitude of charge | d = distance between charges
A molecule has net μ only if bond dipoles do not cancel each other vectorially.

Key Bond Moments

Disubstituted Benzene (Ortho / Meta / Para)

When two identical groups are attached to benzene ring at different positions, the angle between their bond moments changes. Using vector addition:
μ_net = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cosθ) = 2μ cos(θ/2) [when μ₁=μ₂]

💡 This is a common exam numerical — para always gives μ = 0 for identical substituents

1.2 Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy

IR spectroscopy studies how molecules absorb infrared radiation. When IR light hits a molecule, bonds start vibrating. The frequency at which they vibrate depends on bond strength (k) and masses of atoms (μ).
ν̃ = (1/2πc) √(k/μ)    where μ (reduced mass) = m₁m₂ / (m₁+m₂)
k = force constant (bond strength) | c = speed of light
Stronger bond → higher k → higher frequency
Heavier atoms → larger μ → lower frequency

Types of Vibrations

A. Stretching — bond length changes (along bond axis):
  • Symmetric: Both atoms move away from centre simultaneously
  • Asymmetric: One atom moves in, the other moves out

B. Bending — bond angle changes:
  • In-plane: Scissoring (atoms move towards each other) | Rocking (group swings in plane)
  • Out-of-plane: Wagging (both move in/out same direction) | Twisting (opposite directions)

Degrees of freedom: Non-linear molecule = 3N–6 | Linear molecule = 3N–5
💡 Stretching requires MORE energy than bending

Characteristic Group Frequencies

BondFrequency (cm⁻¹)Notes

Fingerprint Region (900–1400 cm⁻¹)

This spectral region is called the fingerprint region because it is unique for every molecule — just like a human fingerprint. It contains many overlapping bands from complex vibrations of the whole molecule. Even tiny structural changes produce different patterns. Used for:
  • Identifying unknown compounds
  • Comparing two compounds (if same fingerprint = same compound)
  • Detecting structural differences

Isotopic Effect

When a light atom is replaced by its heavier isotope (e.g., H → D), the reduced mass (μ) increases. Since ν̃ ∝ 1/√μ, the frequency decreases.
ν̃(H–X) / ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX / μ_HX) ≈ √2 ≈ 1.414
Example: O–H absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹ → O–D absorbs at ~2630 cm⁻¹
This isotopic shift is used to confirm the presence of O–H or N–H groups in a molecule.

1.3 Raman Spectroscopy

Discovery: C.V. Raman (1928) — Nobel Prize 1930

When monochromatic light is scattered by a molecule, most scattered light has the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering). A tiny fraction (~1%) has different frequency — this is the Raman Effect. The change in frequency corresponds to vibrational energy levels of the molecule.

Stokes & Anti-Stokes Lines

TypeFrequencyIntensityExplanation
Rayleighν₀ (same)Highest (~99%)Elastic scattering, no energy change
Stokesν₀ − Δν (lower)StrongMolecule gains energy; photon loses energy
Anti-Stokesν₀ + Δν (higher)WeakMolecule loses energy; photon gains energy
💡 Stokes lines are more intense because most molecules are in the ground vibrational state at room temperature → easier to absorb energy
Raman Shift: Δν = ν₀ − ν_Stokes = ν_Anti-Stokes − ν₀

Raman Activity Condition

A vibration is Raman active if there is a change in polarisability of the molecule during vibration.
A vibration is IR active if there is a change in dipole moment during vibration.

Mutual Exclusion Rule: In centrosymmetric molecules (e.g., CO₂), IR active vibrations are Raman inactive and vice versa.
💡 IR + Raman together give complete molecular structure information. Raman is useful for non-polar molecules like N₂, O₂ that have no IR spectrum.

1.4 Molecular Polarisation & Debye Equation

When a molecule is placed in an electric field, its charge distribution gets distorted — this is called polarisation. Total molar polarisation (PT) has three parts:
PT = Pe + Pa + Po

Types of Polarisation

TypeSymbolCauseMoleculesTemp Dependence
ElectronicPeDisplacement of electron cloud relative to nucleusAll moleculesIndependent
Atomic/IonicPaDisplacement of atoms/ions relative to each otherAll moleculesIndependent
OrientationPoPermanent dipoles align along electric fieldPolar onlyDecreases as T↑
💡 Non-polar: PT = Pe + Pa | Polar: PT = Pe + Pa + Po

Clausius-Mosotti Equation (non-polar molecules)

PM = [(εr−1)/(εr+2)] × (M/ρ) = (4/3)πNAα
εr = dielectric constant | M = mol. weight | ρ = density | NA = Avogadro number | α = polarisability
Valid only for non-polar molecules (no orientation polarisation).

Debye Equation (polar molecules)

Debye extended Clausius-Mosotti to include orientation polarisation for polar molecules:
PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA / 3kT
This is of the form PT = A + B/T where:
A = (4/3)πNAα — temperature-independent (induced polarisation)
B = μ²NA/3k — gives dipole moment from slope
How to determine μ experimentally (Debye method):
  1. Measure total molar polarisation PT at several different temperatures
  2. Plot PT on Y-axis vs 1/T on X-axis → straight line
  3. Slope = μ²NA/3k → rearrange to find μ
  4. Intercept = (4/3)πNAα → gives polarisability α
μ = √(3k × Slope / NA)

⭐ Chapter 1 – All Important Q&A with Full Explanation

Q1. What is dipole moment? How is it used to determine the structure of a molecule? (5M)
Dipole Moment (μ): It is the product of the magnitude of charge (q) and the distance (d) between the charges.
Formula: μ = q × d   Unit: Debye (1 D = 3.336×10⁻³⁰ C·m)

Use in determining structure:
  • If μ = 0 → molecule is symmetrical (e.g., CO₂ is linear, BF₃ is planar triangular)
  • If μ ≠ 0 → molecule is unsymmetrical (e.g., SO₂ is angular, NH₃ is pyramidal)
  • Used to distinguish cis/trans isomers: cis has higher μ than trans (in cis, dipoles add; in trans, they cancel)
  • Used to identify ortho, meta, para isomers: ortho (2.6D) > meta (1.5D) > para (0D)
Q2. Why does CO₂ have μ = 0 but SO₂ has μ = 1.63 D? (4M)
CO₂ (μ = 0): Carbon dioxide has a linear structure (O=C=O). The two C=O bond moments are equal in magnitude but point in exactly opposite directions (180°). By vector addition, they cancel completely → net μ = 0.

SO₂ (μ = 1.63 D): Sulphur dioxide has an angular (bent) structure due to the lone pair on the sulphur atom. The two S=O bond moments do NOT cancel because the angle between them is not 180°. The lone pair also contributes → net dipole moment = 1.63 D.
Q3. Identification of ortho, meta, para isomers using dipole moment (5M)
For disubstituted benzene with identical groups (μ₁ = μ₂ = μ):
μ_net = 2μ cos(θ/2)
  • Ortho (θ = 60°): μ = 2μ cos30° = 2×1.5×(√3/2) ≈ 2.6 D — highest dipole
  • Meta (θ = 120°): μ = 2μ cos60° = 2×1.5×0.5 = 1.5 D
  • Para (θ = 180°): μ = 2μ cos90° = 0 D — moments cancel completely
Calculated values match experimental values → dipole moment is a reliable tool for identifying isomers.
Q4. Types of molecular vibrations with diagrams (6M)
A. Stretching Vibrations — change in bond length (along bond axis):
  • Symmetric stretching: Both atoms move away from central atom simultaneously (e.g., both O atoms in H₂O move away from O)
  • Asymmetric stretching: One atom moves towards central atom while other moves away
B. Bending Vibrations — change in bond angle:
  • In-plane Scissoring: Two H atoms in H₂O move towards each other like scissors
  • In-plane Rocking: The whole group oscillates within the plane
  • Out-of-plane Wagging: Both atoms move in and out of plane in same direction
  • Out-of-plane Twisting: Both atoms move in and out of plane in opposite directions
Degrees of freedom: Non-linear molecule (N atoms) = 3N–6 | Linear = 3N–5
Q5. What is the fingerprint region in IR spectroscopy? What is its significance? (3M)
The spectral region 900–1400 cm⁻¹ is called the fingerprint region. It contains a large number of overlapping absorption bands arising from complex vibrations of the entire molecule.

Significance:
  • Unique for every compound — just like a human fingerprint
  • No two different compounds show the same absorption pattern in this region
  • Used for identification and comparison of unknown compounds
  • Even a small change in molecular structure causes a remarkable change in this region
Q6. Explain Raman Effect. What are Stokes and Anti-Stokes lines? (6M)
Raman Effect (C.V. Raman, 1928): When monochromatic light is incident on a substance, most scattered light has the same frequency as incident light (Rayleigh scattering). A small fraction (~1%) has a different frequency — this is called the Raman Effect.

Stokes Lines: Scattered light has lower frequency (ν_s < ν₀). The molecule absorbs energy and goes to a higher vibrational level. These lines appear on the low-frequency side. They are more intense (most molecules are in ground state).

Anti-Stokes Lines: Scattered light has higher frequency (ν_as > ν₀). Molecule was already in excited state and loses energy. These are weaker (fewer molecules in excited state).

Raman Shift: Δν = ν₀ − ν_Stokes = ν_Anti-Stokes − ν₀ (same magnitude on both sides)
Q7. Isotopic effect in IR spectroscopy with example (4M)
When a lighter atom is replaced by its heavier isotope, the reduced mass (μ) increases. Since stretching frequency is inversely proportional to √μ, the frequency decreases.
ν̃ = (1/2πc)√(k/μ)   →   ν̃(H–X)/ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX/μ_HX) ≈ √2 ≈ 1.414
Example: O–H bond absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹. When H is replaced by D (deuterium, twice as heavy):
O–D absorbs at ~2630 cm⁻¹ (lower frequency)

Application: This isotopic shift is used to confirm the presence of O–H or N–H groups in a molecule. If a peak shifts on deuteration, it confirms an O–H or N–H bond.
Q8. Condition for IR active and Raman active vibrations (3M)
(a) IR Active: A vibration is IR active if it involves a change in the electric dipole moment during the vibration. Only molecules with polar bonds that undergo asymmetric vibrations are IR active. (e.g., HCl, H₂O — YES; Symmetric N₂, Cl₂ — NO)

(b) Raman Active: A vibration is Raman active if it involves a change in polarisability of the molecule during vibration.

Mutual Exclusion Rule: For centrosymmetric molecules (e.g., CO₂), vibrations that are IR active are Raman inactive and vice versa. No vibration can be both IR and Raman active in such molecules.
Q9. Explain Debye equation. How is dipole moment determined experimentally? (5M)
Debye Equation: Debye extended the Clausius-Mosotti equation for polar molecules by including orientation polarisation:
PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA/3kT = A + B/T
where A = (4/3)πNAα (temp-independent) and B = μ²NA/3k

Experimental determination of μ:
  • Measure total molar polarisation PT at several different temperatures
  • Plot PT (y-axis) vs 1/T (x-axis) → straight line obtained
  • Slope of line = μ²NA/3k
  • Rearrange: μ = √(3k × Slope / NA)
  • Intercept = (4/3)πNAα → gives polarisability α
This is the Debye method for experimental determination of dipole moment.
Q10. Types of molecular polarisation — explain each (4M)
Total polarisation: PT = Pe + Pa + Po

(i) Electronic Polarisation (Pe): Applied electric field displaces the electron cloud relative to the nucleus. Present in ALL molecules (polar and non-polar). Temperature-independent. Occurs even at high frequencies.

(ii) Atomic/Ionic Polarisation (Pa): Electric field causes displacement of atoms or ions relative to each other within the molecule. Temperature-independent. Disappears at high frequencies.

(iii) Orientation Polarisation (Po): Polar molecules have permanent dipoles. In an electric field, these dipoles try to align along the field direction. Temperature-dependent — higher temperature means more thermal energy opposing alignment → Po decreases. Present only in polar molecules.

Summary: Non-polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa | Polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa + Po
🌡️ CHAPTER 2 – CHEMICAL THERMODYNAMICS: COLLIGATIVE PROPERTIES

What are Colligative Properties?

Colligative properties are properties of solutions that depend only on the number of solute particles dissolved, NOT on the nature or identity of the solute. For example, 1 mole of glucose and 1 mole of NaCl dissolved in 1L water will produce different colligative effects (because NaCl dissociates into 2 ions).
Four Colligative Properties: 1. Lowering of vapour pressure   2. Elevation of boiling point   3. Depression of freezing point   4. Osmotic pressure

2.1 Vapour Pressure & Raoult's Law

Vapour Pressure: Pressure exerted by vapour above a liquid in dynamic equilibrium with the liquid at a given temperature. Liquids with weak intermolecular forces have high VP (more volatile). VP increases with temperature.

Raoult's Law

When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, some solute molecules occupy surface positions → fewer solvent molecules can escape → VP decreases.
p = p° × x₁    (x₁ = mole fraction of solvent)
Relative Lowering of VP: (p° − p) / p° = x₂    (x₂ = mole fraction of solute)
Derivation: x₁ + x₂ = 1 → x₁ = 1−x₂ → p = p°(1−x₂) → p°−p = p°x₂ → (p°−p)/p° = x₂
Molecular weight: M₂ = (W₂ × M₁ × p°) / (W₁ × (p°−p))

2.2 Elevation of Boiling Point & Depression of Freezing Point

Boiling Point Elevation (ΔTb)

Boiling point = temperature at which VP = atmospheric pressure. When solute is added → VP decreases → the solution needs to be heated to a higher temperature to reach atmospheric pressure → boiling point increases.
ΔTb = Kb × m    (m = molality of solution)
Kb = (R × T_b² × M₁) / (1000 × lv)    (lv = latent heat of vaporisation/gram)

Freezing Point Depression (ΔTf)

Freezing point = temperature at which solid and liquid are in equilibrium (VP of solid = VP of liquid). Dissolved solute lowers the VP of the liquid → the temperature must be lowered further to match the VP of solid → freezing point decreases.
ΔTf = Kf × m
Kf = (R × T_f² × M₁) / (1000 × lf)    (lf = latent heat of fusion/gram)
Molecular weight: M₂ = (1000 × Kf × W₂) / (ΔTf × W₁)

2.3 Osmotic Pressure & Van't Hoff's Equation

Osmosis

Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane (SPM) from a region of lower solute concentration (dilute) to higher solute concentration (concentrated). The SPM allows solvent molecules to pass but not solute molecules.

Osmotic Pressure (π): The minimum pressure that must be applied on the solution side to just stop osmosis when separated from pure solvent by a SPM.

Van't Hoff's Laws

Van't Hoff found that osmotic pressure follows laws similar to ideal gas laws:
Boyle's Law: At constant T, π ∝ 1/V → πV = constant
Charles' Law: At constant V, π ∝ T → π/T = constant
Combining: πV = nRT
πV = nRT   →   π = CRT   →   M = WRT / πV
💡 This equation is analogous to ideal gas equation PV = nRT

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

If pressure applied on the solution side is greater than osmotic pressure, solvent molecules are forced to flow from the solution to the pure solvent side — this is Reverse Osmosis.

Key terms: Feed water = input solution | Permeate = purified water output | Reject stream = concentrated residue
Applications: Seawater desalination, household water purifiers, industrial wastewater treatment, removing heavy metals/bacteria.

Van't Hoff Factor (i)

Electrolytes (like NaCl, BaCl₂) dissociate in solution → produce more particles than expected → greater colligative effect than predicted. The Van't Hoff factor (i) accounts for this:
i = Total moles of particles in solution / Moles of solute dissolved
Modified equations: ΔTb = i·Kb·m | ΔTf = i·Kf·m | π = i·CRT

⭐ Chapter 2 – All Important Q&A with Full Explanation

Q1. State and explain Raoult's Law. Derive relative lowering of VP. (6M)
Raoult's Law: The vapour pressure of a solvent above a solution equals the product of the vapour pressure of the pure solvent and the mole fraction of the solvent.
p = p° × x₁

Derivation of Relative Lowering:
Since x₁ + x₂ = 1 → x₁ = 1 − x₂
Substituting: p = p°(1 − x₂) = p° − p°x₂
∴ p° − p = p°x₂
∴ (p° − p)/p° = x₂ = relative lowering of VP

Conclusion: Relative lowering of VP equals the mole fraction of solute. It depends only on the number of solute particles (not their nature) → hence it is a colligative property.
Q2. Define osmosis and osmotic pressure. Give Van't Hoff's equation with derivation. (6M)
Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane from a dilute solution (or pure solvent) to a concentrated solution, driven by the difference in concentration.

Osmotic Pressure (π): The minimum pressure that must be applied on the solution side to just stop osmotic flow when it is separated from pure solvent by a SPM.

Van't Hoff Equation Derivation:
Van't Hoff Boyle's law: πV = constant (at constant T)
Van't Hoff Charles' law: π/T = constant (at constant V)
Combining both: πV/T = constant = R (per mole)
For 1 mole: πV = RT
For n moles in volume V: πV = nRT
∴ π = (n/V)RT = CRT (C = concentration)
∴ M = WRT/πV (used to find molecular weight)
Q3. Depression of freezing point. Derive expression for Kf. (6M)
Depression of Freezing Point: When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, the vapour pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent. The solid-liquid equilibrium (freezing) is disturbed and the temperature must be lowered further → freezing point is depressed.
ΔTf = Kf × m

Derivation of Kf (from Clausius-Clapeyron equation):
Kf = (R × T_f² × M₁) / (1000 × lf)
where: T_f = freezing point of pure solvent (Kelvin) | lf = latent heat of fusion per gram of solvent | M₁ = mol. weight of solvent | R = gas constant

Molar depression constant: Kf is defined as the depression in freezing point produced when 1 mole of solute is dissolved in 1 kg (1000g) of solvent.
Q4. Beckmann method for molecular weight by depression of freezing point. (5M)
Apparatus: Freezing tube with Beckmann thermometer and stirrer, placed inside an outer vessel (cooling bath) containing a freezing mixture (ice + salt).

Procedure:
  • Take known weight (W₁) of pure solvent in freezing tube
  • Cool slowly with stirring → note freezing point of pure solvent (T_f°)
  • Add known weight (W₂) of solute, dissolve completely
  • Cool solution → note new freezing point (T_f)
  • Calculate: ΔTf = T_f° − T_f
M₂ = (1000 × Kf × W₂) / (ΔTf × W₁)

Rast Method: Uses camphor as solvent (Kf = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹ — very high). Because Kf is so large, even a small amount of solute gives a measurable ΔTf → more accurate.
Q5. Explain Reverse Osmosis. Applications. (4M)
Reverse Osmosis (RO): When pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied on the solution side, solvent molecules are forced to flow from the solution through the SPM to the pure solvent side. This is the reverse of normal osmosis.

Key Terms:
  • Feed water: The solution from which pure water is to be extracted
  • Permeate: Purified/desalinated water obtained on the other side
  • Reject stream: Concentrated salt/brine water that doesn't pass through
Applications:
  • Desalination of seawater to produce drinking water
  • Household water purifiers (RO purifiers)
  • Removal of heavy metals, bacteria, dissolved salts from water
  • Industrial wastewater treatment
Q6. Explain Van't Hoff factor. How does it modify colligative equations? (4M)
Van't Hoff Factor (i): When electrolytes dissolve, they dissociate into ions, producing more particles than expected. This increases the colligative effect beyond what we'd predict for a non-dissociating solute. The Van't Hoff factor (i) accounts for this:
i = Total moles of particles in solution / Moles of solute dissolved

Modified Colligative Equations:
  • ΔTb = i × Kb × m
  • ΔTf = i × Kf × m
  • (p°−p)/p° = i × x₂
  • π = i × CRT
Examples: Glucose (i=1), NaCl (i=2), BaCl₂ (i=3), K₂SO₄ (i=3)
Q7. Vapour pressure of liquid. Factors affecting it. (4M)
Vapour Pressure: The pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid above its surface when liquid and vapour are in dynamic equilibrium at a given temperature.

Factors affecting VP:
  • Nature of liquid: Liquids with weak intermolecular forces (e.g., diethyl ether) have high VP (more volatile). Liquids with strong forces (e.g., water, glycerol) have low VP.
  • Temperature: VP increases with temperature — more molecules gain enough kinetic energy to escape from the liquid surface into the vapour phase.
  • Presence of solute: Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the VP of the solvent (Raoult's Law) because solute molecules occupy surface area.
Q8. NUMERICAL – Calculate molecular weight by Rast method
Given: Kf = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹ | W₂ (solute) = 0.033 g | W₁ (camphor) = 0.450 g | ΔTf = 17.7 K

Formula: M₂ = (1000 × Kf × W₂) / (ΔTf × W₁)

Calculation:
M₂ = (1000 × 37.7 × 0.033) / (17.7 × 0.450)
M₂ = 1244.1 / 7.965
M₂ ≈ 156 g·mol⁻¹ ✓
