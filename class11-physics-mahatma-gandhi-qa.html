Mechanics — Dimensional Analysis, Laws of Motion, Work-Energy
Q.1
Define dimensional analysis. State its applications and limitations.
Definition
Dimensional analysis is the method of expressing a physical quantity in terms of fundamental quantities (M, L, T) and using this to check equations, derive relations, or convert units.
Applications
  • To check the correctness of a physical equation.
  • To derive a relation between physical quantities.
  • To convert a physical quantity from one system of units to another.
Limitations
  • Cannot determine dimensionless constants (like 2π, ½, etc.).
  • Cannot be used if the relation involves more than three unknown quantities.
  • Cannot derive relations involving trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic functions.
  • Does not tell whether a quantity is a vector or scalar.
Q.2
State Newton's second law of motion.
Statement
The rate of change of linear momentum of a body is directly proportional to the net external force applied on it, and the change takes place in the direction of the applied force.
Mathematically
F = dp/dt = ma   where p = mv is linear momentum.
Note
This is the most general law of motion and includes Newton's first law as a special case (F=0 ⟹ a=0).
∴ F = ma
SI unit of force is Newton (N) = kg·m/s²
Q.3
State and explain the work-energy theorem.
Statement
The net work done by all forces acting on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.
Mathematically
W_net = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu²
Explanation
  • Using Newton's 2nd law: F = ma
  • Work done: W = F·s = mas
  • From v² = u² + 2as → as = (v²−u²)/2
  • ∴ W = m(v²−u²)/2 = ½mv² − ½mu²
Significance
If W is positive → KE increases. If W is negative → KE decreases.
∴ W_net
= KE_final − KE_initial
Q.12
What is mechanical equilibrium? Explain its different states.
Definition
A body is said to be in mechanical equilibrium when both the net force and net torque acting on it are zero.
3 States
  • Stable Equilibrium: When displaced slightly, the body returns to its original position. Centre of gravity is low. (e.g. ball in a bowl)
  • Unstable Equilibrium: When displaced slightly, the body moves further away from original position. Centre of gravity is high. (e.g. pencil standing on tip)
  • Neutral Equilibrium: When displaced, the body stays in the new position. Centre of gravity remains at the same height. (e.g. ball on flat surface)
∴ Conditions
ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0
Q.15
State any two laws of kinetic friction.
Law 1
The force of kinetic friction is directly proportional to the normal reaction (N) between the two surfaces in contact. f_k = μ_k × N
Law 2
The force of kinetic friction is independent of the area of contact between the surfaces, as long as the normal reaction remains the same.
Note
Kinetic friction is always less than static friction: f_k < f_s
Q.17
State and explain the laws of friction.
Law 1
Law of static friction: The limiting friction is directly proportional to the normal reaction. f_s ≤ μ_s × N
Law 2
Independent of area: The force of friction is independent of the apparent area of contact between the surfaces.
Law 3
Nature of surfaces: Friction depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact (roughness, material).
Law 4
Kinetic friction: Kinetic friction is independent of the velocity of sliding, but is always less than maximum static friction.
∴ f = μN
μ = coefficient of friction
Q.22
State the principle of conservation of energy and explain its application to a freely falling body.
Principle
Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system remains constant.
Free Fall Application
  • At top (h): KE = 0, PE = mgh, Total E = mgh
  • At midpoint (h/2): KE = mgh/2, PE = mgh/2, Total E = mgh
  • At bottom (h=0): KE = mgh, PE = 0, Total E = mgh
∴ At every point:
KE + PE = mgh = constant

Gravitation — Kepler's Laws, Satellites, Escape Velocity
Q.4
State Kepler's laws of planetary motion.
Law 1 (Orbit)
Law of Ellipses: Every planet revolves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one of the two foci of the ellipse.
Law 2 (Area)
Law of Equal Areas: The line joining the planet to the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal intervals of time. (Planet moves faster when closer to Sun.)
Law 3 (Period)
Law of Periods: The square of the period of revolution of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of its mean distance from the Sun. T² ∝ r³
∴ T²/r³
= constant (same for all planets)
Q.5
What is a geostationary satellite? Explain its significance.
Definition
A geostationary satellite is a satellite that orbits the Earth in the equatorial plane at a height of about 36,000 km, with a time period of exactly 24 hours — same as Earth's rotation period. It appears stationary relative to Earth.
Conditions
  • Orbital height ≈ 36,000 km above equator
  • Time period = 24 hours
  • Orbits in West-to-East direction (same as Earth's rotation)
  • Orbital plane = equatorial plane
Significance
  • Used for TV broadcasting and telecommunications.
  • Used for weather forecasting.
  • Remains fixed over one point — no need to track antenna.
  • Covers a large area of Earth's surface continuously.
Examples
INSAT series (India), Intelsat satellites
Q.9
Derive an expression for escape velocity.
Definition
Escape velocity is the minimum velocity required for a body to escape the gravitational field of Earth (or any planet) without any further propulsion.
Derivation
  • For escape, KE at surface ≥ magnitude of PE
  • ½mv_e² = GMm/R
  • v_e² = 2GM/R
  • Since g = GM/R² → GM = gR²
  • v_e = √(2gR)
Formula
v_e = √(2gR) = √(2GM/R)
For Earth
g = 9.8 m/s², R = 6.4×10⁶ m → v_e ≈ 11.2 km/s
∴ v_e
= √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s for Earth
Q.20
Explain the variation of acceleration due to gravity with altitude.
At altitude h
g_h = GM/(R+h)² = g·R²/(R+h)²
Simplified
For h << R:   g_h ≈ g(1 − 2h/R)
Conclusion
  • As altitude increases, g decreases.
  • At very large heights (h → ∞), g → 0.
  • g reduces by a factor of (R/(R+h))².
∴ g decreases
with increase in altitude above Earth's surface.
Q.21
Explain the variation of acceleration due to gravity with depth below the Earth's surface.
At depth d
g_d = g(1 − d/R)
Explanation
  • As depth increases, effective mass contributing to gravity decreases.
  • At depth d: g_d = g(R−d)/R
  • At the centre of Earth (d=R): g = 0
∴ g decreases
with depth and becomes zero at the centre of Earth.

Elasticity — Stress, Strain, Hooke's Law
Q.6
State Hooke's law. Define Young's modulus.
Hooke's Law
Within the elastic limit, the stress developed in a body is directly proportional to the strain produced in it.
Mathematically
Stress ∝ Strain  ⟹  Stress = E × Strain   where E is the modulus of elasticity.
Young's Modulus
Young's modulus (Y) is defined as the ratio of longitudinal (tensile/compressive) stress to the longitudinal strain within the elastic limit.
Formula
Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L) = FL / AΔL
SI Unit
N/m² (Pascal)
Q.18
Define strain. Explain the different types of strain.
Definition
Strain is defined as the ratio of change in dimension to the original dimension. It is a dimensionless quantity (no units).
Type 1
Longitudinal Strain (Tensile/Compressive): Change in length per unit original length.  ε = ΔL/L
Type 2
Volumetric Strain: Change in volume per unit original volume.  ε_v = ΔV/V
Type 3
Shear Strain: The angular deformation (in radians) produced due to tangential stress. Defined as the ratio of relative displacement of layers to the distance between them.  φ = x/L = tan φ
Q.19
Define stress. Explain the different types of stress.
Definition
Stress is defined as the restoring force per unit area set up inside the body when it is deformed. Stress = F/A   SI unit: N/m² (Pa)
Type 1
Tensile / Compressive Stress (Normal Stress): Force acts perpendicular to the surface. Tensile — elongation. Compressive — compression.
Type 2
Volumetric Stress (Bulk Stress): Equal forces act from all sides. Causes change in volume. Related to Bulk modulus.
Type 3
Shear Stress (Tangential Stress): Force acts parallel to the surface. Causes change in shape without change in volume.
Q.10
Derive an expression for strain energy per unit volume of a stretched wire.
Derivation
  • Work done to stretch wire by dx: dW = F·dx
  • Total work = W = ∫F·dx from 0 to ΔL
  • Since F = (YA/L)·x: W = (YA/L) ∫x·dx = (YA/L)(ΔL²/2)
  • W = ½·(YAΔl²/L) = ½·(F·ΔL)
  • W = ½ × stress × strain × A × L
Strain energy/volume
u = W/(AL) = ½ × Stress × Strain = ½ × Y × (strain)²
∴ u
= ½ × stress × strain   (J/m³)

Waves — Types, Sound, Newton's Formula
Q.7
Distinguish between transverse waves and longitudinal waves.
Transverse Waves
  • Particles vibrate perpendicular to direction of wave propagation.
  • Form crests and troughs.
  • Can travel in solids and on liquid surfaces.
  • Example: Light waves, waves on a stretched string.
Longitudinal Waves
  • Particles vibrate parallel to direction of wave propagation.
  • Form compressions and rarefactions.
  • Can travel in solids, liquids, and gases.
  • Example: Sound waves.
Key diff:
Direction of vibration — Transverse: ⊥ to propagation; Longitudinal: ∥ to propagation.
Q.8
Explain the effect of humidity on the velocity of sound in air.
Effect
The velocity of sound increases with increase in humidity (moisture content) of air.
Reason
  • Moist air contains water vapour (H₂O, M=18 g/mol).
  • Dry air contains N₂ and O₂ (avg M≈29 g/mol).
  • Moist air is less dense than dry air at same pressure.
  • v = √(γP/ρ) — lower density → higher velocity.
∴ Sound travels faster
in humid air than in dry air.
Q.16
State Newton's formula for the velocity of sound and explain its limitation.
Newton's Formula
Newton assumed that sound propagation in air is an isothermal process. He derived: v = √(P/ρ) where P = pressure of air, ρ = density of air.
Value
At STP: v = √(1.013×10⁵/1.29) ≈ 280 m/s
Limitation
Experimental value is 332 m/s, not 280 m/s. The error is because Newton's assumption of isothermal process is wrong — sound propagation is actually an adiabatic process (no heat exchange).
Laplace Correction
Laplace corrected this: v = √(γP/ρ) where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 1.4 for air. This gives v ≈ 332 m/s ✓
∴ Newton's formula
underestimates v. Laplace correction (adiabatic) gives correct value.

Electrostatics — Coulomb's Law, Electric Field, Lines of Force
Q.11
State Coulomb's law and write its mathematical expression.
Statement
The electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force acts along the line joining the two charges.
Mathematically
F = k·q₁q₂/r² = q₁q₂ / (4πε₀r²)
Constants
k = 9×10⁹ N·m²/C²  |  ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m² (permittivity of free space)
Note
Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Force is central and conservative.
∴ F
= kq₁q₂/r²   (SI unit: Newton)
Q.13
State any two properties of electric lines of force.
Property 1
Electric field lines start from a positive charge and end at a negative charge (or go to infinity from an isolated positive charge).
Property 2
Two electric field lines can never cross or intersect each other because at any point the electric field can have only one direction.
Q.24
Explain the properties of electric lines of force.
1
They start from a positive charge and end on a negative charge.
2
They never intersect each other (electric field is unique at every point).
3
They are perpendicular to the surface of a conductor at every point.
4
The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
5
The density (number per unit area) of field lines indicates the magnitude of the electric field — closer lines mean stronger field.
6
In a uniform electric field, the lines are parallel and equally spaced.
7
Field lines do not pass through a conductor; the field inside a conductor is zero.
Q.23
Define electric field intensity. State its SI unit.
Definition
Electric field intensity (E) at a point is defined as the force experienced by a unit positive test charge placed at that point.
Formula
E = F/q₀   where F is force on test charge q₀.
SI Unit
N/C (Newton per Coulomb) or V/m (Volt per metre)
Direction
Same as force on a positive test charge (from + to −).
Note
E is a vector quantity. For a point charge Q: E = kQ/r²
∴ E = F/q₀
SI unit: N/C or V/m

Vectors — Scalar Product, Vector Product
Q.14
Define the scalar product of two vectors. State any three characteristics.
Definition
The scalar (dot) product of two vectors A and B is defined as the product of their magnitudes and the cosine of the angle between them. A·B = |A||B|cosθ
Result
The scalar product is a scalar quantity (no direction).
Characteristics
  • Commutative: A·B = B·A
  • Distributive: A·(B+C) = A·B + A·C
  • If perpendicular: A·B = 0 (since cos90° = 0)
  • If parallel: A·B = |A||B| (maximum)
  • Self product: A·A = |A|² = A²
  • Unit vectors: î·î = ĵ·ĵ = k̂·k̂ = 1;   î·ĵ = 0
∴ A·B
= ABcosθ (scalar result)
Q.25
Define the vector product of two vectors and state its characteristics.
Definition
The vector (cross) product of two vectors A and B is a vector whose magnitude is |A||B|sinθ and direction is perpendicular to the plane containing A and B, given by the right-hand rule.
Formula
A×B = |A||B|sinθ · n̂ where n̂ is unit vector ⊥ to plane of A and B.
Characteristics
  • Anti-commutative: A×B = −(B×A)
  • If parallel (θ=0°): A×B = 0 (since sin0°=0)
  • If perpendicular (θ=90°): |A×B| = AB (maximum)
  • Self product: A×A = 0
  • Unit vectors: î×ĵ = k̂; ĵ×k̂ = î; k̂×î = ĵ
  • Result: Vector (has both magnitude and direction)
Applications
Torque τ = r×F; Angular momentum L = r×p
∴ A×B
= ABsinθ n̂ (vector result, ⊥ to plane of A and B)
