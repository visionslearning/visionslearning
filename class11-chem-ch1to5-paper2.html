Ch1 i. State the Law of Definite Proportions (Proust's Law). (1M)
A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined together in the same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of the source or method of preparation. E.g., water always contains H:O = 1:8 by mass.
Ch1 ii. What is a mole fraction? How is it calculated? (1M)
Mole fraction (X) = moles of one component / total moles of all components in the mixture. Xₐ = nₐ / (nₐ + n_b) Mole fractions are dimensionless; sum of all mole fractions = 1.
Ch2 iii. Define normality. What is the n-factor for H₂SO₄ in acid-base reactions? (1M)
Normality (N) = number of gram equivalents of solute dissolved per litre of solution. N = gram equivalents / V(L) = M × n-factor For H₂SO₄ in acid-base reactions: n-factor = 2 (it donates 2 H⁺ ions).
Ch2 iv. What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative analysis? (1M)
Qualitative analysis: Identifies what substances are present in a sample (nature/type of components). E.g., detecting presence of Fe²⁺ ions.
Quantitative analysis: Determines how much of each component is present (amount/concentration). E.g., finding exact concentration of Fe²⁺ by titration.
Ch3 v. What is the principle of solvent extraction? (1M)
Solvent extraction is based on the principle of differential solubility — a substance is more soluble in one solvent than another. The desired compound preferentially dissolves in the extracting solvent (which is immiscible with the original solvent) and is separated using a separating funnel.
Ch4 vi. What are quantum numbers? Name all four quantum numbers. (1M)
Quantum numbers are sets of numbers that describe the position and energy of an electron in an atom. The four quantum numbers are:
  • (1) Principal quantum number n — shell/energy level
  • (2) Azimuthal/Angular momentum l — subshell shape
  • (3) Magnetic m — orientation of orbital
  • (4) Spin s — spin of electron (+½ or –½)
Ch4 vii. State de Broglie's hypothesis. Give its significance. (1M)
De Broglie proposed that matter (like electrons) has dual nature — both particle and wave nature. Every moving particle has an associated wavelength: λ = h/mv = h/p Significance: Explains why electrons exist in fixed orbits (standing wave condition) and led to wave mechanics/quantum mechanics.
Ch5 viii. Differentiate between sigma (σ) bond and pi (π) bond. (1M)
  • σ bond: Formed by head-on/axial overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis. Stronger, allows free rotation, always the first bond formed.
  • π bond: Formed by lateral (sideways) overlap of unhybridised p-orbitals, perpendicular to internuclear axis. Weaker, no free rotation, always formed in addition to σ bond (in double/triple bonds).

Ch1 A. State and explain the Law of Conservation of Mass and Law of Constant Proportions with examples. (3M)
Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1789):
  • In a chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
  • Matter is neither created nor destroyed.
  • Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; Mass of H₂ (4g) + O₂ (32g) = Mass of H₂O (36g) ✓
Law of Definite/Constant Proportions (Proust, 1799):
  • A pure compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed ratio by mass.
  • Example: Water always has H:O = 1:8 by mass, whether from rain, river, or laboratory.
  • CO₂ always has C:O = 12:32 = 3:8 by mass.
Ch1 B. What is stoichiometry? How many moles of O₂ are needed to burn 2 moles of C₃H₈? (C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O) (3M)
Stoichiometry: The branch of chemistry that deals with the quantitative relationships between reactants and products in a chemical reaction based on the balanced equation.
Balanced equation: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
From equation: 1 mol C₃H₈ reacts with 5 mol O₂
For 2 mol C₃H₈: moles of O₂ = 2 × 5 = 10 mol O₂
Mass of O₂ needed = 10 × 32 = 320 g
∴ 10 moles (320 g) of O₂ needed to burn 2 moles of C₃H₈
Ch2 C. Explain back titration. When is it used? Give one example. (3M)
Back Titration: A titration technique in which an excess of a reagent is added to a sample, and the unreacted (excess) reagent is then titrated with a second standard solution. Used when:
  • The analyte does not react directly with the titrant.
  • The reaction is too slow or the endpoint is difficult to detect directly.
  • The sample is insoluble in the titrant.
Example: Determining purity of CaCO₃:
  • Add excess standard HCl (say 50 mL of 0.1M) to CaCO₃.
  • CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
  • After reaction, back-titrate the excess HCl with standard NaOH.
  • HCl used = total HCl – excess HCl (found by back titration) → moles of CaCO₃ calculated.
Ch3 D. Explain fractional distillation with a diagram. Where is it used industrially? (3M)
Fractional Distillation: Used to separate a mixture of miscible liquids with close but different boiling points using a fractionating column.
  • Principle: The vapours of the mixture rise through a fractionating column and condense at different heights based on boiling points. The more volatile (lower bp) liquid rises higher and distils first.
  • Fractionating column: Packed with glass beads/plates — provides repeated vaporisation-condensation cycles, giving better separation.
📐 Diagram: Flask with liquid mixture → fractionating column → condenser → receiver. Label: fractionating column, thermometer, condenser, distillate collector. Show fractions condensing at different heights.
Industrial uses:
  • Fractional distillation of petroleum → petrol, kerosene, diesel, lubricating oil, etc.
  • Fractional distillation of liquid air → N₂ (bp –196°C), O₂ (bp –183°C), Ar (bp –186°C).
Ch4 E. State and explain the photoelectric effect. How did Einstein explain it? (3M)
Photoelectric Effect: When light of suitable frequency is incident on a metal surface, electrons are emitted from the metal. These electrons are called photoelectrons.
  • Observations: (1) No emission below threshold frequency (ν₀). (2) Emission is instantaneous above ν₀. (3) Number of electrons ∝ intensity of light. (4) KE of electrons depends only on frequency, not intensity.
Einstein's Explanation (1905):
  • Light consists of particles called photons, each with energy: E = hν
  • One photon ejects one electron. Energy is used to overcome work function (φ = hν₀) and give KE to electron: hν = φ + KE_max = hν₀ + ½mv²_max
  • Below threshold frequency, photon energy < φ → no emission.
  • This proved the particle nature of light and earned Einstein the Nobel Prize (1921).
Ch5 F. Explain covalent bond formation. Describe the formation of Cl₂ and N₂ molecules with Lewis structures. (3M)
Covalent Bond: A chemical bond formed by mutual sharing of electron pairs between two non-metal atoms, both atoms attaining the noble gas configuration (octet).
  • Proposed by G.N. Lewis; represented by Lewis dot structures.
Cl₂ (Single bond — 1 shared pair):
  • Each Cl has 7 valence electrons (2,8,7). Each needs 1 more → share 1 pair.
  • Cl–Cl: :Cl:Cl: → single σ bond; each Cl has 3 lone pairs + 1 bond pair → octet complete.
  • Bond order = 1
N₂ (Triple bond — 3 shared pairs):
  • Each N has 5 valence electrons. Each needs 3 more → share 3 pairs.
  • :N≡N: → triple bond (1σ + 2π); each N has 1 lone pair + 3 bond pairs → octet complete.
  • Bond order = 3; N₂ is very stable (high bond energy = 945 kJ/mol).
📐 Lewis structures: Cl:Cl with dots showing 3 lone pairs on each Cl + shared pair. N≡N with dots showing 1 lone pair on each N + triple bond lines.

Ch1 A. Iron (II) oxide (FeO) has the formula Fe₀.₉₅O. Calculate the percentage of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions. (Fe=56, O=16) (4M)
Given: Fe₀.₉₅O — 0.95 mol Fe for every 1 mol O. Let x = fraction of Fe²⁺ and (0.95–x) = fraction of Fe³⁺.
Charge balance (oxide has charge –2 for 1 O²⁻):
Total positive charge from Fe = 2x + 3(0.95–x) = 2 (from O²⁻)
2x + 2.85 – 3x = 2
–x = 2 – 2.85 = –0.85
x = 0.85 mol Fe²⁺
Fe³⁺ = 0.95 – 0.85 = 0.10 mol
% Fe²⁺ = (0.85/0.95) × 100 = 89.47%
% Fe³⁺ = (0.10/0.95) × 100 = 10.53%
Fe²⁺ = 89.47%; Fe³⁺ = 10.53%
Ch2 B. 25 mL of H₂SO₄ solution is neutralised by 50 mL of 0.1 M NaOH. Find the molarity and normality of H₂SO₄. Equation: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (4M)
Moles of NaOH used: n(NaOH) = 0.1 × 50/1000 = 0.005 mol
From equation: H₂SO₄ : NaOH = 1:2
Moles of H₂SO₄: n = 0.005/2 = 0.0025 mol
Molarity of H₂SO₄: M = 0.0025 / (25/1000) = 0.0025/0.025
M(H₂SO₄) = 0.1 mol/L
Normality: n-factor of H₂SO₄ = 2 (diprotic acid)
N = M × n-factor = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 N
Molarity of H₂SO₄ = 0.1 M; Normality = 0.2 N
Ch4 C. Explain the quantum mechanical model of atom. What are atomic orbitals? Describe the shapes of s, p, and d orbitals. (4M)
Quantum Mechanical Model (Schrödinger, 1926):
  • Based on wave nature of electrons (de Broglie) and Heisenberg uncertainty principle.
  • Schrödinger equation: Ĥψ = Eψ (ψ = wave function)
  • |ψ|² gives the probability density of finding an electron at a point in space.
  • Electrons do not have definite paths; they exist in probability regions called orbitals.
Atomic Orbitals: A 3-dimensional region in space around the nucleus where the probability of finding an electron is maximum (≥90%).

Shapes:
  • s orbital (l=0): Spherically symmetric. 1 orbital per subshell. Size increases with n (1s<2s<3s).
  • p orbital (l=1): Dumbbell shaped — 2 lobes on either side of nucleus along x, y, z axes. 3 orbitals per subshell (p_x, p_y, p_z).
  • d orbital (l=2): Double dumbbell / clover-leaf shape. 5 orbitals per subshell (d_xy, d_xz, d_yz, d_x²-y², d_z²).
📐 Diagram: (1) s orbital — sphere. (2) p_x orbital — two lobes along x-axis. (3) d_xy orbital — four lobes in xy-plane. Label nucleus at centre.
Ch5 D. Explain hybridisation of carbon in ethylene (C₂H₄) — sp² hybridisation. Include bond angles and diagram. (4M)
sp² Hybridisation in Ethylene (C₂H₄):
  • Each C in C₂H₄ undergoes sp² hybridisation.
  • Carbon excited state: 2s¹ 2p³ (4 unpaired electrons)
  • 1 s-orbital + 2 p-orbitals mix → 3 sp² hybrid orbitals + 1 unhybridised p-orbital (2pz)
  • 3 sp² orbitals lie in a plane, pointing to corners of equilateral triangle → trigonal planar arrangement, angle = 120°
Bonding in C₂H₄:
  • Each C forms 2 σ bonds with H (sp²–1s overlap) and 1 σ bond with the other C (sp²–sp² overlap).
  • The 2 unhybridised 2pz orbitals on each C overlap laterally → 1 π bond between the two C atoms.
  • C=C double bond = 1σ + 1π
  • Bond angle H–C–H ≈ 120°; C=C bond length = 134 pm (shorter than C–C = 154 pm)
📐 Diagram: Two C atoms, each with 3 sp² orbitals (triangular). sp²–sp² σ bond between C atoms. Two sp²–H σ bonds on each C. Show π bond (p-orbital lobes above and below the plane). Label: 120°, σ bond, π bond, H atoms.
Ch5 E. What is hydrogen bonding? Explain inter and intramolecular hydrogen bonding with examples. Why does water have an unusually high boiling point? (4M)
Hydrogen Bonding: A special type of electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O, N) and a lone pair of another electronegative atom. It is stronger than van der Waals forces but weaker than covalent/ionic bonds.
Represented as: X–H···Y (where X and Y = F, O or N)

Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding:
  • Occurs between different molecules of the same or different substances.
  • Example: Water (H₂O)–H···O–H₂O; HF–H···F–HF; NH₃–H···N–NH₃
  • Results in: Higher bp, higher viscosity, surface tension.
Intramolecular Hydrogen Bonding:
  • Occurs within the same molecule — between –H and another electronegative group in the same molecule (forms a ring).
  • Example: ortho-nitrophenol — O–H···O–N forms a 6-membered ring within the molecule.
  • Results in: Lower bp than expected (molecule already "satisfied" internally).
Why water has high boiling point:
  • Each water molecule forms up to 4 hydrogen bonds (2 as donor, 2 as acceptor).
  • These extensive H-bonds create a strong network → more energy needed to break them → unusually high bp (100°C) for a small molecule.
  • Compare: H₂S (no H-bond) has bp = –60°C; H₂O = 100°C — difference due to H-bonding.
