⚛️ Solid State
Q1
Define 'unit cell' in a crystal lattice.★★★
Show ▾
A unit cell is the smallest repeating structural unit of a crystalline solid that, when repeated in three dimensions, produces the complete crystal lattice.

Key points:
  • It is the fundamental building block of a crystal
  • Characterized by its edge lengths (a, b, c) and angles (α, β, γ) between them
  • Types: Primitive (simple), Body-centred (BCC), Face-centred (FCC/CCP), End-centred
  • In a primitive unit cell — atoms only at corners
  • In BCC — atoms at corners + one atom at body centre
  • In FCC — atoms at corners + atoms at all face centres
Number of atoms in FCC unit cell = 8×(1/8) + 6×(1/2) = 1 + 3 = 4 atoms
Q2
Distinguish between crystalline and amorphous solids. (Any three points)★★
Show ▾
Crystalline SolidsAmorphous Solids
Have a definite, regular and orderly arrangement of particles (long-range order)Particles are arranged randomly with no regular order (short-range order only)
Have a sharp melting pointMelt gradually over a range of temperatures — no sharp melting point
Anisotropic — different properties in different directionsIsotropic — same properties in all directions
True solids. E.g., NaCl, Diamond, Quartz, CopperPseudo-solids (supercooled liquids). E.g., Glass, Rubber, Plastic, Wax
Q3
Calculate the number of atoms in 0.5 mole of a face-centred cubic (FCC) metal.★★
Show ▾
Step 1 — Atoms per FCC unit cellIn FCC: atoms at 8 corners (8 × 1/8 = 1) + atoms at 6 faces (6 × 1/2 = 3)
Total = 4 atoms per unit cell
Step 2 — Total atoms in 0.5 mole1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms (Avogadro's number)
0.5 mole = 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³ atoms
Answer: 3.011 × 10²³ atoms

Note: The question asks for atoms in 0.5 mole of the metal — this doesn't depend on the unit cell type, but on the mole concept.

Q4
Calculate the packing efficiency of a body-centred cubic (BCC) unit cell.★★★
Show ▾
Step 1 — Number of atoms in BCCAtoms at 8 corners (8 × 1/8) + 1 atom at body centre = 1 + 1 = 2 atoms per BCC unit cell
Step 2 — Relation between r and aIn BCC, atoms touch along body diagonal:
Body diagonal = 4r and √3 a = 4r → r = (√3/4) a
Step 3 — Volume of atomsVolume of 2 spheres = 2 × (4/3)πr³ = 2 × (4/3)π × (√3/4)³ × a³ = (√3 π / 8) a³
Step 4 — Packing efficiencyPacking efficiency = (Volume of atoms / Volume of unit cell) × 100
Packing efficiency of BCC = (√3 π / 8) × 100 = 68%

Comparison: Simple cubic = 52.4%, BCC = 68%, FCC/CCP = 74% (most efficient)

🧪 Solutions & Colligative Properties
Q5
State Henry's Law.★★★
Show ▾
Henry's Law: "At constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid surface."

p = K_H × x    OR    C = K × P
Where:
  • p = partial pressure of the gas
  • K_H = Henry's law constant (different for each gas at a given temperature)
  • x = mole fraction of the gas in solution
Applications:
  • Carbonated beverages — CO₂ dissolved under high pressure
  • Deep-sea diving — bends (decompression sickness) explained by Henry's law
  • Oxygen supply to aquatic animals depends on dissolved O₂
Limitations: Valid only at low pressures and when the gas does not react with the solvent.
Q6
A solution containing 0.9 g of a non-volatile solute in 50 g of water boils at 100.1°C. Calculate the molar mass of the solute. (Kb for water = 0.52 K kg mol⁻¹)★★★
Show ▾
Given:
  • Mass of solute (w₂) = 0.9 g
  • Mass of solvent (w₁) = 50 g = 0.050 kg
  • Boiling point of solution = 100.1°C
  • Normal boiling point of water = 100°C
  • ΔTb = 100.1 − 100 = 0.1°C = 0.1 K
  • Kb = 0.52 K kg mol⁻¹
ΔTb = Kb × m = Kb × (w₂ / M₂) / w₁(kg)
Rearranging for Molar Mass (M₂)M₂ = (Kb × w₂) / (ΔTb × w₁)
M₂ = (0.52 × 0.9) / (0.1 × 0.050)
M₂ = 0.468 / 0.005
Molar Mass (M₂) = 93.6 g/mol ≈ 94 g/mol
⚖️ Ionic Equilibrium & Buffer Solutions
Q7
Write the expression for pH of a buffer solution (Henderson-Hasselbalch equation).★★★
Show ▾
The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives the pH of a buffer solution:

For an acidic buffer (weak acid + its salt):
pH = pKa + log [Salt] / [Acid]
For a basic buffer (weak base + its salt):
pOH = pKb + log [Salt] / [Base]
Where:
  • pKa = −log Ka (acid dissociation constant)
  • [Salt] = concentration of conjugate base (salt)
  • [Acid] = concentration of weak acid
Buffer action: A buffer resists change in pH on addition of small amounts of acid or base. When [Salt] = [Acid], pH = pKa (most effective buffer).
Q8
Calculate the pH of a buffer solution containing 0.1 M CH₃COOH and 0.1 M CH₃COONa. (Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵)★★★
Show ▾
Given:
  • [CH₃COOH] = 0.1 M (weak acid)
  • [CH₃COONa] = 0.1 M (salt/conjugate base)
  • Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵
Step 1 — Find pKapKa = −log Ka = −log (1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 5 − log 1.8 = 5 − 0.255 = 4.745
Step 2 — Apply Henderson-HasselbalchpH = pKa + log ([Salt]/[Acid])
pH = 4.745 + log (0.1/0.1)
pH = 4.745 + log (1)
pH = 4.745 + 0
pH = 4.74 ≈ 4.74

When [Salt] = [Acid], log ratio = 0 and pH = pKa. This is the most effective buffer condition.

Q9
Explain common ion effect with a suitable example.★★
Show ▾
Common Ion Effect: The suppression of ionization (dissociation) of a weak electrolyte when a strong electrolyte containing a common ion is added to its solution. This is an application of Le Chatelier's Principle.

Example:
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺   (weak acid, partially ionized)
When CH₃COONa (strong electrolyte) is added, it completely ionizes:
CH₃COONa → CH₃COO⁻ + Na⁺
The added CH₃COO⁻ (common ion) shifts the equilibrium to the left → suppresses ionization of CH₃COOH → reduces [H⁺] → pH increases.

Applications:
  • Buffer solutions work on the principle of common ion effect
  • Salting out of soaps — adding NaCl precipitates soap
  • Qualitative analysis — precipitation of specific ions
Q10
Derive the relationship between degree of dissociation (α) and van't Hoff factor (i) for an electrolyte AB.★★★
Show ▾
Consider electrolyte AB that dissociates into A⁺ and B⁻:
AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻
Let α = degree of dissociation (fraction of molecules that dissociate).
Start with 1 mole of AB:
SpeciesInitial molesAt equilibrium
AB11 − α
A⁺0α
B⁻0α
Total moles at equilibrium = (1 − α) + α + α = 1 + α

Van't Hoff factor: i = observed moles / expected moles (if no dissociation)
i = (1 + α) / 1 = 1 + α
Therefore: α = i − 1
For an electrolyte giving n ions: AB → nA, then i = 1 + (n−1)α → α = (i−1)/(n−1)
🔗 Halogen Compounds
Q11
Write the mechanism of SN2 reaction of CH₃Br with OH⁻.★★★
Show ▾
SN2 = Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular

Reaction:
CH₃Br + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Br⁻
Mechanism:
  • Step 1: OH⁻ (nucleophile) attacks the carbon atom of CH₃Br from the back side (opposite to the leaving group Br)
  • Step 2: A transition state is formed where both OH and Br are partially bonded to the carbon — penta-coordinate transition state [HO---CH₃---Br]⁻
  • Step 3: As OH forms a complete bond, Br⁻ leaves — this is a single-step concerted mechanism
  • Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) occurs — like an umbrella turning inside out
Key features of SN2:
  • Single-step mechanism — no intermediate
  • Rate = k[CH₃Br][OH⁻] — bimolecular (second order overall)
  • Favoured by primary alkyl halides (less steric hindrance)
  • Complete inversion of configuration at chiral centre
  • Strong nucleophile required (OH⁻, CN⁻, I⁻)
🌿 Organic Chemistry — Alcohols, Phenol & Aldehydes
Q12
Write the product when ethanol is heated with conc. H₂SO₄ at 170°C.★★★
Show ▾
When ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is heated with concentrated H₂SO₄ at 170°C, elimination reaction (dehydration) occurs to form ethene (ethylene):
C₂H₅OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O   (conc. H₂SO₄, 170°C)
Mechanism (E1cb / E2):
  • H₂SO₄ protonates the OH group → forms oxonium ion
  • Water leaves as leaving group → carbocation forms
  • H⁺ is removed from adjacent carbon → double bond (C=C) forms
Note: At lower temperature (140°C), the same reaction with ethanol gives diethyl ether:
2 C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ + H₂O   (conc. H₂SO₄, 140°C)
Q13
Explain why phenol undergoes electrophilic substitution more readily than benzene.★★★
Show ▾
Phenol (C₆H₅OH) undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) much more readily than benzene because of the activating effect of the –OH group.

Reason:
  • The oxygen atom of –OH group has lone pairs of electrons
  • These lone pairs are in resonance with the benzene ring → the electron density of the ring increases (especially at ortho and para positions)
  • This makes the ring more electron-rich → more attractive for electrophiles (E⁺)
  • –OH is a strong ortho-para directing group and ring activator
In benzene: No activating group → ring is less electron-rich → requires stronger electrophile and harsher conditions.

Result: Phenol reacts with bromine water (Br₂/H₂O) directly at room temperature to give 2,4,6-tribromophenol — no catalyst needed. Benzene requires Br₂/FeBr₃ (Lewis acid catalyst).
Q14
Write the reactions involved in the preparation of phenol from cumene.★★★
Show ▾
Cumene process (industrial preparation of phenol):

Step 1 — Oxidation of Cumene
C₆H₅–CH(CH₃)₂ + O₂ → C₆H₅–C(CH₃)₂–OOH
(Cumene)                (Cumene hydroperoxide)
Cumene (isopropylbenzene) is oxidised in air in the presence of traces of metal catalyst at moderate temperature to form cumene hydroperoxide.

Step 2 — Acid hydrolysis of Cumene hydroperoxide
C₆H₅–C(CH₃)₂–OOH → C₆H₅OH + CH₃COCH₃
(Cumene hydroperoxide) → (Phenol) + (Acetone)
(dil. H₂SO₄)
Cumene hydroperoxide is treated with dilute H₂SO₄ to yield phenol and acetone as a byproduct.

Significance: This process gives two useful products — phenol AND acetone — simultaneously. It is the major industrial method for phenol production.
Q15
(a) Write the reactions for conversion of: (i) Ethanol to ethanal (ii) Acetone to isopropyl alcohol★★
Show ▾
(i) Ethanol to Ethanal (Oxidation of ethanol):
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃CHO
(Ethanol)       (Ethanal/Acetaldehyde)
Oxidising agent: PCC (Pyridinium chlorochromate) or [O] mild
Ethanol (primary alcohol) is oxidised to ethanal (aldehyde) using mild oxidising agents like PCC, or CrO₃/pyridine. Strong oxidation gives acetic acid (ethanoic acid).

(ii) Acetone to Isopropyl Alcohol (Reduction of ketone):
CH₃COCH₃ + H₂ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃
(Acetone)           (Isopropyl alcohol / Propan-2-ol)
Catalyst: Ni, Pt or LiAlH₄ (NaBH₄)
Acetone (ketone) is reduced to isopropyl alcohol (secondary alcohol) using NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ or catalytic hydrogenation.
Q16
(b) Explain Cannizzaro reaction with an example.★★★
Show ▾
Cannizzaro Reaction: Aldehydes having no α-hydrogen (alpha hydrogen) undergo self-disproportionation (one molecule gets oxidised, another gets reduced) on treatment with concentrated alkali (NaOH).

Example — Formaldehyde (HCHO):
2 HCHO + NaOH(conc.) → HCOONa + CH₃OH
(Formaldehyde)             (Sodium formate) + (Methanol)
Example — Benzaldehyde (C₆H₅CHO):
2 C₆H₅CHO + NaOH(conc.) → C₆H₅COONa + C₆H₅CH₂OH
(Benzaldehyde)                 (Sodium benzoate) + (Benzyl alcohol)
Key points:
  • Only aldehydes WITHOUT α-hydrogen (like HCHO, C₆H₅CHO) undergo Cannizzaro reaction
  • One molecule acts as reducing agent (gets oxidised to carboxylate salt)
  • Other molecule acts as oxidising agent (gets reduced to alcohol)
  • Requires concentrated alkali
Q17
(c) Why do aldehydes have lower boiling points than corresponding alcohols?★★
Show ▾
Aldehydes have lower boiling points than corresponding alcohols of similar molecular mass because of differences in intermolecular forces:

Alcohols:
  • Have –OH group → can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with other alcohol molecules
  • Hydrogen bonding is a strong intermolecular force → requires more energy to break → higher boiling point
Aldehydes:
  • Have C=O group (carbonyl) but NO –OH hydrogen
  • Cannot form hydrogen bonds with each other (only dipole-dipole and van der Waals forces)
  • Weaker intermolecular forces → less energy needed to vaporise → lower boiling point
Example: Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) boils at 78°C; Acetaldehyde/Ethanal (CH₃CHO) boils at only 20°C — despite similar molecular mass.
🌐 p-Block Elements
Q18
(a) Explain the structure of ozone (O₃) with a diagram.★★★
Show ▾
Ozone (O₃) — allotrope of oxygen:

Structure:
  • Ozone is a triatomic bent (V-shaped) molecule
  • Bond angle: 117° (approximately)
  • The central oxygen is sp² hybridized
  • Bond length: 1.28 Å (intermediate between O–O single bond 1.48 Å and O=O double bond 1.21 Å)
  • This intermediate bond length is due to resonance
Resonance structures:
O=O–O⁻ ↔ ⁻O–O=O    (Two resonance forms)
The actual structure is a resonance hybrid — both O–O bonds are equivalent with bond order 1.5.

Properties:
  • Pale blue gas with characteristic smell
  • Strong oxidising agent
  • Absorbs harmful UV-B and UV-C radiation in stratosphere (ozone layer)
  • Decomposes on heating: 2O₃ → 3O₂
Q19
(b) Why is H₂O a liquid whereas H₂S is a gas at room temperature?★★★
Show ▾
H₂O is a liquid and H₂S is a gas at room temperature because of the difference in their ability to form hydrogen bonds:

H₂O (Water — liquid at 25°C):
  • Oxygen (O) has high electronegativity (3.5) and small atomic size
  • The O–H bond is highly polar → water molecules form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O–H···O)
  • Each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds
  • Strong hydrogen bonding requires much more energy to overcome → high boiling point (100°C) → liquid at room temperature
H₂S (Hydrogen sulphide — gas at 25°C):
  • Sulphur (S) has lower electronegativity (2.5) and larger atomic size
  • S–H bond is less polar → H₂S cannot form effective hydrogen bonds
  • Only weak van der Waals forces between H₂S molecules
  • Much less energy needed to vaporise → low boiling point (−60°C) → gas at room temperature
Conclusion: Hydrogen bonding in H₂O is responsible for its anomalously high boiling point compared to H₂S.
Q20
(c) Write two uses of noble gases.★★
Show ▾
Noble gases (Group 18: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) — Uses:

Helium (He):
  • Used in airships and weather balloons (non-flammable, light)
  • Used in deep-sea diving — mixed with oxygen (helium-oxygen mixture) to avoid nitrogen narcosis
  • Used in MRI machines as a coolant for superconducting magnets
Neon (Ne):
  • Used in neon signs and advertisement boards — gives characteristic orange-red glow
  • Used in lasers (He-Ne laser)
Argon (Ar):
  • Used in electric bulbs and fluorescent tubes as an inert filler gas (prevents filament from burning)
  • Used in welding as a shielding gas (prevents oxidation)
Radon (Rn): Used in radiotherapy for cancer treatment.
