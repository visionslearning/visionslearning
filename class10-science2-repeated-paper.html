Ch1🟠 4× A. Homologous and analogous organs — definitions with 2 examples each and their evolutionary significance. (4M)
Homologous Organs: Organs with the same basic structural plan and origin (derived from same ancestral structure) but performing different functions due to adaptation to different environments.
  • Examples: Forelimbs of human (grasping), bat (flying), whale (swimming), horse (running) — same bones (humerus, radius, ulna) differently modified.
  • Thorns (rose) and tendrils (cucumber) — both modified stems.
  • Significance: Evidence of divergent evolution — all evolved from a common ancestor.
Analogous Organs: Organs with different structural origins but performing the same function due to adaptation to similar environments.
  • Examples: Wings of butterfly (exoskeleton modification) and wings of bird (forelimb modification) — both used for flying but structurally different.
  • Eyes of octopus (mollusc) and human (vertebrate) — similar function, different origin.
  • Significance: Evidence of convergent evolution — unrelated organisms evolving similar traits independently.

Examples:
  • Aerobic: all higher organisms under normal conditions.
  • Anaerobic: yeast during fermentation; muscle cells during intense exercise; bacteria in marshy/waterlogged soil.
Ch4🟠 4× C. Water pollution — causes, effects, control and eutrophication. (4M)
Causes of Water Pollution:
  • Industrial effluents (heavy metals, dyes, chemicals) discharged into rivers.
  • Domestic sewage and untreated wastewater.
  • Agricultural runoff — fertilisers (nitrates, phosphates), pesticides, herbicides.
  • Plastic waste; oil spills; religious waste (idol immersion).
Effects:
  • Waterborne diseases — cholera, typhoid, dysentery.
  • Death of aquatic organisms (fish kills).
  • Bioaccumulation of toxins in food chain (biomagnification).
  • Decreased dissolved oxygen → suffocation of aquatic life.
Control Measures:
  • Sewage treatment plants (STPs) before discharge.
  • Industrial effluent treatment; ban on plastic near water bodies.
  • Organic farming; Namami Gange programme.
Eutrophication: Excess nitrates/phosphates from agricultural runoff → explosive algal bloom → algae block sunlight → submerged plants die → bacteria decompose algae → consume all dissolved O₂ → aquatic animals die → dead zone.
Ch5🟠 4× D. Biogas — production, composition, uses and why it is considered green energy. (4M)
Biogas Production:
  • Organic waste (cattle dung + water, 1:1) fed into airtight digester tank.
  • Anaerobic bacteria decompose organic matter → biogas collects in dome.
  • Slurry (spent material) flows out → used as organic fertiliser.
Composition: Methane (CH₄) 55–70% + CO₂ 25–40% + traces of H₂S, H₂.
Uses: Cooking fuel, electricity generation, street lighting, running engines.
Why Green Energy?
  • Uses waste → solves waste disposal problem.
  • Carbon neutral — CO₂ released was recently absorbed by plants.
  • Reduces deforestation (replaces firewood).
  • Slurry is better fertiliser than raw dung → improves soil fertility.
  • No net environmental pollution.

Ch1🔴 5× A. Explain Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection with the example of giraffe's neck. State the limitations of Darwin's theory. (4M)
Darwin's Theory (1859) — Key Points:
  • Overproduction: Organisms produce more offspring than can survive (e.g., one fern plant produces millions of spores).
  • Natural variation: Individuals in a population vary in traits due to random mutations and genetic mixing.
  • Struggle for existence: Limited food, space, mates → competition among individuals.
  • Survival of the fittest: Individuals with favourable variations survive; others die out. "Fit" = best adapted to the environment.
  • Natural selection: Environment selects which variations survive.
  • Inheritance of favourable traits: Survivors pass advantageous traits to offspring → gradual change in population → evolution.
Giraffe's long neck (example):
  • Ancestral giraffes had varying neck lengths.
  • Long-necked individuals reached more leaves → better nourishment → survived + reproduced more.
  • Short-necked ones could not compete → gradually eliminated.
  • After many generations → all giraffes became long-necked.
Limitations:
  • Did not explain the origin/source of variations (genetics was unknown to Darwin).
  • Cannot explain sudden large changes (saltation/macromutations).
  • Does not incorporate molecular biology, genetics, or fossil dating methods.
Ch3🔴 5× B. With a neat labelled diagram, explain the structure of the human heart. Describe the double circulation and the role of valves. (4M)
Structure of Human Heart:
  • Muscular organ about the size of a fist; located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs.
  • Divided into 4 chambers: Right Auricle (RA), Right Ventricle (RV), Left Auricle (LA), Left Ventricle (LV).
  • Right side → deoxygenated blood; Left side → oxygenated blood.
  • Septum separates left and right sides (prevents mixing).
Double Circulation:
  • Pulmonary circulation: RV → Pulmonary artery → Lungs (gaseous exchange: CO₂ out, O₂ in) → Pulmonary vein → LA → LV.
  • Systemic circulation: LV → Aorta → All body organs (O₂ delivered, CO₂ collected) → Superior/Inferior Vena Cava → RA → RV.
  • Blood passes through heart twice per circuit → called double circulation → ensures higher blood pressure for efficient O₂ delivery.
Role of Valves:
  • Tricuspid valve (RA→RV): Prevents backflow from RV to RA.
  • Bicuspid/Mitral valve (LA→LV): Prevents backflow from LV to LA.
  • Semilunar valves (in aorta and pulmonary artery): Prevent backflow into ventricles after pumping.
  • Valves ensure one-directional blood flow.

Ch2🔴 5× C. Explain photosynthesis — raw materials, products, conditions, two stages, and the overall chemical equation. (4M)
Photosynthesis: Process by which green plants synthesise food (glucose) from inorganic substances using sunlight energy.
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → (sunlight + chlorophyll) → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Raw MaterialsProductsConditions Required
CO₂ (from air via stomata)Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)Sunlight (energy source)
H₂O (from soil via roots)Oxygen (O₂) — releasedChlorophyll (absorbs light)
Sunlight, ChlorophyllWater vapour (transpired)Suitable temperature (25–35°C)
Two Stages of Photosynthesis:
  • 1. Light Reaction (in thylakoid membranes):
    • Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight.
    • Water is split by photolysis: 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂↑
    • O₂ is released; ATP and NADPH (energy carriers) are produced.
  • 2. Dark Reaction / Calvin Cycle (in stroma):
    • CO₂ is fixed (combined with RuBP) using ATP and NADPH.
    • Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is synthesised.
    • Does not directly require light but uses products of light reaction.
Importance: Base of all food chains; releases O₂ for respiration; removes CO₂ from atmosphere; stores solar energy as chemical energy.

Ch2🔴 5× A. A student places a Hydrilla (water plant) in a beaker of water in bright sunlight. After 30 minutes, gas bubbles are seen rising from the plant. Answer: (4M)
🧪 Activity Answer — Appeared in 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023
  • (i) Name the gas coming out of the plant.
    → Oxygen (O₂) — produced during the light reaction of photosynthesis when water is split (photolysis of water).
  • (ii) Write the chemical reaction taking place inside the plant.
    → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → (sunlight + chlorophyll) → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂↑
  • (iii) What will happen if the same experiment is done in the dark?
    → No gas bubbles will be produced. Without sunlight, photosynthesis cannot occur (light reaction stops) → no O₂ is produced.
  • (iv) How can you confirm the gas produced is oxygen?
    → Collect the gas in a test tube and insert a glowing (not burning) wooden splint — it will burst into flame (relight). Oxygen supports combustion — this is the glowing splint test for O₂.
  • (v) Conclusion:
    → Sunlight is essential for photosynthesis. Green plants release O₂ as a by-product when they convert CO₂ and H₂O to glucose in the presence of light and chlorophyll.
Ch3🟠 4× B. A student touches a hot object and immediately withdraws their hand. They feel pain slightly later. Explain this observation using the concept of reflex arc. (4M)
🧪 Activity Answer — Appeared in 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024
  • (i) Why does the hand withdraw before feeling pain?
    → This is a reflex action. The reflex arc passes through the spinal cord (not the brain), so the response is faster. The pain signal takes longer because it must travel all the way to the brain. The hand withdraws even before the brain processes the sensation of pain.
  • (ii) Draw and explain the reflex arc for this action.
    → Stimulus (hot object) → Receptor (skin pain receptors) → Sensory neuron → Spinal cord (relay neuron interprets; sends motor signal) → Motor neuron → Effector (arm muscles contract) → Response (hand withdrawn).
    → Simultaneously, a signal travels up the spinal cord to the brain → brain registers pain → person feels pain.
  • (iii) What is the advantage of reflex action over voluntary action?
    → Reflex actions are extremely fast because the pathway (spinal cord) is shorter than involving the brain. This protects the body from damage — e.g., withdrawing from heat, sneezing, blinking prevent injury before the brain can respond deliberately.
  • (iv) Name two other examples of reflex action.
    → (a) Knee-jerk reflex (patellar reflex) — tapping below the knee makes the leg kick. (b) Blinking when something approaches the eye suddenly. (c) Sneezing/coughing when irritants enter nose/throat.
📐 Draw reflex arc: receptor (skin) → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector (muscle). Label direction of impulse. Show separate pathway from spinal cord to brain (pain perception).
Ch4🟠 4× C. During a field visit, students observe a lake near a factory. The lake water is greenish, has a foul smell and most fish are dead. There is no plant growth below the water surface. Answer: (4M)
🧪 Activity Answer — Appeared in 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023
  • (i) What phenomenon does the greenish colour of water indicate?
    → Eutrophication and algal bloom. Excess nutrients (nitrates, phosphates) from factory effluents/agricultural runoff → explosive growth of algae → water turns green.
  • (ii) Why are the fish dead?
    → The algae cover the water surface → block sunlight to underwater plants → plants die → bacteria decompose dead algae and plants → bacteria consume all dissolved oxygen (O₂) from water → fish and other aquatic organisms suffocate and die (hypoxia — oxygen depletion).
  • (iii) Why is there no plant growth below the water surface?
    → The algal bloom on the surface blocks sunlight from penetrating the water → submerged aquatic plants cannot carry out photosynthesis → they die and disappear.
  • (iv) Suggest two solutions to restore the lake.
    → (a) Stop factory effluents from entering the lake — implement sewage treatment plants (STPs) to treat wastewater before discharge.
    → (b) Phytoremediation — introduce water hyacinth or algae-eating fish (like Tilapia) to absorb excess nutrients and restore balance. Also aerate the water (add O₂) to help recovery.
