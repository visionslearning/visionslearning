Chapter 1 — Units and Measurements
Pages 1–15
Q.1
What are fundamental quantities and derived quantities? Give examples.
Fundamental
Independent quantities: Length, Mass, Time, Temperature, Current, Luminous intensity, Amount of substance. (7 total)
Derived
Expressed in terms of fundamentals. e.g. velocity = m/s, force = kg·m/s², energy = kg·m²/s², pressure = kg/m·s²
∴ 7 fundamental quantities + many derived make the SI system.
Q.2
Show that 1 newton = 10⁵ dyne and 1 joule = 10⁷ erg.
1 N = 10⁵ dyne
F = [M¹L¹T⁻²]. 1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻² = 1000 g × 100 cm × s⁻² = 10⁵ dyne ✓
1 J = 10⁷ erg
E = [M¹L²T⁻²]. 1 J = 1 kg·m²·s⁻² = 1000 g × (100)² cm² × s⁻² = 10⁷ erg ✓
Q.3
State applications and limitations of dimensional analysis.
Applications
  • Check correctness of equations (principle of homogeneity)
  • Derive relations between physical quantities
  • Convert units between systems (CGS ↔ SI)
  • Find dimensions of physical constants
Limitations
  • Cannot find dimensionless constants (2π, ½ etc.)
  • Cannot derive relations with more than 3 unknowns
  • Cannot work with trigonometric, exponential, log functions
  • Cannot tell scalar from vector
Q.4
Define dimensions. State the principle of homogeneity with example.
Dimensions
Powers to which fundamental units (M, L, T) are raised to represent a quantity. e.g. Force = [M¹L¹T⁻²], Velocity = [L¹T⁻¹]
Homogeneity
An equation is correct only if dimensions on both sides are equal. Check v = u + at: [LT⁻¹] = [LT⁻¹] + [LT⁻²·T] = [LT⁻¹] ✓
Q.5
Define significant figures. State rules for counting them.
Definition
Digits that carry meaning and contribute to measurement accuracy.
Rules
  • All non-zero digits significant: 3274 → 4 s.f.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits significant: 3007 → 4 s.f.
  • Leading zeros NOT significant: 0.0034 → 2 s.f.
  • Trailing zeros after decimal ARE significant: 3.200 → 4 s.f.
Q.6
Define error. Explain types of errors — absolute, relative, percentage.
Error
Difference between measured value and true value.
Types
  • Systematic: Consistent errors due to faulty instrument/method
  • Random: Unpredictable fluctuations; reduced by taking mean
  • Absolute error: |True − Measured|   Δa
  • Relative error: Absolute error/Mean value   Δa/aₘ
  • Percentage error: Relative × 100   (Δa/aₘ)×100

Chapter 2 — Mathematical Methods (Vectors)
Pages 16–29
Q.1
State and derive the Parallelogram law of vector addition. Find magnitude and direction of resultant.
Statement
If two vectors are represented by adjacent sides of a parallelogram from a common point, the diagonal gives their resultant.
Derivation
Let angle between A and B = θ. Drop ⊥ from tip of B to extended A.
  • R² = (A + B cosθ)² + (B sinθ)² = A² + 2AB cosθ + B²
Formula
R = √(A²+B²+2AB cosθ)  ;  tan α = B sinθ/(A + B cosθ)
Special cases
θ=0°: R=A+B (max); θ=90°: R=√(A²+B²); θ=180°: R=|A−B| (min)
∴ R = √(A²+B²+2AB cosθ)
Q.2
Define scalar product. State its characteristics. Give physical examples.
Definition
A·B = |A||B|cosθ — scalar result.
Characteristics
  • Commutative: A·B = B·A
  • Distributive: A·(B+C) = A·B + A·C
  • A⊥B → A·B = 0; A∥B → A·B = AB (max)
  • Self: A·A = A²;   î·î=1, î·ĵ=0
Examples
Work W = F·d; Power P = F·v; both scalar results.
∴ A·B = ABcosθ (scalar)
Q.3
Define vector product. State its characteristics. Show |A×B| = area of parallelogram.
Definition
A×B = ABsinθ n̂ — vector, ⊥ to plane of A & B (right-hand rule)
Characteristics
  • Anti-commutative: A×B = −(B×A)
  • A∥B → A×B = 0; A⊥B → |A×B| = AB (max)
  • Self: A×A = 0; î×ĵ = k̂; ĵ×k̂ = î; k̂×î = ĵ
Proof area
Area of ∥gram = base × height = A × B sinθ = |A×B| ✓
Examples
Torque τ = r×F; Angular momentum L = r×p
∴ A×B = ABsinθ n̂ (vector); |A×B| = area of parallelogram
Q.4
What is resolution of a vector? Resolve vector A in 2D and 3D.
Definition
Splitting a vector into components along chosen directions.
2D
Aₓ = A cosθ (x-component);   Aᵧ = A sinθ (y-component);   A = √(Aₓ²+Aᵧ²)
3D
A⃗ = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ + A_zk̂;   A = √(Aₓ²+Aᵧ²+A_z²)
Q.5
Express vector product in determinant form.
Determinant
A×B = | î   ĵ   k̂ |
       | Aₓ Aᵧ A_z |
       | Bₓ Bᵧ B_z |
Expanding
= î(AᵧB_z−A_zBᵧ) − ĵ(AₓB_z−A_zBₓ) + k̂(AₓBᵧ−AᵧBₓ)

Chapter 6 — Mechanical Properties of Solids (Elasticity)
Pages 100–113
Q.1
State Hooke's law. Define modulus of elasticity. Write formula for Young's modulus.
Hooke's Law
Within elastic limit, stress ∝ strain.   Stress = E × Strain where E = modulus of elasticity.
Young's Modulus
Ratio of longitudinal stress to longitudinal strain. Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/AΔL   Unit: N/m² (Pa)
Bulk Modulus
K = −V(ΔP/ΔV) — for volumetric stress/strain.
Modulus of Rigidity
η = Shear stress / Shear strain
∴ Stress ∝ Strain (within elastic limit) → Y = FL/AΔL
Q.2
Define stress and its types. Define strain and its types.
Stress
Restoring force per unit area. σ = F/A Unit: N/m² (Pa)
Types of Stress
  • Tensile/Compressive (Normal): Force ⊥ to surface — elongation or compression
  • Volumetric (Bulk): Equal forces from all sides — change in volume
  • Shear (Tangential): Force ∥ to surface — change in shape
Strain
Change in dimension / Original dimension. Dimensionless.
Types of Strain
  • Longitudinal: ΔL/L (change in length)
  • Volumetric: ΔV/V (change in volume)
  • Shear: Angular deformation φ (in radians)
Q.3
Derive expression for strain energy per unit volume of a stretched wire.
Derivation
  • Work done to stretch by dx: dW = F·dx
  • F = (YA/L)·x (from Hooke's law)
  • Total work W = ∫₀^ΔL (YA/L)x·dx = YA(ΔL)²/2L = ½F·ΔL
Per unit volume
u = W/(AL) = ½ × Stress × Strain = ½ × Y × (strain)²   Unit: J/m³
∴ Strain energy/volume = ½ × stress × strain
Q.4
Explain the stress-strain curve. Define elastic limit, yield point, breaking stress.
OA (Elastic region)
Stress ∝ strain (Hooke's law obeyed). Point A = proportional limit.
AB
Hooke's law not followed but body still elastic. Point B = elastic limit — body returns to original shape.
BC
Plastic deformation begins. Point C = yield point — large strain with little increase in stress.
CD
Strain hardening. Point D = ultimate/breaking stress (maximum stress the material can withstand).
DE
Necking — wire thins at one point and fractures at E = breaking point.
Q.5
Define elasticity and plasticity. What is elastic fatigue? What is Poisson's ratio?
Elasticity
Property by which a body regains its original shape after removal of deforming force. e.g. rubber, steel spring.
Plasticity
Property by which a body does NOT regain its original shape. e.g. clay, putty.
Elastic Fatigue
Loss of elastic property of a material due to repeated stress and strain. Material eventually breaks at stress below its breaking point.
Poisson's Ratio
σ = (Lateral strain)/(Longitudinal strain) = (ΔD/D)/(ΔL/L) — dimensionless. Range: −1 to 0.5.
Q.6
State factors affecting elasticity. Which is more elastic — steel or rubber? Why?
Factors
  • Temperature: Elasticity decreases with rise in temperature (exception: invar steel)
  • Impurities: Generally decrease elasticity
  • Hammering/Annealing: Affects crystal structure
Steel vs Rubber
Steel is more elastic than rubber because for the same stress, steel shows less strain (higher Young's modulus). More elastic = greater restoring force per unit deformation.
∴ Steel is more elastic: Y_steel >> Y_rubber

Chapter 8 — Sound
Pages 142–158
Q.1
State Newton's formula for velocity of sound. Explain Laplace's correction.
Newton's Formula
Assumed isothermal: v = √(P/ρ). At STP: v = 280 m/s — incorrect.
Limitation
Sound propagation is NOT isothermal. Rapid compressions/rarefactions — no heat exchange.
Laplace Correction
Process is adiabatic: v = √(γP/ρ) where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 1.4 for air.
Result
v = √(1.4 × 10⁵/1.29) ≈ 332 m/s ✓ (matches experiment)
∴ v = √(γP/ρ) ≈ 332 m/s at STP — Laplace's corrected formula.
Q.2
Explain the effect of temperature, pressure and humidity on velocity of sound in air.
Temperature
v ∝ √T. Velocity increases with temperature. For every 1°C rise: v increases by 0.61 m/s. vₜ = v₀√(T/273)
Pressure
At constant temperature, velocity is independent of pressure. (Both P and ρ change proportionally, P/ρ = constant)
Humidity
Moist air (H₂O, M=18) lighter than dry air (M=29). Density decreases. v ∝ 1/√ρ → velocity greater in humid air.
∴ v increases with T and humidity; independent of pressure at constant T.
Q.3
What are stationary (standing) waves? State conditions for their formation and properties.
Definition
Formed by superposition of two identical progressive waves travelling in opposite directions in same medium. No energy transfer.
Formation
Incident wave + Reflected wave → superpose → form nodes and antinodes.
Properties
  • Nodes: zero amplitude, fixed. Distance between nodes = λ/2
  • Antinodes: max amplitude. Distance between antinodes = λ/2
  • Node to nearest antinode = λ/4
  • Particles between nodes vibrate in same phase
  • No energy propagation
Q.4
Distinguish between transverse and longitudinal waves.
Transverse
Vibration ⊥ to propagation. Crests and troughs. In solids and liquid surface. e.g. Light, string.
Longitudinal
Vibration ∥ to propagation. Compressions and rarefactions. In all media (solid, liquid, gas). e.g. Sound.
Q.5
Explain the principle of superposition. Define beats and state the condition for beats.
Superposition
Resultant displacement = algebraic sum of individual displacements: y = y₁ + y₂
Beats
Periodic variation in loudness (waxing and waning) due to superposition of two sound waves of slightly different frequencies.
Beat frequency
n_beats = |n₁ − n₂| (difference of frequencies). Audible if difference < 10 Hz.
Applications
Tuning musical instruments; finding unknown frequency.
Q.6
Explain Doppler effect in sound. Give its applications.
Definition
Apparent change in frequency of sound due to relative motion between source and observer.
Formula
n' = n × (v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s) where v = speed of sound, v_o = observer speed, v_s = source speed.
+/− Rule
Upper sign when moving towards each other (frequency increases); lower sign when moving apart.
Applications
RADAR, SONAR, Ambulance siren, Measuring speed of vehicles, Astronomy (redshift/blueshift).

Chapter 10 — Electrostatics
Pages 188–206
Q.1
State Coulomb's law. Write its vector form. Define electric field intensity.
Coulomb's Law
Force between two point charges is ∝ product of charges and ∝ 1/r². F = kq₁q₂/r² k = 9×10⁹ N·m²/C²
Vector form
F⃗₁₂ = kq₁q₂/r² · r̂₁₂ (−ve for attraction, +ve for repulsion)
Electric Field
Force per unit positive test charge: E = F/q₀ = kQ/r² Unit: N/C or V/m (vector)
∴ F = kq₁q₂/r²; E = kQ/r²
Q.2
State and prove Gauss's theorem. Apply it to find E due to infinite plane sheet.
Gauss's Theorem
Total electric flux through any closed surface = charge enclosed/ε₀. ∮E·dA = q_enc/ε₀
Proof
For sphere of radius r around charge Q: φ = E × 4πr² = Q/ε₀ → E = Q/(4πε₀r²) = kQ/r² ✓
Plane Sheet
Gaussian surface = cylinder through sheet. Flux = 2EA = σA/ε₀ → E = σ/(2ε₀)   (uniform, independent of distance)
∴ E = σ/(2ε₀) for infinite plane sheet of charge density σ.
Q.3
Define electric potential. Derive V due to a point charge. Explain equipotential surfaces.
Potential
Work done per unit +ve charge to bring it from ∞ to that point. V = W/q Unit: Volt (V) = J/C (scalar)
Derivation
W = ∫F·dr = kQ∫dr/r² = kQ/r → V = kQ/r
Equipotential
Surface on which all points are at same potential. Properties: E ⊥ to it; no work done moving charge on it; closer surfaces = stronger E; for point charge: concentric spheres.
Q.4
Define capacitance. Derive capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor. Effect of dielectric.
Capacitance
C = Q/V Unit: Farad (F). Ability to store charge.
Derivation
E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A); V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A) → C = Q/V = ε₀A/d
With dielectric
C = Kε₀A/d where K = dielectric constant (K>1 → C increases)
∴ C = ε₀A/d (air); C = Kε₀A/d (with dielectric)
Q.5
Define electric dipole and dipole moment. Find E on axial and equatorial positions.
Dipole
+q and −q separated by 2l. Dipole moment: p = q·2l C·m, direction: −q → +q.
Axial E
E_axial = 2kp/r³ (for r>>l) — along dipole direction
Equatorial E
E_eq = kp/r³ (for r>>l) — antiparallel to dipole
Note
E_axial = 2×E_equatorial at same distance r
Q.6
State properties of electric lines of force. Define electric flux.
Properties
  • Start from +ve charge, end at −ve charge
  • Never intersect (E unique at every point)
  • Always ⊥ to conductor surface
  • Tangent = direction of E at that point
  • Denser lines = stronger field
  • E = 0 inside conductor (no lines inside)
Electric Flux
φ = E·A cosθ Unit: N·m²/C. Total field lines through a surface.

Chapter 12 — Magnetism
Pages 221–228
Q.1
Classify magnetic materials — Diamagnetic, Paramagnetic and Ferromagnetic. State their properties.
Diamagnetic
  • Weakly repelled by external magnets
  • Susceptibility χ < 0 (small negative); μᵣ slightly < 1
  • Examples: Bismuth, copper, gold, silver, water
  • Not affected by temperature
Paramagnetic
  • Weakly attracted by external magnets
  • χ > 0 (small positive); μᵣ slightly > 1
  • Examples: Aluminium, platinum, oxygen, sodium
  • χ ∝ 1/T (Curie's law)
Ferromagnetic
  • Strongly attracted by external magnets
  • χ >> 0 (large positive); μᵣ >> 1
  • Examples: Iron, nickel, cobalt
  • Retain magnetism (hysteresis); domains present
  • Lose ferromagnetism above Curie temperature (become paramagnetic)
∴ Dia: repelled (χ<0); Para: weakly attracted; Ferro: strongly attracted (χ>>0)
Q.2
Define magnetic susceptibility, permeability and retentivity. State Curie's law.
Susceptibility χ
χ = M/H — ratio of magnetisation to magnetic intensity. Dimensionless. Measures ease of magnetisation.
Permeability μ
μ = B/H — ability to allow magnetic field through material. μ = μ₀μᵣ. Unit: H/m. μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.
Retentivity
Magnetic flux density remaining in a material when magnetising field H is reduced to zero. Measure of permanent magnetism.
Curie's Law
For paramagnetic: χ = C/T (C = Curie constant). Susceptibility decreases as temperature increases.
∴ χ = M/H; μ = B/H; Curie's law: χ = C/T
Q.3
Define magnetisation (M), magnetic intensity (H) and state their relation with B.
Magnetisation M
Magnetic moment per unit volume. M = m/V Unit: A/m.
Magnetic Intensity H
The magnetising field. H = B/μ₀ − M Unit: A/m. Applied external field's ability to magnetise.
Relation B, H, M
B = μ₀(H + M) = μ₀μᵣH = μH
μᵣ
Relative permeability = μ/μ₀ = 1 + χ
Q.4
State properties of magnetic field lines. Define magnetic flux and its unit.
Properties
  • Form closed loops (unlike electric field lines)
  • Outside magnet: N → S; Inside magnet: S → N
  • Never intersect each other
  • Tangent gives direction of B
  • Denser lines = stronger B
Magnetic Flux
φ = B·A cosθ Unit: Weber (Wb) = T·m²
Q.5
What is hysteresis in ferromagnetic materials? Define coercivity and retentivity.
Hysteresis
The lagging of magnetisation behind the magnetising field H during magnetisation and demagnetisation cycle. The B-H loop is called hysteresis loop.
Retentivity
Value of B when H = 0 (residual magnetism). Higher retentivity = better permanent magnet.
Coercivity
Value of H (reverse field) needed to reduce B to zero. Higher coercivity = harder to demagnetise.
Applications
Permanent magnets (high retentivity): iron, alnico. Electromagnets (low coercivity): soft iron (easy magnetise/demagnetise).
Q.6
Define bar magnet as a magnetic dipole. Derive B on axial and equatorial positions.
Magnetic Dipole
Bar magnet = two poles ±m separated by 2l. Dipole moment: M = m × 2l A·m², direction S to N.
Axial B
B_axial = μ₀·2Mr/[4π(r²−l²)²] ≈ μ₀·2M/(4πr³) for r>>l, along dipole axis
Equatorial B
B_eq = μ₀·M/[4π(r²+l²)^(3/2)] ≈ μ₀·M/(4πr³) for r>>l, antiparallel to dipole
Note
B_axial = 2 × B_equatorial at same distance r >> l
∴ B_axial = 2B_equatorial (for r >> l)
