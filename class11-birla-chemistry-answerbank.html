Chapter 1 — Basic Concepts of Chemistry (Q. 1–12)
Q1
Write the relations used to convert temperature in Celsius into Kelvin and Kelvin into Celsius.
K = °C + 273.15   (Celsius → Kelvin)
°C = K − 273.15   (Kelvin → Celsius)
  • 0°C = 273.15 K (freezing point of water)
  • 100°C = 373.15 K (boiling point of water)
  • Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15°C
Q2
State the law of conservation of mass, Avogadro's law, and the law of definite proportions.
  • Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier): In a chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants = total mass of products. Matter cannot be created or destroyed.
  • Avogadro's Law: Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal number of molecules. Also: 1 mole of any substance contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number).
  • Law of Definite Proportions (Proust): A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of its source or preparation method. E.g., H₂O always has H:O = 1:8 by mass.
Q3
Define atomic mass unit, atomic mass and average atomic mass.
  • Atomic Mass Unit (u or amu): 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g. It is the unit used to express atomic and molecular masses.
  • Atomic Mass: The relative mass of one atom of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of carbon-12. E.g., H = 1u, O = 16u, Fe = 56u.
  • Average Atomic Mass: The weighted average of the atomic masses of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, taking into account their percentage abundance. Average atomic mass = Σ (isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Q5
Define mole, molar mass and molar volume.
  • Mole: The amount of a substance containing as many elementary particles (atoms, molecules, ions) as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles.
  • Molar Mass: The mass of one mole of a substance expressed in grams per mole (g/mol). It is numerically equal to the molecular/atomic mass in u. E.g., molar mass of H₂O = 18 g/mol.
  • Molar Volume: The volume occupied by one mole of any gas at STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure: 0°C, 1 atm). Molar volume = 22.4 L/mol at STP

Chapter 3 — Some Basic Concepts in Analytical Chemistry (Q. 13–22)
Q13
What is stoichiometry? Explain its importance in chemical calculations.
Stoichiometry is the study of the quantitative relationships between reactants and products in a chemical reaction, based on the balanced chemical equation.
  • It tells us the exact mole ratios of reactants and products.
  • Importance:
    • Predicts the amount of product formed from given reactants.
    • Identifies the limiting reagent and the excess reagent.
    • Calculates percent yield and theoretical yield.
    • Used in industrial processes to minimise waste and cost.
Q14
Define mass percentage, volume percentage and mole fraction.
  • Mass percentage (w/w%): Mass of solute per 100 g of solution. w/w% = (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 100
  • Volume percentage (v/v%): Volume of solute per 100 mL of solution. v/v% = (volume of solute / volume of solution) × 100
  • Mole fraction (X): Ratio of moles of one component to total moles of all components. Xₐ = nₐ / (nₐ + n_b + ...) Mole fractions are dimensionless; sum = 1.
Q15
Define molarity and molality. State one difference between them.
  • Molarity (M): Number of moles of solute per litre of solution. M = n / V(L) Unit: mol L⁻¹ or M
  • Molality (m): Number of moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. m = n / W_solvent(kg) Unit: mol kg⁻¹
  • Difference: Molarity depends on volume of solution (changes with temperature), while molality depends on mass of solvent (temperature-independent). Molality is preferred for accurate work.
Q16
What is a limiting reagent?
Limiting reagent (or limiting reactant) is the reactant that is completely consumed first in a chemical reaction, thereby limiting the amount of product that can be formed. Once the limiting reagent is used up, the reaction stops, even if other reactants are still present.
  • The other reactant present in excess is called the excess reagent.
  • To identify: calculate moles of each reactant and compare with stoichiometric ratio. Whichever runs out first is the limiting reagent.

Q21
For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, identify the limiting reagent when 4 g of H₂ reacts with 10 g of O₂. Give a reason. (H=1, O=16)
Moles of H₂ = 4/2 = 2 mol
Moles of O₂ = 10/32 = 0.3125 mol
Stoichiometry: 2H₂ : 1O₂ → ratio = 2:1
O₂ needed for 2 mol H₂ = 2/2 = 1 mol O₂ required
But only 0.3125 mol O₂ available (less than 1 mol needed)
∴ O₂ is the limiting reagent (it is completely consumed first)
H₂O formed = 2 × 0.3125 = 0.625 mol = 0.625 × 18 = 11.25 g

For 2 mol N₂: NH₃ = 2 × 2 = 4 mol
4 moles of NH₃ are formed (= 4 × 17 = 68 g)

Chapter 4 — Structure of Atom (Q. 23–30)
Q23
Write the drawbacks of Rutherford's atomic model.
  • 1. Could not explain stability of atom: According to classical electrodynamics, an accelerating electron should continuously emit radiation and lose energy → spiral into nucleus → atom should collapse in ~10⁻⁸ s. But atoms are stable.
  • 2. Could not explain line spectra: If electrons spiralled inward continuously, they would emit continuous spectrum. But atoms emit discrete line spectra — Rutherford's model had no explanation for this.
  • 3. No information about electron arrangement: It did not specify how electrons were arranged or distributed around the nucleus.
Q24
Write the postulates of Bohr's atomic model and state two drawbacks.
Postulates of Bohr's Model:
  • 1. Electrons revolve around nucleus in fixed circular paths (orbits/shells) without emitting energy.
  • 2. Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/2π
  • 3. Energy is absorbed/emitted only when electron jumps between orbits: ΔE = hν
  • 4. Orbit with n=1 has lowest energy (ground state).
Drawbacks:
  • 1. Cannot explain spectra of multi-electron atoms (only works for hydrogen-like atoms).
  • 2. Cannot explain fine structure of spectral lines (Zeeman effect, Stark effect).
  • 3. Does not incorporate de Broglie's wave nature of electrons or Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.
Q25
State and explain Pauli's exclusion principle, Aufbau principle and Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity.
  • Aufbau Principle ("building up"): Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy. Lower energy orbitals are filled first. Order: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p... Rule: n+l rule — lower (n+l) filled first; if equal, lower n filled first.
  • Pauli's Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in the same atom can have identical values for all four quantum numbers (n, l, m, s). Each orbital holds max 2 electrons with opposite spins (↑↓).
  • Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: In a set of degenerate (equal energy) orbitals, electrons first occupy separate orbitals with the same spin (parallel ↑↑) before pairing. This minimises electron-electron repulsion and gives the atom maximum stability.

Q27
Draw the shapes of s, px, py and pz orbitals.
  • s orbital: Spherically symmetric (sphere) around nucleus. Non-directional. One s orbital per subshell.
  • p orbitals: Dumbbell shaped — two lobes on either side of the nucleus along the respective axis.
    • pₓ: lobes along x-axis
    • p_y: lobes along y-axis
    • p_z: lobes along z-axis
  • Three p orbitals are mutually perpendicular. All have a node at the nucleus.

Chapter 13 — Nuclear Chemistry (Q. 31–34)
Q31
Define isotopes, isobars and isotones with one example of each.
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A). They have the same number of protons but different neutrons.
    Example: ¹H (protium), ²H (deuterium), ³H (tritium) — all hydrogen (Z=1).
  • Isobars: Atoms of different elements with the same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z).
    Example: ⁴⁰Ar (Z=18) and ⁴⁰Ca (Z=20) — same A=40.
  • Isotones: Atoms of different elements with the same number of neutrons but different atomic numbers and mass numbers.
    Example: ¹⁴C (Z=6, N=8) and ¹⁵N (Z=7, N=8) — both have 8 neutrons.
Q32
What are nuclear magic numbers? List the commonly accepted magic numbers.
Nuclear Magic Numbers: Certain numbers of protons or neutrons that confer extra stability to a nucleus (analogous to closed electron shells in chemistry). Nuclei with magic numbers of protons or neutrons are exceptionally stable.
  • Magic numbers: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126
  • Examples of "doubly magic" nuclei (magic Z and N): ⁴He (Z=2, N=2), ¹⁶O (Z=8, N=8), ⁴⁰Ca (Z=20, N=20), ²⁰⁸Pb (Z=82, N=126)
  • These nuclei have higher binding energy per nucleon and greater stability.
Q33
What are nuclear magic nuclei? Give one example.
Nuclei with protons or neutrons equal to a magic number (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) are called magic nuclei. Nuclei with both proton and neutron numbers equal to magic numbers are called doubly magic nuclei.
  • Example: ²⁰⁸Pb (Lead) — Z=82 (magic), N=126 (magic) → doubly magic nucleus. It is the heaviest stable nuclide.
  • Other example: ⁴He — Z=2, N=2 (doubly magic) — extremely stable (used as alpha particle).
Q34
State the rate law.
Rate Law: For a reaction aA + bB → products, the rate law states that the rate of reaction is proportional to the product of concentrations of reactants, each raised to the power of their respective experimentally determined order (not necessarily the stoichiometric coefficient): Rate = k[A]ˣ[B]^y where:
  • k = rate constant (depends on temperature, not concentration)
  • x = order with respect to A; y = order with respect to B
  • Overall order = x + y
  • The order must be determined experimentally (cannot be deduced from the balanced equation, except for elementary reactions).

Q37
Write characteristics of Homologous series (Any four).
A homologous series is a group of organic compounds with the same functional group and general formula, where each successive member differs by a CH₂ group (14 u).
  • 1. All members have the same general formula (e.g., alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂).
  • 2. Successive members differ by –CH₂– (14 u).
  • 3. Members show a gradual change in physical properties (bp, mp, density increase with molecular mass).
  • 4. All members have similar chemical properties due to same functional group.
  • 5. They can be prepared by similar methods.
  • 6. Molecular formulae of consecutive members differ by CH₂.
Q38
Explain primary, secondary and tertiary carbon atoms with examples.
Carbon atoms in organic compounds are classified based on the number of other carbon atoms directly attached:
  • Primary (1°) carbon: Attached to only ONE other carbon. E.g., the CH₃ groups in propane (CH₃–CH₂–CH₃).
  • Secondary (2°) carbon: Attached to TWO other carbon atoms. E.g., the middle carbon in propane (–CH₂–).
  • Tertiary (3°) carbon: Attached to THREE other carbon atoms. E.g., the central C in isobutane CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃.
  • Quaternary (4°) carbon: Attached to FOUR other carbon atoms. E.g., the central C in neopentane C(CH₃)₄ — no H attached.
Q39
What is isomerism? Explain chain isomerism with a suitable example.
Isomerism: The phenomenon in which two or more compounds have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae (arrangement of atoms) is called isomerism. The compounds are called isomers.

Chain Isomerism (Skeletal isomerism): Isomers that differ in the arrangement of the carbon skeleton (chain). Same molecular formula, different branching of carbon chain.
  • Example: C₅H₁₂ (pentane) has three chain isomers:
  • n-pentane: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (straight chain)
  • Isopentane (2-methylbutane): CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃
  • Neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane): C(CH₃)₄

Q41
What are electrophiles and nucleophiles? Give one example of each.
  • Electrophile ("electron lover"): An electron-deficient species that accepts an electron pair from a nucleophile. It seeks electrons to form a bond.
    Examples: H⁺, Cl⁺, NO₂⁺, AlCl₃, BF₃, carbocations (R⁺)
  • Nucleophile ("nucleus lover"): An electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to an electrophile. It seeks a positive centre to attack.
    Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O, RNH₂, :CH₂⁻ (carbanion)
  • In a reaction: Nucleophile attacks the electrophile → new bond formed using the nucleophile's lone pair.
Q42
Explain the inductive effect and resonance effect briefly with a suitable example.
Inductive Effect:
  • The permanent displacement of electrons along a chain of carbon atoms due to the presence of an electronegative/electropositive atom or group is called the inductive effect.
  • –I effect (electron withdrawing): –Cl, –Br, –NO₂, –CN pull electrons toward themselves.
  • +I effect (electron donating): alkyl groups (–CH₃) push electrons away.
  • Example: In CH₃–CH₂–Cl, Cl pulls electron density from C1 → C1 is slightly δ⁺.
  • Effect decreases with distance and is transmitted through σ bonds.
Resonance Effect (Mesomeric Effect):
  • The delocalisation of π electrons or lone pairs over a conjugated system is called the resonance effect.
  • +M effect: groups that donate electrons into the ring (–OH, –NH₂, –OR).
  • –M effect: groups that withdraw electrons from the ring (–NO₂, –CHO, –COOH).
  • Example: In phenol (C₆H₅–OH), the lone pair of O is delocalised into the benzene ring (+M effect), increasing electron density at ortho and para positions.

Chapter 15 — Alkanes (Q. 44–46)
Q44
Write the general formula of alkanes and the molecular formula of an alkane containing six carbon atoms.
  • General formula of alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (saturated hydrocarbons, only C–C and C–H single bonds)
  • For n = 6 (hexane): C₆H₂(6)+2 = C₆H₁₄
  • Molecular formula of hexane = C₆H₁₄
  • Structure: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (n-hexane)
  • Other common alkanes: methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈), butane (C₄H₁₀), pentane (C₅H₁₂)
Q45
Describe following methods of preparation of alkanes and write suitable chemical equations: (i) From unsaturated hydrocarbons (ii) What is Grignard's reagent? Write Reaction of CH₃Br with Mg metal in presence of dry ether. (iii) Write Wurtz reaction for 2CH₃–CH₂–Cl + 2Na → dry ether → ?
(i) From Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Catalytic Hydrogenation):
  • Alkenes or alkynes react with hydrogen in presence of Ni/Pt/Pd catalyst (hydrogenation) to give alkanes.
  • CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → (Ni/Δ) → CH₃–CH₃ (ethylene → ethane)
  • CH≡CH + 2H₂ → (Ni/Δ) → CH₃–CH₃ (acetylene → ethane)
(ii) Grignard's Reagent:
  • Grignard's reagent is an organomagnesium halide (R–Mg–X) prepared by reacting an alkyl halide with magnesium metal in dry ether.
  • Reaction: CH₃Br + Mg → (dry ether) → CH₃MgBr (methyl magnesium bromide)
  • It is a very reactive reagent used in organic synthesis.
(iii) Wurtz Reaction:
  • Two molecules of alkyl halide react with sodium metal in dry ether to give a higher alkane.
  • 2CH₃–CH₂–Cl + 2Na → (dry ether) → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ + 2NaCl
  • 2 ethyl chloride + 2Na → butane + 2 sodium chloride
  • Used to prepare symmetric alkanes with even number of carbons.
Q46
State four uses of alkanes.
  • 1. Fuel: Methane (natural gas), propane and butane (LPG) are used as domestic and industrial fuels.
  • 2. Petrol and diesel: Pentane, hexane, heptane and octane are components of petrol used in motor vehicles.
  • 3. Solvents: Hexane and heptane are used as solvents in industrial processes (e.g., extraction of vegetable oils).
  • 4. Lubricants: Higher alkanes (paraffin wax) are used as lubricants, wax for candles, and coating for paper/food packaging.
  • 5. Raw material: Alkanes serve as starting material (feedstock) for manufacture of other chemicals like alcohols, alkyl halides, etc.
