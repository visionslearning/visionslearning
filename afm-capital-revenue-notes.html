Chapter: Capital and Revenue — Expenditure and Receipts

Capital Expenditure Revenue Expenditure Deferred Revenue Capital Receipts Revenue Receipts Comparison Profits & Losses Wrong Treatment Quick Q&A
💡 Golden Rule: Ask yourself — Does this expenditure/receipt bring a long-term asset or future benefit? → Capital. Is it routine, recurring, and for running the business? → Revenue.
💰

1. Capital Expenditure

▼
Definition: Capital expenditure means an expenditure carrying probable future benefits (Guidance Note – ICAI). Eric Kohler defined it as "an expenditure intended to benefit future periods; an addition to fixed assets. The term is generally restricted to expenditures that add fixed asset units or that have the effect of increasing the capacity, efficiency, life span or economy of operation of an existing asset."

Characteristics / Tests

1. Long-term Benefits

Benefits can be enjoyed for a number of years.

2. Investing Activity

Helps to set up and develop a business; creates and increases earning capacity.

3. Recoverable

Money spent can be recovered through income generated by the asset or by selling it.

4. Non-recurring

Need not be incurred again and again.

5. Effect on Funds & Profits

Decreases funds in current year but may increase profits and funds in future.

6. Disclosure in Final A/c

Shown as Assets in Balance Sheet.

Examples of Capital Expenditure

  • Purchase of land, building, machinery, furniture
  • Cost of addition or extension to an existing asset (inseparable part)
  • Investments — shares, securities, debentures, immovable properties
  • Cost of acquisition of an intangible asset — goodwill, patents, trade-mark, copyrights
  • Carriage, installation charges, legal fees paid while purchasing a fixed asset
  • Wages paid for erection of new machinery
  • Cost of acquiring and development of a wasting asset (mine, oil-well, tea estate)
📊

2. Revenue Expenditure

▼
Definition: Revenue expenditure means an expenditure from which no future benefit is expected (Guidance Note – ICAI). Eric Kohler defined it as "an expenditure charged against operation; a term used to contrast with capital expenditure." Items of expenditure having immediate or short-term benefits (less than 1 year) are treated as revenue expenditure.

Characteristics / Tests

1. Business Activity

Pertains to business activity; helps to run a business and earn income.

2. Maintain Asset

Helps to maintain an asset in working condition (e.g. repairs).

3. Not Recoverable

Money spent is irretrievably gone; expired costs and revenue losses are treated as revenue expenditure.

4. Recurring

Same type of expenditure is incurred again and again (e.g. purchase of goods, payment of rent).

5. Reduces Funds & Profits

As money goes out irretrievably, it reduces both funds and profits of the current year.

Disclosure in Final Accounts

  • Current year → shown as expenses in Profit & Loss Account
  • Future period (prepaid) → shown as Asset (pre-paid expenses) in Balance Sheet
  • Incurred but not yet paid → shown as Liability (outstanding expenses) in Balance Sheet

Examples of Revenue Expenditure

  • Cost of production — wages, factory expenses, power
  • Cost of administration — salaries, rent, stationery
  • Cost of selling and distribution — travelling, freight outward
  • Cost of finance — interest on loans
  • Repairs to keep machinery in working condition
  • Excise duty paid on manufacture of goods
  • Payment of MVAT, annual fees, audit fees
⏳

3. Deferred Revenue Expenditure

▼
Definition: Deferred Revenue Expenditure is that expenditure which is carried forward on the presumption that it will be of benefit over subsequent period(s) (Guidance Note – ICAI). It is also known as 'deferred expenditure'. It has a mixed nature with features of both revenue and capital expenditure.

Key Points

  • Items with medium-term benefits (say 3 years) are treated as deferred revenue expenditure.
  • Proportionate cost (1/3) related to current year is charged as expenses in P&L.
  • Balance (unexpired) cost (2/3) is carried forward as 'fictitious asset' in the Balance Sheet.
  • Gradual and systematic writing off is called amortisation (Guidance Note – ICAI).

Examples (as per ICAI Guidance Note on Revised Schedule VI)

  • Share issue expenses
  • Discount on issue of Shares
  • Debenture issue expenses
  • Discount on issue of Debentures
  • Premium payable on redemption of Debentures
📝 Tutorial Note: As per Accounting Standard 26, items like research expenses, heavy advertisement expenditure, preliminary expenses, and expenses on shifting the business are now treated as revenue expenses, not deferred revenue expenditure.
🏦

4. Capital Receipts

▼
Definition: Capital Receipt means an amount received by a concern in the course of its financing activity (obtaining money as capital or loan or sale of assets). A concern needs money to carry on its business activities and investment activities (purchase of assets or investments).

Characteristics / Tests

1. Financing Activity

Receipt arising out of financing activity; provides funds to acquire new assets.

2. Returnable

Money received as Capital or Loan has to be paid back.

3. Non-recurring

Such amounts are not received regularly or again and again.

4. Effect on Funds & Profits

Increases funds in current year but may decrease profits in future due to interest payments.

Disclosure in Final Accounts

  • Capital receipt is shown as Liability in Balance Sheet.
  • Capital receipt from sale of asset or investment is deducted from the concerned fixed asset on the Assets side.

Examples of Capital Receipts

  • Cash brought in by the proprietor
  • Loan received from the Bank
  • Money received on issue of shares or debentures
  • Premium on issue of shares
  • Amount received on sale of old machinery
  • Amount received on sale of shares held as investment
  • Insurance compensation for machinery destroyed in fire
💵

5. Revenue Receipts

▼
Definition: Revenue receipts means the receipts from customers for sale of goods, for services given, or for use of funds (loans, capital) or use of assets (patents, know-how) (Guidance Note – ICAI). Revenue receipts are recurring in nature and arise from past capital expenditure.

Characteristics / Tests

1. Business Activity

Receipt in the course of business activity (e.g. sale of goods, fees received).

2. Recurring

Same type of receipt is received again and again on a regular basis.

3. Not Returnable

Money received as revenue is not refundable. Goods or services have been supplied against it.

4. Increases Funds & Profits

Increases both funds and profits of the current year.

Disclosure in Final Accounts

  • Current year → shown as income in Profit & Loss Account
  • Received now but pertaining to future period → shown as Liability (advance income) in Balance Sheet
  • Earned in current year but not yet received → shown as Asset (income receivable) in Balance Sheet

Examples of Revenue Receipts

  • Sale of goods
  • Fees received for rendering services
  • Interest on loans given
  • Dividend on shares
  • Royalty for use of patents, know-how
  • Insurance claims for loss of current assets (e.g. stock destroyed in fire)
  • Recoveries and refunds (e.g. recovery of bad debt written off)
  • Rent received from letting out business property

7. Revenue Profits & Capital Profits / Losses

▼

Profits

  • Revenue / Operating Profit: Net profit arising from the normal operations and activities of a concern. Net profit = excess of revenue over expenses during a particular period.
  • Capital Profit: Arises when a capital asset is sold at a price higher than its cost (e.g. shares issued at premium — share premium is capital profit).
    • Capital profits on sale of assets → credited to P&L account as extraordinary income.
    • Capital profits related to shares (share premium) → credited to Capital Reserve, not P&L.

Losses

  • Revenue Loss: Loss in the course of business (e.g. loss of goods due to fire, theft, bad debts). Written off by debit to P&L account.
  • Capital Loss: Loss in the course of investing and finance activities (e.g. sale of fixed asset at a loss, issue of shares at discount).
    • Capital losses related to assets/investments → debited to P&L (expired cost).
    • Capital losses related to shares (discount on shares) → carried forward as 'Fictitious assets' in Balance Sheet.
⚠️

8. Effect of Wrong Treatment on Profits

▼

If an item of revenue is wrongly recorded as capital or vice versa, the profit and the assets or liabilities are shown at a wrong amount.

❌ Case 1: Revenue Exp → shown as Capital Exp
  • P&L shows lower expenses → higher profits
  • Balance Sheet shows higher assets
❌ Case 2: Capital Exp → shown as Revenue Exp
  • P&L shows lower profits
  • Balance Sheet shows lower assets
❌ Case 3: Revenue Receipt → shown as Capital Receipt
  • P&L shows lower profits
  • Balance Sheet shows higher liabilities
❌ Case 4: Capital Receipt → shown as Revenue Receipt
  • P&L shows higher profits
  • Balance Sheet shows lower liabilities
