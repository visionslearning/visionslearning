Chapter 1: Unit and Measurement (1.1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.7, 1.8, 1.8.1, 1.9)
1.1
What is a physical quantity? Distinguish between fundamental and derived quantities.
Physical Qty
Any quantity that can be measured is called a physical quantity. e.g. length, mass, force, temperature.
Fundamental
Independent quantities not derived from others. 7 SI fundamental quantities: Length (m), Mass (kg), Time (s), Temperature (K), Current (A), Luminous intensity (cd), Amount of substance (mol).
Derived
Expressed in terms of fundamental quantities. Examples: velocity (m/s), force (kg·m/s²), energy (kg·m²/s²).
1.2
Define SI system. Name the 7 fundamental units with their symbols.
SI System
The International System of Units (Système International d'Unités) is the modern standard for measurement, used worldwide in science and technology.
7 Units
  • Length — metre (m)
  • Mass — kilogram (kg)
  • Time — second (s)
  • Electric current — ampere (A)
  • Temperature — kelvin (K)
  • Luminous intensity — candela (cd)
  • Amount of substance — mole (mol)
1.5
Define dimensions. State the principle of homogeneity and its use in checking correctness of equations.
Dimensions
Dimensions are the powers to which fundamental quantities (M, L, T...) are raised to represent a physical quantity. e.g. Force = [M¹L¹T⁻²]
Principle of Homogeneity
An equation is dimensionally correct only if the dimensions on both sides (LHS = RHS) are the same.
Use
Check correctness of physical formulas. e.g. v = u + at: [LT⁻¹] = [LT⁻¹] + [LT⁻²][T] = [LT⁻¹] ✓
1.7
Show that 1 newton = 10⁵ dyne and 1 joule = 10⁷ erg.
1 N = 10⁵ dyne
Force: [M¹L¹T⁻²]. 1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻² = 1000 g × 100 cm × s⁻² = 10⁵ g·cm·s⁻² = 10⁵ dyne ✓
1 J = 10⁷ erg
Energy: [M¹L²T⁻²]. 1 J = 1 kg·m²·s⁻² = 1000 g × (100)² cm² × s⁻² = 1000 × 10000 erg = 10⁷ erg ✓
∴
1 N = 10⁵ dyne;   1 J = 10⁷ erg
1.8
State the applications and limitations of dimensional analysis.
Applications
  • Check correctness of a physical equation.
  • Derive a relation between physical quantities.
  • Convert units from one system to another (CGS ↔ SI).
  • Find dimensions of physical constants.
Limitations
  • Cannot determine dimensionless constants (2π, ½, etc.).
  • Cannot derive relations with more than 3 unknowns.
  • Cannot derive relations involving trigonometric/log/exponential functions.
  • Cannot tell if a quantity is scalar or vector.
1.8.1
What are significant figures? State the rules for counting them.
Definition
The significant figures in a number are the digits that carry meaning and contribute to its measurement accuracy.
Rules
  • All non-zero digits are significant. e.g. 3274 → 4 sig. figs.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. e.g. 3007 → 4 sig. figs.
  • Leading zeros are NOT significant. e.g. 0.0034 → 2 sig. figs.
  • Trailing zeros after decimal ARE significant. e.g. 3.200 → 4 sig. figs.
  • Trailing zeros before decimal MAY or MAY NOT be significant.
1.9
Define accuracy and precision. Define error — types of errors.
Accuracy
How close a measured value is to the true value.
Precision
How close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility).
Types of Error
  • Systematic error: Consistent error due to faulty instrument/method. (Instrumental, personal, environmental)
  • Random error: Unpredictable fluctuations; reduced by taking mean of many readings.
  • Gross error: Due to carelessness or mistakes in recording.
  • Absolute error: |True value − Measured value|
  • Relative error: Absolute error / Mean value
  • Percentage error: Relative error × 100

Chapter 2: Mathematical Methods (2.1–2.5.2)
2.1
Define scalar and vector quantities. Give two examples each.
Scalar
Quantity with magnitude only. e.g. mass, temperature, speed, energy, distance.
Vector
Quantity with both magnitude and direction, obeys vector addition laws. e.g. displacement, velocity, force, acceleration, momentum.
2.2
State and explain the Triangle law of vector addition. Find the magnitude and direction of resultant.
Statement
If two vectors are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in order, then the third side taken in opposite order represents their resultant in magnitude and direction.
Magnitude
R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cosθ)
Direction
tan α = B sinθ / (A + B cosθ)   (α = angle R makes with A)
Special cases
θ=0°: R=A+B (max);   θ=90°: R=√(A²+B²);   θ=180°: R=|A−B| (min)
2.3
State the Parallelogram law of vector addition and derive the expression for magnitude and direction of resultant.
Statement
If two vectors are represented by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram drawn from a common point, the diagonal from that point gives their resultant in magnitude and direction.
Derivation
Let A and B make angle θ. Diagonal = R. Draw perpendicular from tip of B to extended A.
  • Component of B along A = B cosθ
  • Component of B ⊥ to A = B sinθ
  • R² = (A + B cosθ)² + (B sinθ)² = A²+2AB cosθ+B²
Result
R = √(A²+B²+2AB cosθ)  ;  tan α = B sinθ/(A+B cosθ)
∴ R
= √(A²+B²+2AB cosθ)
2.4
Explain resolution of a vector into components.
Definition
Splitting a single vector into two (or more) component vectors along chosen directions is called resolution of vector.
2D Resolution
Vector A at angle θ to x-axis: Aₓ = A cosθ (x-component), Aᵧ = A sinθ (y-component)
Reconstructing
A = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)  ;  θ = tan⁻¹(Aᵧ/Aₓ)
3D unit vectors
A⃗ = Aₓ î + Aᵧ ĵ + A_z k̂
2.5
Define scalar (dot) product and vector (cross) product. State characteristics of each.
Scalar Product
A·B = AB cosθ — scalar result.
Characteristics
Commutative (A·B=B·A); Distributive; A·B=0 if ⊥; A·A=A²; î·î=1, î·ĵ=0
Vector Product
A×B = AB sinθ n̂ — vector result, ⊥ to plane of A and B.
Characteristics
Anti-commutative (A×B = −B×A); A×A=0; A×B=0 if ∥; î×ĵ=k̂, ĵ×k̂=î, k̂×î=ĵ
Applications
Dot: Work (W=F·d), Power (P=F·v). Cross: Torque (τ=r×F), Angular momentum (L=r×p).
2.5.1
Express cross product in determinant form.
Determinant form
If A = Aₓî+Aᵧĵ+A_zk̂ and B = Bₓî+Bᵧĵ+B_zk̂:
A×B = |î   ĵ   k̂|
       |Aₓ Aᵧ A_z|
       |Bₓ Bᵧ B_z|
Expanding
= î(AᵧB_z−A_zBᵧ) − ĵ(AₓB_z−A_zBₓ) + k̂(AₓBᵧ−AᵧBₓ)
2.5.2
Prove that the magnitude of vector product equals the area of a parallelogram.
Proof
Consider parallelogram with adjacent sides A and B, angle θ between them.
Step 1
Area = base × height = A × B sinθ
Step 2
|A×B| = AB sinθ
Step 3
Area = AB sinθ = |A×B| ✓
∴ |A×B|
= Area of parallelogram formed by A and B.

Chapter 10: Electrostatics (10.1–10.10)
10.1
State and explain Coulomb's law of electrostatics. Write its vector form.
Statement
The electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of magnitudes of charges and inversely proportional to square of distance between them, along the line joining them.
Scalar
F = kq₁q₂/r² = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r²)   k = 9×10⁹ N·m²/C²
Vector form
F⃗₁₂ = kq₁q₂/r² · r̂₁₂ (repulsive for like charges, attractive for unlike)
Note
ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m² (permittivity of free space)
10.2
Define electric field intensity. Derive expression for E due to a point charge.
Definition
Force experienced by a unit positive test charge at that point. E = F/q₀   Unit: N/C or V/m
Derivation
  • Force on q₀ at distance r from charge Q: F = kQq₀/r²
  • E = F/q₀ = kQ/r²
Result
E = kQ/r² = Q/(4πε₀r²)   Direction: away from +Q, towards −Q
10.3
State and explain the principle of superposition of electric forces.
Principle
The net force on a charge due to a system of charges is the vector sum of individual forces due to each charge separately. The presence of other charges does not affect the individual force.
Mathematically
F⃗_total = F⃗₁ + F⃗₂ + F⃗₃ + ... = Σ Fᵢ
10.4
Define electric lines of force and state their properties.
Definition
Imaginary lines in electric field such that the tangent at any point gives the direction of E at that point.
Properties
  • Start from +ve charge, end at −ve charge.
  • Never intersect each other.
  • Perpendicular to conductor surface.
  • Denser lines = stronger field.
  • Do not pass through conductor (E=0 inside).
  • In uniform field: parallel and equally spaced.
10.5
Define electric flux. State Gauss's theorem and its applications.
Electric Flux
φ = E·A cosθ — Total field lines through a surface. Unit: N·m²/C
Gauss's Theorem
Total electric flux through any closed (Gaussian) surface is equal to 1/ε₀ times the total charge enclosed. φ = q_enc/ε₀
Applications
  • E due to an infinite line charge: E = λ/(2πε₀r)
  • E due to infinite plane sheet: E = σ/(2ε₀)
  • E inside a conductor = 0
10.6
Define electric potential. Derive expression for potential due to a point charge.
Definition
Work done per unit positive charge to bring it from infinity to that point. V = W/q   Unit: Volt (V) = J/C
Derivation
W = ∫F·dr from ∞ to r = −∫kQ/r² dr = kQ/r
Result
V = kQ/r = Q/(4πε₀r)
Note
V is a scalar. Positive for +ve charge, negative for −ve charge.
10.6.1
Define equipotential surface and state its properties.
Definition
A surface on which all points are at the same electric potential is called an equipotential surface.
Properties
  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.
  • Electric field is always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.
  • Equipotential surfaces never intersect each other.
  • For a point charge: concentric spheres are equipotential surfaces.
  • Closer surfaces → stronger field.
10.6.3
Define capacitance and derive capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor.
Capacitance
Ability to store electric charge. C = Q/V   Unit: Farad (F) = C/V
Parallel Plate
Two plates, area A, separation d. E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A). V = E·d = Qd/(ε₀A)
Result
C = Q/V = ε₀A/d
With dielectric
C = Kε₀A/d where K = dielectric constant
∴ C
= ε₀A/d (for air/vacuum between plates)
10.9
Define electric dipole. Derive expression for E on axial and equatorial position.
Dipole
System of +q and −q separated by distance 2l. Dipole moment p = q·2l (C·m), direction: −q to +q.
Axial (End-on)
E_axial = 2kpr/(r²−l²)² ≈ 2kp/r³ (for r>>l) — along dipole direction
Equatorial (Broad-side)
E_eq = kp/(r²+l²)^(3/2) ≈ kp/r³ (for r>>l) — antiparallel to dipole
Note
E_axial = 2 × E_equatorial for same distance r >> l
10.10
Define linear, surface and volume charge density.
Linear λ
λ = dQ/dl — Charge per unit length. Unit: C/m. (wires, rods)
Surface σ
σ = dQ/dA — Charge per unit area. Unit: C/m². (sheets, conductors)
Volume ρ
ρ = dQ/dV — Charge per unit volume. Unit: C/m³. (charged spheres, clouds)

Chapter 11: Electric Current through Conductors (11.1–11.14)
11.1
Define electric current. State its SI unit. Define drift velocity.
Electric Current
Rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. I = dQ/dt   Unit: Ampere (A) = C/s
Drift Velocity
The average velocity with which free electrons move in a conductor under the influence of an applied electric field. v_d = eEτ/m where τ = relaxation time.
Note
Conventional current flows from + to −; electron flow is from − to +.
11.2
Derive the relation between electric current and drift velocity.
Setup
Conductor: cross-section A, n free electrons/m³, drift velocity v_d, charge of electron e.
Step 1
In time dt, volume swept = A·v_d·dt
Step 2
Number of electrons = n·A·v_d·dt
Step 3
Charge: dQ = n·A·v_d·dt·e
Step 4
I = dQ/dt = neAv_d
Result
I = neAv_d  ;  J = I/A = nev_d (current density)
∴ I = neAv_d
Current is proportional to drift velocity.
11.3
State and explain Ohm's law. Define resistance.
Ohm's Law
At constant temperature, current through a conductor is directly proportional to potential difference across its ends. V ∝ I → V = IR
Resistance
Opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current. R = V/I   Unit: Ohm (Ω)
Factors affecting R
R ∝ L (length); R ∝ 1/A (area); R depends on material and temperature. R = ρL/A
Limitation
Not valid for semiconductors, diodes, at high T or high V.
11.4
Define drift velocity. Prove that current density J = nev_d. [Definition of drift velocity as per syllabus]
Drift Velocity
Average velocity of free electrons in a conductor under applied electric field. Direction: opposite to E (for electrons). v_d = eEτ/m
Proof J = nev_d
I = neAv_d → J = I/A = nev_d ✓
Key fact
v_d is very small (≈10⁻⁴ m/s) but the electric signal travels at speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s).
11.5
Define resistivity and conductivity. State temperature effect on resistance (except physical origin).
Resistivity (ρ)
Resistance of unit length and unit cross-section conductor. ρ = RA/L   Unit: Ω·m
Conductivity (σ)
Reciprocal of resistivity. σ = 1/ρ   Unit: S/m
Temperature effect
R_T = R₀(1 + αT) where α = temperature coefficient.
  • Metals: α > 0 (R increases with T)
  • Semiconductors: α < 0 (R decreases with T)
  • Alloys (nichrome): α ≈ 0 (R almost constant)
11.6
Derive expressions for series and parallel combination of resistances.
Series
Same current I through all. V = V₁+V₂+V₃ = IR₁+IR₂+IR₃ = I(R₁+R₂+R₃)
R_series = R₁+R₂+R₃   (R increases)
Parallel
Same V across all. I = I₁+I₂+I₃ = V/R₁+V/R₂+V/R₃ = V(1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃)
1/R_p = 1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃   (R decreases)
Series: R↑;
Parallel: R↓
11.7
State and explain Kirchhoff's laws.
KCL (Junction Rule)
The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero. ΣI = 0. Currents entering = Currents leaving.
KVL (Loop Rule)
The algebraic sum of potential differences around any closed loop is zero. ΣV = 0. (Based on conservation of energy.)
Applications
Solving complex circuits with multiple sources and resistors. Used in Wheatstone bridge analysis.
11.8
Define EMF, terminal voltage and internal resistance.
EMF (ε)
Energy provided per unit charge by the cell (open circuit voltage). ε = W/Q   Unit: Volt
Terminal Voltage
Actual voltage available across terminals when current flows. V = ε − Ir
Internal Resistance (r)
Resistance offered by electrolyte inside the cell. ε = I(R+r)  →  r = (ε−V)/I
Note
V < ε when current drawn (discharging); V > ε when charging.
11.9
State Wheatstone bridge principle. Derive the condition for balance.
Principle
When no current flows through the galvanometer in a bridge circuit, the bridge is said to be balanced.
Balance condition
At balance, no current through G (I_g = 0). Using KVL:
  • P/Q = R/S
  • If P, Q, R known → S = QR/P (unknown resistance found)
Result
P/Q = R/S (balanced condition)
11.10
Explain the metre bridge (slide wire bridge) and how it is used to find unknown resistance.
Principle
Based on Wheatstone bridge. A 1-metre uniform wire of resistance wire (constantan/manganin) is used.
Working
  • Unknown resistance X on left gap, known R on right gap.
  • Balance point at length l found by jockey.
  • X/R = l/(100−l)
Result
X = R·l/(100−l)
11.11
Explain the potentiometer and its advantages over voltmeter.
Potentiometer
A device for accurate measurement of EMF/potential difference without drawing any current from the source (ideal voltmeter with infinite resistance).
Principle
At balance point: V ∝ l (length). Used to compare EMFs: ε₁/ε₂ = l₁/l₂
Advantages over voltmeter
  • Draws NO current at balance → no error from internal resistance.
  • More accurate — can measure up to microvolt range.
  • Can measure EMF, potential difference, internal resistance.
11.14
State and explain Joule's law of heating. Define electric power.
Joule's Law
Heat produced in a conductor is: directly proportional to I², directly proportional to R, and directly proportional to time t.
H = I²Rt
H = I²Rt = VIt = V²t/R   Unit: Joule (J)
Electric Power
Rate of electrical energy consumed. P = W/t = VI = I²R = V²/R   Unit: Watt (W)
Applications
Electric heater, bulb, iron, fuse (all work on Joule's heating effect).

Chapter 12: Magnetism (12.1–12.5, except magnetic maps & coasts)
12.1
Define magnetic field and state its SI unit. What is magnetic flux?
Magnetic Field (B)
Region around a magnet where magnetic force is experienced. A vector quantity. SI Unit: Tesla (T) = Wb/m²
Magnetic Flux (φ)
φ = B·A cosθ   Unit: Weber (Wb). Total magnetic field lines through a surface.
12.2
State the properties of magnetic field lines.
Properties
  • Form closed loops — unlike electric field lines, they have no start or end.
  • Outside magnet: N→S; Inside magnet: S→N.
  • Never intersect each other.
  • Tangent gives direction of B at that point.
  • Denser lines = stronger magnetic field.
  • Parallel and equidistant lines = uniform field.
12.3
Define magnetisation, magnetic intensity and permeability. State relation between B, H and M.
Magnetisation (M)
Magnetic moment per unit volume of a magnetised material. Unit: A/m
Magnetic Intensity (H)
The capacity of an external magnetic field to magnetise a material. H = B/μ₀ − M Unit: A/m
Permeability (μ)
Ability of material to allow magnetic field lines through it. μ = B/H   μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A (free space)
Relation
B = μ₀(H + M) = μ₀μᵣH
12.3.1
Classify magnetic materials — dia, para and ferromagnetic. State their properties.
Diamagnetic
Weakly repelled by magnets. χ < 0 (small negative susceptibility). μᵣ slightly < 1. Examples: Bismuth, copper, gold, water.
Paramagnetic
Weakly attracted by magnets. χ > 0 (small positive susceptibility). μᵣ slightly > 1. Examples: Aluminium, oxygen, platinum.
Ferromagnetic
Strongly attracted. χ >> 0 (large positive). μᵣ >> 1. Retain magnetism (hysteresis). Examples: Iron, nickel, cobalt.
12.3.2
What is magnetic susceptibility? Define Curie's law.
Magnetic Susceptibility (χ)
The ratio of magnetisation to magnetic intensity. χ = M/H — dimensionless. Measures how easily a material gets magnetised.
Curie's Law
For paramagnetic materials, magnetisation is inversely proportional to absolute temperature: χ = C/T where C = Curie constant.
Note
Above Curie temperature, ferromagnetic becomes paramagnetic.
12.5
What is a bar magnet as a magnetic dipole? Define magnetic dipole moment.
Bar Magnet as Dipole
A bar magnet behaves like a magnetic dipole — two equal and opposite magnetic poles (N and S) separated by distance 2l.
Magnetic Dipole Moment (m)
m = pole strength × 2l   Unit: A·m² or J/T. Direction: S to N inside magnet.
B due to bar magnet
Axial: B = μ₀·2m/(4π·r³)  ; Equatorial: B = μ₀·m/(4π·r³)

Chapter 8: Sound (8.1–8.5.2)
8.1
What is a wave? Define progressive wave and its characteristics.
Wave
Disturbance that propagates through a medium transferring energy from one place to another without net transfer of matter.
Progressive Wave
A wave that continuously travels through a medium transporting energy. Equation: y = A sin(kx−ωt)
Characteristics
  • Amplitude (A): max displacement
  • Wavelength (λ): distance between two consecutive crests/troughs
  • Frequency (n): number of oscillations per second. Unit: Hz
  • Time period (T = 1/n)
  • Wave velocity: v = nλ
8.2
Distinguish between transverse and longitudinal waves.
Transverse
Particles vibrate perpendicular to wave direction. Form crests and troughs. Travel in solids and liquid surfaces. e.g. Light, string waves.
Longitudinal
Particles vibrate parallel to wave direction. Form compressions and rarefactions. Travel in all media (solid, liquid, gas). e.g. Sound waves.
8.3
Explain the principle of superposition of waves. Define interference.
Superposition
When two or more waves overlap at a point, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of individual displacements: y = y₁ + y₂
Interference
  • Constructive: Crests meet crests → amplitude increases. (same phase, path diff = nλ)
  • Destructive: Crest meets trough → amplitude decreases/cancels. (path diff = (2n−1)λ/2)
8.5
State Newton's formula for velocity of sound. Explain Laplace's correction.
Newton's Formula
Assumed isothermal process. v = √(P/ρ) At STP: v = 280 m/s (incorrect).
Limitation
Experimental value = 332 m/s. Newton's formula underestimates — isothermal assumption is wrong.
Laplace's Correction
Sound propagation is adiabatic (rapid, no heat exchange). Corrected formula: v = √(γP/ρ) where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 1.4 for air.
Result
v = √(1.4 × 1.013×10⁵/1.29) ≈ 332 m/s ✓
∴ v = √(γP/ρ)
≈ 332 m/s at STP
8.5.1
Explain the effect of temperature, pressure and humidity on velocity of sound.
Temperature
v ∝ √T. As temperature increases, velocity increases. For every 1°C rise: v increases by 0.61 m/s. v_T = v₀√(T/273)
Pressure
At constant temperature, velocity is independent of pressure. (When P increases, ρ also increases proportionally — P/ρ stays constant)
Humidity
Moist air (H₂O, M=18) is less dense than dry air (M=29). v ∝ 1/√ρ → velocity is greater in moist/humid air than in dry air.
8.5.2
What are stationary waves? How are they formed? State their properties.
Stationary Waves
Waves formed by superposition of two identical progressive waves travelling in opposite directions. They do not transport energy.
Formation
When an incident wave and its reflected wave superpose, nodes (zero displacement) and antinodes (maximum displacement) are formed.
Properties
  • Nodes: points of zero amplitude. Distance between two nodes = λ/2.
  • Antinodes: points of maximum amplitude. Distance between two antinodes = λ/2.
  • All particles between two nodes vibrate in phase.
  • No energy is transferred in stationary waves.
  • Distance between adjacent node and antinode = λ/4.
