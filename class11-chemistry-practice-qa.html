⚖️ Q.1 — State and Explain the Laws
Q.1aLaw of Definite Proportion (Law of Constant Composition)
Answer Statement: A chemical compound always contains the same elements combined together in the same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation. Proposed by: Joseph Louis Proust (1799) Explanation: • Every pure sample of a given compound contains the same elements in the same mass ratio. • The ratio does not depend on the source of the compound, method of preparation, or amount taken. Example — Water (H₂O): • Water always contains H and O in the mass ratio 2 : 16 = 1 : 8 by mass. • Whether water is obtained from rain, river, electrolysis, or burning of H₂ — ratio is always H : O = 1 : 8. Example — CO₂: • CO₂ always has C : O = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8 by mass. Significance: • Confirms that elements combine in fixed amounts to form compounds. • Basis for calculating molecular formulas and stoichiometry. Note: This law holds for all pure compounds. Exceptions occur in non-stoichiometric compounds (Berthollides) like FeO (ratio varies).
Q.1bLaw of Conservation of Mass
Answer Statement: Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. Proposed by: Antoine Lavoisier (1774) — "Father of Modern Chemistry" Explanation: • In any chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed — they are only rearranged. • Since all atoms present before the reaction are still present after the reaction, total mass is conserved. Example: Mg + O₂ → MgO 24g + 16g = 40g (before) = 40g (after) ✓ H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl 2g + 71g = 73g = 2×36.5g = 73g ✓ Lavoisier's Experiment: • Heated mercury in a sealed vessel. Mass of Hg + O₂ consumed = mass of HgO formed. • Verified: mass before = mass after reaction. Modern note: E = mc² shows mass-energy equivalence in nuclear reactions, but for chemical reactions the law holds perfectly.
Q.1cAvogadro's Law
Answer Statement: Equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal number of molecules. Proposed by: Amedeo Avogadro (1811) Mathematical form: V ∝ n   (at constant T and P, where n = number of moles) V/n = constant   OR   V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂ Explanation with example: H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g) 1 vol + 1 vol = 2 vol (1 mol) (1 mol) (2 mol) Equal volumes = equal molecules Avogadro's Number (Nₐ): One mole of any substance contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number). At STP (0°C, 1 atm): 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L (molar volume). Deductions from Avogadro's law: 1. Atomicity of gases — H₂, O₂, N₂ are diatomic (not monoatomic). 2. Molar mass of gas = 2 × vapour density. 3. Relationship between volumes in gaseous reactions.

📖 Q.4 — Define the Terms
Q.4Define: Molarity, Normality, Mole fraction, Crystallization, 1 amu, Molality, Formula mass, Wavelength
Answer 1) Molarity (M): Number of moles of solute dissolved per litre (1000 mL) of solution. M = (Moles of solute) / (Volume of solution in L) = n/V Unit: mol/L or mol dm⁻³ (M). Temperature dependent (volume changes with T). Example: 2M HCl = 2 mol HCl dissolved in 1 litre of solution. 2) Normality (N): Number of gram equivalents of solute dissolved per litre of solution. N = (Gram equivalents of solute) / (Volume of solution in L) Gram equivalent = molar mass / n-factor (valency factor) N = M × n-factor. Unit: eq/L or N. Example: 1N H₂SO₄ = 1 gram equivalent (49 g) of H₂SO₄ per litre. 3) Mole Fraction (x): The ratio of the number of moles of one component to the total number of moles of all components in the solution. x_A = n_A / (n_A + n_B)   and   x_B = n_B / (n_A + n_B) x_A + x_B = 1 (dimensionless, no units). Temperature independent. Example: 2 mol NaCl in 8 mol water → x_NaCl = 2/10 = 0.2 4) Crystallization: A process of purification in which a substance is dissolved in a minimum amount of hot solvent and then cooled slowly to allow pure crystals to separate out. Principle: Solubility of most solids increases with temperature. On cooling, excess solute crystallises out as pure crystals. Example: Purification of impure copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O), alum, common salt (NaCl). Steps: Dissolve → filter (hot) → cool slowly → filter crystals → dry. 5) One amu (Atomic Mass Unit): One atomic mass unit (amu or u) is defined as exactly 1/12th of the mass of one atom of carbon-12 (¹²C). 1 amu = 1/12 × mass of ¹²C atom = 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁴ g Also called Dalton (Da). Used to express masses of atoms and molecules. Example: Mass of H = 1.008 amu; mass of O = 16 amu; mass of C = 12 amu (exactly). 6) Molality (m): Number of moles of solute dissolved per kilogram (1000 g) of solvent (NOT solution). m = (Moles of solute) / (Mass of solvent in kg) Unit: mol/kg or mol kg⁻¹. Temperature independent (mass doesn't change with T). Example: 1m NaOH = 1 mol NaOH dissolved in 1 kg of water. 7) Formula Mass: The sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms present in the empirical (simplest) formula of a compound. Used for ionic compounds (which do not have discrete molecules). Example: NaCl — Formula mass = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 amu CaCO₃ — Formula mass = 40 + 12 + (3×16) = 100 amu Difference from molecular mass: Molecular mass = formula mass × n (for covalent compounds). 8) Wavelength (λ): Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive points in phase in a wave — e.g., crest to crest or trough to trough. It is the distance covered by one complete wave cycle. Symbol: λ (lambda). Unit: metre (m); also nm (10⁻⁹ m), Å (10⁻¹⁰ m), pm (10⁻¹² m). Relation: c = νλ   where c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s), ν = frequency. Example: Visible light λ = 400–700 nm. X-rays: λ = 0.1–10 nm. Microwaves: λ = 1 mm – 1 m.
💡 Q.5 — Explain the Following
Q.5.1Quantum Numbers
Answer Quantum numbers are a set of four numbers that completely describe the location and energy of an electron in an atom. They arise from the solution of the Schrödinger wave equation. 1. Principal Quantum Number (n): • Determines the main energy level (shell) of the electron. • n = 1, 2, 3, 4, ... (positive integers). Shell: K(n=1), L(n=2), M(n=3), N(n=4). • Higher n = farther from nucleus = higher energy. Max electrons in shell = 2n². 2. Azimuthal (Angular Momentum) Quantum Number (l): • Determines the subshell (shape of orbital). • l = 0, 1, 2, ..., (n−1). For n=3: l = 0(s), 1(p), 2(d). • l=0: s (spherical), l=1: p (dumbbell), l=2: d (cloverleaf), l=3: f. 3. Magnetic Quantum Number (ml): • Determines the orientation of the orbital in space. • ml = −l to +l (including 0). Total values = (2l+1). • For l=1 (p): ml = −1, 0, +1 → 3 p-orbitals (px, py, pz). 4. Spin Quantum Number (ms): • Describes the spin of the electron on its own axis. • ms = +½ (spin up ↑) or −½ (spin down ↓). • Maximum 2 electrons per orbital (with opposite spins — Pauli exclusion principle). Together, these four quantum numbers uniquely identify every electron in an atom.

Answer Hund's rule states: In filling orbitals of equal energy (degenerate orbitals), electrons are distributed so that as many orbitals as possible are singly occupied first, and all singly occupied orbitals have the same spin direction (parallel spins) before pairing begins. Reason: • Single electrons in separate orbitals repel each other less than paired electrons in the same orbital. • Parallel spins (same spin direction) give maximum exchange energy → greater stability. Q.5.3Crystallization (with example)
Answer Crystallization is a technique used to purify solids. It is based on the principle that solubility of most solids increases with temperature. Impure solid is dissolved in hot solvent and cooled to obtain pure crystals. Steps: 1. Dissolve: Dissolve the impure substance in a minimum amount of hot suitable solvent (e.g., water). 2. Filter (hot): Filter the hot solution to remove insoluble impurities. 3. Cool slowly: Allow the filtrate to cool slowly. Pure substance crystallises out as solubility decreases. 4. Filter (cold): Filter the crystals using a Buchner funnel under vacuum. 5. Dry: Dry the crystals in an oven or on filter paper. Example: Purification of CuSO₄·5H₂O (copper sulphate): • Dissolve impure CuSO₄ in hot water → filter (removes insoluble impurities) → cool → blue crystals of pure CuSO₄·5H₂O form → filter and dry. Other examples: • NaCl (common salt) purification, alum (K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O), potassium nitrate (KNO₃). Note: Slow cooling gives large, well-formed crystals. Rapid cooling gives fine crystals.
Q.5.4Simple Distillation (with example)
Answer Simple distillation is a separation and purification technique used to separate a volatile liquid from a non-volatile dissolved solid or to separate two liquids with a large difference in boiling points (>25°C). Principle: When a mixture is heated, the more volatile component (lower boiling point) vaporises first, is collected and condensed into a pure liquid (distillate). Apparatus: Distillation flask → Thermometer → Condenser (Liebig condenser) → Receiver (conical flask) Procedure: 1. Put mixture in round-bottom flask. Add anti-bumping granules. 2. Heat. The component with lower boiling point vaporises. 3. Vapour passes into the condenser (cooled by water) and condenses. 4. Distillate collected in the receiver. Non-volatile solid remains in flask. Example: • Separation of water from sea water (brine) — water distils over, salt remains. • Separation of acetone (bp 56°C) from water (bp 100°C). • Purification of distilled water from tap water.
Q.5.5Adsorption Chromatography
Answer Adsorption chromatography is a separation technique based on the difference in the degree of adsorption of the components of a mixture on a solid stationary phase (adsorbent) when carried by a mobile phase (eluent/solvent). Principle: Different components of a mixture are adsorbed to different extents on the stationary phase. The more strongly adsorbed component moves slower; the weakly adsorbed component moves faster in the mobile phase. Types: 1. Column chromatography: Stationary phase packed in a glass column. Mixture loaded at top, eluted with solvent. Components separate as bands. 2. Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC): Stationary phase (silica gel/alumina) coated on a glass plate. Sample spotted at bottom. Plate placed in solvent. Components separate by Rf value. Rf (Retardation factor): Rf = distance moved by solute / distance moved by solvent. Each compound has a specific Rf value. Example: • Separation of plant pigments (chlorophylls, carotenoids) by column chromatography. • Identification of amino acids in a mixture using TLC. Common adsorbents: Silica gel (SiO₂), alumina (Al₂O₃). Common eluents: petroleum ether, chloroform, ethyl acetate, methanol.

Answer Geometrical isomerism (cis-trans isomerism) is a type of stereoisomerism arising in compounds with restricted rotation (like C=C in alkenes or cyclic compounds), where different spatial arrangements are possible for the same connectivity. Conditions for geometrical isomerism: 1. Restricted rotation around C=C bond (no rotation possible unlike C−C). 2. Each carbon of C=C must have TWO different groups attached to it. Types: • cis-isomer: Similar/same groups on the SAME side of the double bond. • trans-isomer: Similar/same groups on OPPOSITE sides of the double bond. Example — 2-Butene (CH₃−CH=CH−CH₃):

Q.5.7Ionic Bond
Answer An ionic bond (electrovalent bond) is the electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions (cation and anion) formed by the complete transfer of one or more electrons from one atom (metal) to another atom (non-metal). Conditions for ionic bond formation: 1. Large difference in electronegativity (> 1.7) between the atoms. 2. Metal (low ionisation energy) donates electrons → forms cation (+). 3. Non-metal (high electron affinity) accepts electrons → forms anion (−). 4. Both ions achieve noble gas electronic configuration. Example — Formation of NaCl: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻    (ionisation, ΔH = +495 kJ/mol) Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻    (electron affinity, ΔH = −349 kJ/mol) Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl    (lattice energy, ΔH = −787 kJ/mol) Overall: Na + Cl → NaCl (ΔH = −411 kJ/mol) Properties of ionic compounds: High melting/boiling point, soluble in polar solvents, conduct electricity in molten/dissolved state, brittle, crystalline solids.
Q.5.8Covalent Bond
Answer A covalent bond is formed by the mutual sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between two atoms, usually non-metals, where both atoms contribute equal number of electrons to the shared pair. Conditions for covalent bond: • Both atoms have similar electronegativity (small difference < 1.7). • Both atoms need electrons to complete their octet. Types of covalent bonds: • Single bond (σ bond): 1 shared pair. E.g., H−H, H−Cl, C−C in ethane. Formed by head-on overlap. • Double bond (1σ + 1π): 2 shared pairs. E.g., C=C in ethene, O=O, C=O. • Triple bond (1σ + 2π): 3 shared pairs. E.g., N≡N, C≡C in ethyne, C≡N. Examples: H• + •H → H:H (H₂) — single covalent bond (both share 1e⁻ each) :O=C=O: (CO₂) — two double bonds (carbon shares with each oxygen) H−C≡C−H (acetylene) — triple bond between carbons Properties: Low melting/boiling point (generally), non-conductor, soluble in non-polar solvents, directional bonds.
Q.5.9Dalton's Atomic Theory
Answer John Dalton (1808) proposed the first scientific atomic theory. Main postulates: 1. All matter is made up of extremely small, indivisible and indestructible particles called atoms. 2. All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties. 3. Atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties. 4. Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed (law of conservation of mass). 5. Compounds are formed when atoms of two or more elements combine in small, fixed, whole number ratios (law of definite proportion). 6. The atom is the smallest unit that takes part in chemical reactions. Limitations of Dalton's Theory: • Atom IS divisible (protons, neutrons, electrons discovered). • Isotopes: atoms of same element with different masses exist. • Isobars: atoms of different elements with same mass number. • Does not explain structure of atom or nature of bonds.
Q.5.10Postulates of Bohr's Atomic Theory
Answer Niels Bohr (1913) proposed a model for the hydrogen atom based on quantum concepts to explain atomic spectra. His postulates: 1. Stationary orbits: Electrons revolve around the nucleus in definite circular paths called orbits or shells, without emitting energy. These are called "stationary states" (K, L, M, N...). 2. Quantised angular momentum: Only those orbits are allowed where the angular momentum (mvr) of the electron is a whole number multiple of h/2π.   mvr = nh/2π (n = 1,2,3,...) 3. Energy emission/absorption: An electron can jump from higher orbit to lower orbit by emitting a photon of energy equal to the difference in energies of the two orbits. E₂ − E₁ = hν. 4. Energy of orbit: E_n = −13.6/n² eV. Energy is negative (electron is bound). n=1 is ground state. 5. Radius of orbit: r_n = n² × 0.529 Å (Bohr radius = 0.529 Å for n=1 of hydrogen). Limitations of Bohr's model: • Only explains hydrogen (one electron) — fails for multi-electron atoms. • Cannot explain fine structure of spectral lines (Zeeman and Stark effects). • Contradicts Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (fixed orbit = known position + momentum). • Does not explain chemical bonding.
Q.5.11Analgesics
Answer Analgesics are drugs that relieve pain without causing loss of consciousness. They act on the central or peripheral nervous system to reduce pain sensation. Types of Analgesics: 1. Non-narcotic (Non-opioid) analgesics: • Mild to moderate pain. No addiction. E.g., Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), Paracetamol (acetaminophen), Ibuprofen, Diclofenac. • Aspirin: Also anti-inflammatory, antipyretic. Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis. • Paracetamol: Reduces fever and relieves headache. Safer than aspirin (no gastric irritation). 2. Narcotic (Opioid) analgesics: • Severe pain. Habit-forming (addictive). Act on CNS. E.g., Morphine, Codeine, Heroin (diacetylmorphine). • Morphine: Most effective for cancer pain. Derived from opium (Papaver somniferum). • Cause sedation, euphoria, depression of CNS — dangerous if misused. Natural sources: Willow bark (salicin → aspirin), Opium poppy (morphine, codeine).
Q.5.12Saponification
Answer Saponification is the hydrolysis of a fat or oil (ester of glycerol and fatty acids) by a strong alkali (NaOH or KOH) to produce soap (sodium or potassium salt of fatty acid) and glycerol. Reaction: Fat/Oil (triglyceride) + 3NaOH → Soap (3 sodium fatty acid salts) + Glycerol (RCOO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3RCOONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃     [Ester]               [Soap]      [Glycerol] Example: Sodium oleate (soap) from olive oil + NaOH: CH₃(CH₂)₇CH=CH(CH₂)₇COO−C₃H₅ + NaOH → CH₃(CH₂)₇CH=CH(CH₂)₇COONa + glycerol Difference from esterification: • Esterification: Acid + Alcohol → Ester + Water (reversible) • Saponification: Ester + Alkali → Salt (soap) + Alcohol (glycerol) — IRREVERSIBLE Industrial process: Cold process: fat + KOH (liquid soap). Hot process: fat + NaOH (hard soap). Salt is added (salting out) to precipitate the soap.
Q.5.13Types of Cleansing Agents
Answer Cleansing agents (detergents) are substances that have the ability to remove dirt, grease, and oils from surfaces. They work by the principle of surface-active agents (surfactants) — have a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail. 1. Soaps (Anionic surfactants): • Sodium/potassium salts of long chain fatty acids (RCOONa). Made by saponification. • Hard soap: NaOH + fat. Soft soap: KOH + fat (for shaving cream). • Disadvantage: Do NOT work in hard water (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ form insoluble scum); do not work in acidic conditions. 2. Synthetic Detergents (Syndets): • Work in hard water and acidic conditions (unlike soaps). • Anionic detergents: E.g., sodium lauryl sulphate (−SO₃Na at head). Used in toothpastes, shampoos. • Cationic detergents: Quaternary ammonium salts. Germicidal properties. Used in hair conditioners. • Non-ionic detergents: No charge on head group. E.g., polyethylene glycol esters. Used in dishwashing liquids, liquid soaps. Mechanism of cleansing: Hydrophobic tail dissolves in grease/oil; hydrophilic head dissolves in water → forms micelles → dirt suspended in water → rinsed away.
Q.5.15Browning of Cut Fruits
Answer Browning of cut fruits is caused by enzymatic oxidation of phenolic compounds present in the fruit, when the fruit flesh is exposed to atmospheric oxygen. Chemistry: • Fruits contain phenolic compounds (mainly catechol and chlorogenic acid) and the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (PPO) / catechol oxidase. • When the cell is cut → cell walls rupture → enzyme and substrate come in contact with O₂. Catechol + O₂ → Quinone (brown) [polyphenol oxidase enzyme] Quinone → Polymerisation → Brown melanin pigments Fruits affected: Apple, banana, avocado, potato, mushroom, pear. Prevention: 1. Lemon juice (citric acid + ascorbic acid/Vit C) — lowers pH (inhibits PPO) and acts as reducing agent (reduces quinones back to catechol). 2. Sugar syrup or salt water — excludes oxygen. 3. Refrigeration — slows enzyme activity. 4. Blanching (brief heat treatment) — denatures the enzyme.
Q.5.16Antiseptics
Answer Antiseptics are chemical substances that inhibit the growth of or destroy microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, viruses) and are safe enough to be applied to living tissue (skin, wounds, mucous membranes) to prevent infection. Difference from Disinfectants: • Antiseptics: Applied to LIVING tissue (safe for humans). E.g., Dettol on wounds. • Disinfectants: Applied to NON-LIVING surfaces/objects (too toxic for skin). E.g., phenol on floors. Examples of Antiseptics: 1. Dettol: Mixture of chloroxylenol and pine oil. Used for wound cleaning, skin antiseptic. 2. Savlon: Chlorhexidine gluconate + cetrimide. Wound antiseptic. 3. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂): 3% solution — wound antiseptic (releases O₂ which kills anaerobic bacteria). 4. Iodine tincture: 2–3% iodine in alcohol. Used on cuts. 5. Boric acid: Mild antiseptic for eyes and skin. 6. Furacin (Nitrofurazone): Used in antiseptic creams. Mechanism: Denature proteins of bacterial cell wall, inhibit enzymes, disrupt cell membrane.
🧪 Q.6 — Physical Properties and Uses
Q.6.1Physical Properties and Uses of Alkanes
Answer Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons with general formula C_nH_(2n+2). All C−C and C−H bonds are single bonds (sigma bonds only). Also called paraffins (little affinity). Physical Properties: 1. State: C₁–C₄ are gases, C₅–C₁₇ are liquids, C₁₈ and above are solids at room temperature. 2. Boiling point: Increases with molecular mass. Branched alkanes have lower bp than straight chain isomers (less surface area = less van der Waals forces). 3. Melting point: Increases with molecular mass. Even-numbered alkanes have higher mp (more symmetrical). 4. Solubility: Insoluble in water (non-polar). Soluble in non-polar solvents (benzene, CCl₄). 5. Density: Less dense than water. Density increases with chain length (all < 1 g/mL). 6. Odour: Lower alkanes (C₁–C₄) are odourless. Intermediate alkanes have petrol-like smell. 7. Combustion: Burn with blue flame (clean combustion, unless insufficient O₂). Uses: • CH₄ (methane): Natural gas — fuel (LNG, CNG), making H₂ (steam reforming), production of carbon black. • C₄H₁₀ (butane): LPG cooking gas, lighter fuel. • Petroleum fractions (C₅–C₁₂): Motor fuel (petrol/gasoline). • Kerosene (C₁₂–C₁₅): Jet fuel, cooking fuel, lamp oil. • Paraffin wax (C₂₀+): Candles, waterproofing, packaging. • Petroleum jelly (Vaseline): Cosmetics, ointment base.
Q.6.2Physical Properties and Uses of Alkenes
Answer Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons with general formula C_nH_(2n). Contain one C=C double bond (σ + π). Also called olefins. Physical Properties: 1. State: C₂–C₄ alkenes are gases, C₅–C₁₇ are liquids, C₁₈+ are solids. 2. Boiling point: Slightly lower than corresponding alkanes (less intermolecular forces). Increases with molecular mass. cis-isomers have higher bp than trans-isomers (higher dipole moment). 3. Solubility: Insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents. 4. Density: Less dense than water. 5. Odour: Ethylene (ethene) has sweet smell; higher alkenes are nearly odourless. 6. Reactivity: More reactive than alkanes due to π bond (easily broken). Undergo addition reactions. Uses: • Ethylene (ethene, CH₂=CH₂): Plant hormone (ripening of fruits). Making polyethylene (polythene), ethanol, ethylene glycol (antifreeze), PVC. • Propene: Making polypropylene, isopropanol, acetone. • Butadiene (CH₂=CH−CH=CH₂): Making synthetic rubber (SBR). • Alkenes used as starting materials in the petrochemical industry.
Q.6.3Physical Properties and Uses of Acetylene (Ethyne)
Answer Acetylene (ethyne, C₂H₂ or HC≡CH) is the simplest alkyne with a C≡C triple bond (1σ + 2π). Formula: C_nH_(2n-2) for alkynes. Physical Properties: 1. State: Colourless gas at room temperature. bp = −84°C. 2. Odour: Pure acetylene is odourless. Commercial acetylene smells garlic-like (due to impurities — H₂S, PH₃). 3. Solubility: Slightly soluble in water. Highly soluble in acetone (dissolved in acetone in cylinders at high pressure for safe storage — Dissolved Acetylene / DA cylinders). 4. Density: Lighter than air (vapour density = 13). 5. Flammability: Burns with luminous sooty flame (high C%). Explosive with air (2–81%). 6. Acidic character: Terminal H of alkyne is slightly acidic (pKa ~25). Reacts with Na, AgNO₃ solution. HC≡CH + 2Na → NaC≡CNa + H₂ (sodium acetylide) HC≡CH + AgNO₃/NH₃ → AgC≡CAg↓ (silver acetylide, white ppt — test for terminal alkyne) Uses: • Oxyacetylene welding and cutting: Burns with O₂ at ~3500°C — the hottest flame. • Manufacture of chemicals: Making acetic acid (vinylacetylene route), acetaldehyde, vinyl chloride (PVC monomer), neoprene rubber. • Lighting: Miners' lamps (carbide lamps) — CaC₂ + H₂O → C₂H₂. • Starting material for acrylonitrile (making nylon/acrylic fibres).
⚗️ Q.7 — Explain giving Reactions
Q.7.1Dehalogenation of vicinal dihalide to prepare alkenes
Answer Vicinal dihalides (1,2-dihalides) on treatment with zinc (Zn) dust in alcoholic solution or with alcoholic KOH lose two halogen atoms from adjacent carbons to form an alkene. This is called dehalogenation (or reductive elimination). Reagent: Zinc (Zn) dust in ethanol OR alcoholic KOH (E2 elimination) Reaction (using Zn dust): CH₂Br−CH₂Br + Zn → CH₂=CH₂ + ZnBr₂ (1,2-dibromoethane)    (Ethene) [Zn dust / ethanol, heat] CH₃−CHBr−CHBr−CH₃ + Zn → CH₃−CH=CH−CH₃ + ZnBr₂ (2,3-dibromobutane)          (but-2-ene) Mechanism: Zinc inserts between the two C−X bonds and removes both halogen atoms → double bond forms between the two carbons. This reaction is the reverse of the addition of halogens to alkenes (bromine test). Used to confirm the structure of dihalides.
Q.7.2Reduction of alkyl halide to prepare methane
Answer Methane can be prepared by reducing an alkyl halide (especially CH₃X) with a suitable reducing agent such as Zn + HCl, LiAlH₄, or H₂/Pd. Method 1: Reduction with Zn + HCl (Clemmensen-type reduction): CH₃Cl + Zn + HCl → CH₄ + ZnCl₂ (Chloromethane)     (Methane) Method 2: Reduction with LiAlH₄: 4CH₃Br + LiAlH₄ → 4CH₄ + LiBr + AlBr₃ (Bromomethane)      (Methane) Method 3: Reaction with H₂ / Pd (Hydrogenolysis): CH₃I + H₂ → CH₄ + HI   (Pd catalyst) (Iodomethane) Industrial method — from Natural Gas / Methanol synthesis: CH₃OH → CH₄ + ½ O₂ (pyrolysis) C−X bond is replaced by C−H bond in this reduction.
Q.7.3Dehydration of alcohols to prepare alkene
Answer Dehydration of alcohols involves the removal of a water molecule (H₂O) from an alcohol in the presence of a dehydrating agent to produce an alkene. Reagents: Conc. H₂SO₄ (170°C) or Al₂O₃ (350°C) or conc. H₃PO₄ Reactions: CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (Ethanol)    (Ethene) [conc. H₂SO₄, 170°C] CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O (1-Propanol)     (Propene) [Al₂O₃, 350°C] CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O (2-Propanol / Isopropanol)  (Propene) [conc. H₂SO₄, 170°C] — faster than 1° alcohol Mechanism (E1 or E2 elimination): Step 1: Protonation of −OH by H₂SO₄ → −OH₂⁺ (good leaving group). Step 2: Loss of H₂O → carbocation intermediate (E1 for 2°, 3°). Step 3: Loss of H⁺ from β-carbon → alkene forms. Ease: 3° > 2° > 1° (order of dehydration ease — more stable carbocation). Saytzeff's rule: Major product has more substituted double bond (more stable alkene).
Q.7.4Friedel-Crafts Alkylation Reaction
Answer Friedel-Crafts alkylation is an electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) reaction in which an alkyl group is introduced into a benzene ring using an alkyl halide in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst (anhydrous AlCl₃). General Reaction: C₆H₆ + R−X → C₆H₅−R + HX   [anhydrous AlCl₃] Benzene + Alkyl halide → Alkylbenzene + HX Example: C₆H₆ + CH₃Cl → C₆H₅−CH₃ + HCl (Benzene) + (Chloromethane) → (Toluene) [AlCl₃ catalyst, dry ether] C₆H₆ + C₂H₅Cl → C₆H₅−C₂H₅ + HCl Benzene + Chloroethane → Ethylbenzene [AlCl₃] Mechanism: Step 1: AlCl₃ + R−Cl → R⁺ (carbocation / electrophile) + AlCl₄⁻ Step 2: R⁺ attacks π electron cloud of benzene → arenium ion (σ-complex). Step 3: AlCl₄⁻ removes H⁺ → alkylbenzene formed, AlCl₃ regenerated. Limitations: • Polyalkylation can occur (alkyl groups activate benzene). Use limited amount of RX. • Rearrangement of carbocation may occur (e.g., n-propyl → isopropyl group). • Does not work with nitrobenzene or aniline (deactivated rings).
Q.7.5Addition of HBr to Ethene
Answer Addition of HBr to ethene is an electrophilic addition reaction in which HBr adds across the C=C double bond to form bromoethane. This follows Markovnikov's rule for unsymmetrical alkenes. Reaction: CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃−CH₂−Br (Ethene)    (Hydrobromic acid) → (Bromoethane) [room temperature, no catalyst] Mechanism (Electrophilic Addition — AdE): Step 1: Protonation — H⁺ from HBr (electrophile) attacks the π bond of ethene → forms carbocation (CH₃CH₂⁺ — ethyl carbocation). CH₂=CH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃−CH₂⁺  (carbocation, slow step) Step 2: Nucleophilic attack — Br⁻ (nucleophile) attacks the carbocation → bromoethane. CH₃CH₂⁺ + Br⁻ → CH₃CH₂Br  (bromoethane, fast step) Markovnikov's rule (for unsymmetrical alkenes like propene): CH₃−CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃−CHBr−CH₃ (major product, 2-bromopropane) NOT CH₃−CH₂−CH₂Br (1-bromopropane, minor) Markovnikov: H⁺ adds to the carbon with MORE H atoms (less substituted). Br⁻ adds to more substituted carbon (more stable carbocation formed). Anti-Markovnikov addition (Peroxide effect / Kharash): In presence of peroxides, Br adds to the less substituted carbon (free radical mechanism).
🌿 Q.8 — Medicinal Property of Plants
Q.8Name the medicinal property of: Turmeric, Clove, Wintergreen, Cinnamon
Answer
PlantScientific NameActive ChemicalMedicinal Properties
1. Turmeric Curcuma longa Curcumin Anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, antiseptic (wound healing), anticancer properties, liver protection. Used in traditional Ayurvedic medicine ("golden spice").
2. Clove Syzygium aromaticum Eugenol Analgesic (toothache relief), antiseptic, antibacterial, antifungal, antispasmodic, carminative (relieves gas). Clove oil used in dentistry as local analgesic. Anti-nausea.
3. Wintergreen Gaultheria procumbens Methyl salicylate Analgesic (pain reliever), anti-inflammatory, counterirritant (muscle pain, arthritis). Used in ointments, pain relief sprays (like Moov). Related to aspirin (both are salicylates).
4. Cinnamon Cinnamomum zeylanicum Cinnamaldehyde, Eugenol Antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic (helps lower blood sugar / insulin sensitiser), antioxidant, digestive aid, antimicrobial. Used for diabetes management and metabolic syndrome.
