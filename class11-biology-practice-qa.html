🔬 Taxonomy & Classification
Q.1What is chemotaxonomy? Give one suitable example.
Answer Chemotaxonomy (chemical taxonomy) is the method of classifying organisms based on similarities and differences in their chemical constituents, especially secondary metabolites like alkaloids, terpenes, flavonoids, and proteins. Basis of Chemotaxonomy: • Amino acid sequences of proteins • DNA base composition (G+C ratio) • Presence of specific secondary metabolites • Serotaxonomy (analysis of proteins in serum) Example: • Opium alkaloids (morphine, codeine) are found in Papaver somniferum (opium poppy). These unique chemicals help confirm its classification. • Quinine alkaloid is specific to Cinchona plants — used to classify them. Advantage: More reliable than morphological taxonomy alone. Reveals evolutionary relationships.
Q.2Write a note on useful and harmful bacteria.
Answer Useful Bacteria: 1. Nitrogen fixation: Rhizobium fixes atmospheric N₂ in root nodules of legumes → increases soil fertility. 2. Decomposers: Bacteria decompose dead organic matter → recycle nutrients in ecosystem. 3. Dairy industry: Lactobacillus converts milk to curd (lactic acid fermentation). 4. Medicine: Streptomyces produces antibiotics like streptomycin. E. coli used to produce insulin. 5. Sewage treatment: Aerobic bacteria decompose sewage in treatment plants. 6. Vinegar production: Acetobacter converts alcohol to acetic acid. Harmful Bacteria: 1. Human diseases: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB), Vibrio cholerae (cholera), Salmonella typhi (typhoid), Streptococcus (strep throat). 2. Plant diseases: Xanthomonas oryzae causes blight of rice. 3. Food spoilage: Bacteria cause food to rot and become unfit for consumption. 4. Animal diseases: Brucella causes brucellosis in cattle.
Q.3The systematic study of organisms is essential. Give a suitable reason.
Answer Systematic study of organisms (systematics/taxonomy) is essential for the following reasons: 1. Identification: Helps to correctly identify and name organisms, avoiding confusion. 2. Classification: Organises millions of organisms into groups based on similarities and differences. 3. Evolutionary relationships: Reveals phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships between organisms. 4. Economic importance: Helps identify useful plants (medicinal, agricultural) and harmful organisms (pests, pathogens). 5. Ecological importance: Helps understand biodiversity and ecosystem interactions. 6. Conservation: Helps in identifying endangered species and planning conservation strategies. 7. Communication: Scientific names (binomial nomenclature) allow scientists worldwide to communicate about the same organism.
Q.4Describe the different types of viruses.
Answer Viruses are sub-microscopic, obligate intracellular parasites consisting of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat (capsid). Types based on Nucleic Acid: 1. DNA viruses: Contain DNA as genetic material. E.g., Herpes virus, Pox virus, Bacteriophage (T4 phage). 2. RNA viruses: Contain RNA as genetic material. E.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Polio virus, HIV, Influenza virus. Types based on Shape/Morphology: 1. Icosahedral (Cubical): Spherical appearance with 20 triangular faces. E.g., Adenovirus, Polio virus, Herpes virus. 2. Helical: Rod-shaped, nucleic acid wound in a helix. E.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Influenza virus. 3. Complex: Combination of icosahedral head and helical tail. E.g., Bacteriophage (T4 phage). 4. Enveloped: Have a lipid bilayer envelope outside capsid. E.g., HIV, Herpes virus, Influenza. Types based on Host: 1. Animal viruses: Infect animals. E.g., Rabies, HIV, Pox virus. 2. Plant viruses: Infect plants. E.g., TMV, Cauliflower Mosaic Virus. 3. Bacteriophages: Infect bacteria. E.g., T4 phage, Lambda phage. 4. Cyanophages: Infect cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). E.g., LPP1.
Q.5What is a herbarium?
Answer A herbarium is a collection of dried, pressed, and preserved plant specimens that are mounted on sheets, labelled with scientific information, and systematically arranged for study and reference. Steps in preparing a herbarium: 1. Collect the plant specimen with roots, stem, leaves, flowers, and fruits. 2. Press the specimen between sheets of blotting paper in a plant press. 3. Dry the specimen (change blotting paper daily for 2–3 weeks). 4. Mount the dried specimen on a herbarium sheet (white card, 28 × 41 cm). 5. Label with: scientific name, family, date of collection, collector's name, locality, altitude. Uses of Herbarium: • Provides reference material for identification of plants. • Used in taxonomic studies and research. • Acts as a permanent repository of biodiversity. • Used for study of plant distribution and ecology. Famous herbaria: Kew Gardens (England), Botanical Survey of India (BSI), Calcutta.
Q.7Write a note on a museum (Biological museum).
Answer A biological museum is a place where specimens of plants, animals, and other organisms are preserved and displayed for educational and research purposes. Types of specimens preserved: 1. Wet specimens: Preserved in formalin (4% formaldehyde). E.g., fishes, amphibians, invertebrates. 2. Dry specimens: Mounted insects on pins, bird skins, skeletal preparations. 3. Herbarium sheets: Dried plant specimens. 4. Fossils: Preserved remains or impressions of ancient organisms. 5. Models and charts: Show internal anatomy. Uses of Museum: • Educational tool for students and researchers. • Helps in identification and taxonomic studies. • Preserves biodiversity for future generations. • Provides evidence of evolution (through fossils). Famous museum: Natural History Museum (London), BNHS (Mumbai).
Q.26Write the meaning of taxonomy.
Answer Taxonomy (Greek: taxis = arrangement, nomos = law) is the branch of biology that deals with the identification, nomenclature, and classification of organisms. Three main activities of taxonomy: 1. Identification: Determining the identity of an organism (what it is). 2. Nomenclature: Giving a scientific name following binomial nomenclature (devised by Linnaeus). 3. Classification: Placing the organism in appropriate group based on characteristics. Hierarchy of classification: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species Father of Taxonomy: Carolus Linnaeus. His system of binomial nomenclature is universally accepted.
Q.27What is DNA barcoding? Mention the steps in this process.
Answer DNA barcoding is a modern technique used for rapid identification of species by analysing a short, standardised sequence of DNA (a "barcode") from an organism's genome. Standard barcode gene: • Animals: COI gene (Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) — part of mitochondrial DNA. • Plants: rbcL and matK genes — from chloroplast DNA. • Fungi: ITS region (Internal Transcribed Spacer). Steps in DNA barcoding: 1. Collection: Collect a small tissue sample from the organism. 2. DNA extraction: Extract total DNA from the tissue. 3. PCR amplification: Amplify the specific barcode gene using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). 4. DNA sequencing: Determine the nucleotide sequence of the amplified region. 5. Comparison: Compare the sequence with sequences in a reference database (e.g., BOLD — Barcode of Life Data System). 6. Identification: Match gives the identity of the species. Advantage: Fast, accurate, can identify even from small or degraded samples (dried specimens, partial remains).
🔬 Cell Biology, Cell Cycle & Cell Division
Q.8Write the significance of mitosis.
Answer Mitosis is an equational cell division in which one parent cell divides to form two daughter cells with the same chromosome number (2n → 2n). It is significant because: 1. Growth: Responsible for growth of multicellular organisms — increases number of somatic cells. 2. Repair and regeneration: Replaces old, dead, or damaged cells. E.g., healing of wounds, replacement of blood cells, regeneration in Hydra. 3. Maintains chromosome number: Daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell — maintains species identity. 4. Vegetative reproduction: In plants, mitosis helps in asexual reproduction (budding, spore formation). 5. Genetic stability: Exact copies of DNA are passed to daughter cells, maintaining genetic stability. 6. Development: From a single fertilised egg (zygote), mitosis produces all cells of the organism. 7. Replacement of RBCs: In humans, ~200 million red blood cells are produced per hour by mitosis in bone marrow.
Q.12Define meiosis. Name the person who coined the term "meiosis".
Answer Meiosis is a reductional cell division in which a diploid parent cell (2n) undergoes two successive divisions to form four haploid daughter cells (n), each having half the chromosome number of the parent cell. Key features: • Consists of two divisions: Meiosis I (reductional) and Meiosis II (equational). • Results in 4 haploid daughter cells (n). • Crossing over occurs during prophase I → genetic recombination. • Occurs in reproductive organs (gonads, anthers, ovaries). • Produces gametes (eggs and sperms) in animals, spores in plants. The term "meiosis" was coined by Farmer and Moore in 1905. The term is derived from Greek "meion" meaning "less/reduction".
Q.19Describe the cell cycle with a neat, labelled diagram.
Answer The cell cycle is the sequence of events that a cell undergoes from one division to the next. It consists of two main phases: Interphase and Mitotic phase (M phase).
                    CELL CYCLE
        ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  INTERPHASE (longest phase)       │
        │  G1 phase → S phase → G2 phase  │
        │  (growth) (DNA copy) (prep)     │
        ├────────────────────────────────────┤
        │  M PHASE (Mitotic phase)         │
        │  Prophase→Metaphase→Anaphase→   │
        │  Telophase → Cytokinesis        │
        └────────────────────────────────────┘ Cell Cycle Diagram (Draw circular diagram with G1, S, G2 as Interphase + M phase)
1. Interphase (90% of cell cycle time): • G1 phase (Gap 1 / First growth phase): Cell increases in size, synthesises proteins and RNA. Metabolically very active. Duration: 6–12 hours. • S phase (Synthesis phase): DNA replication occurs. Amount of DNA doubles (2n → 4n DNA, but still 2n chromosomes). Duration: 6–8 hours. • G2 phase (Gap 2 / Second growth phase): Cell prepares for division — spindle proteins synthesised. Cell grows further. Duration: 3–4 hours. • G0 phase (Quiescent phase): Some cells exit the cycle and remain non-dividing. E.g., nerve cells, muscle cells. 2. M phase (Mitotic phase): Includes prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase (karyokinesis) followed by cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm).
Q.28Describe prophase of mitosis.
Answer Prophase is the first and longest phase of mitosis. It is the phase of preparation where the cell gets ready for division. Events during Prophase: 1. Condensation of chromatin: Chromatin threads condense and shorten to form distinct, visible chromosomes. Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. 2. Spindle formation: Centrioles (in animal cells) move to opposite poles. Spindle fibres (asters and spindle) begin to form. 3. Disappearance of nucleolus: The nucleolus gradually disappears. 4. Nuclear envelope begins to break down (at late prophase/prometaphase). 5. Chromosomes become visible under microscope — each consists of 2 chromatids joined at centromere. Prophase is the longest phase of mitosis. Late prophase is sometimes called prometaphase when nuclear envelope breaks down.

Q.31Write a note on prophase I of meiosis.
Answer Prophase I is the longest and most complex phase of meiosis. It is divided into 5 sub-stages: LZPDD 1. Leptotene (Leptonema) — "thin thread": • Chromosomes begin to condense and become visible as long thin threads. • Chromomeres (bead-like structures) become visible on chromosomes. 2. Zygotene (Zygonema) — "paired thread": • Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) with the help of synaptonemal complex. • Paired homologous chromosomes = bivalent (tetrad = 4 chromatids). 3. Pachytene (Pachynema) — "thick thread": • Chromosomes become shorter and thicker. • Crossing over occurs — exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at chiasmata. Leads to genetic recombination. 4. Diplotene (Diplonema) — "double thread": • Synaptonemal complex dissolves. Homologous chromosomes begin to separate but remain attached at chiasmata. • Chiasmata become visible (X-shaped structures at crossing over points). 5. Diakinesis — "moving through": • Chromosomes are maximally condensed. • Chiasmata move to ends (terminalisation). • Nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear. • Spindle fibres begin to form.
🌱 Plant Kingdom & Biological Classification
Q.11What are the important features of Bryophytes?
Answer Bryophytes are the simplest land plants. They are called "Amphibians of the Plant Kingdom" because they need water for sexual reproduction but can survive on land. Important Features: 1. Habitat: Grow in moist, shady, humid places. Some are aquatic. 2. Plant body: Thalloid (no differentiation into root, stem, leaf) in liverworts; leafy stem in mosses. 3. Vascular tissue: Absent — no xylem or phloem. Transport by diffusion and osmosis. 4. Roots: Absent — unicellular or multicellular rhizoids for anchorage and absorption. 5. Alternation of generations: Dominant gametophyte generation (n). Sporophyte is dependent on gametophyte. 6. Reproduction: Water essential for fertilisation (motile male gametes). Asexual by spores or fragmentation. 7. Economic importance: Sphagnum (peat moss) used as fuel; Marchantia used medicinally. Examples: Marchantia (liverwort), Sphagnum, Funaria (mosses), Anthoceros (hornworts).
Q.13Write the characteristics of Thallophyta.
Answer Thallophyta is the lowest division of the plant kingdom. Organisms have a simple, undifferentiated plant body called a thallus — no root, stem, or leaf. Characteristics: 1. Plant body: Simple, thallus-like — not differentiated into root, stem, and leaf. 2. Vascular tissue: Absent (no xylem and phloem). 3. Habitat: Mostly aquatic (fresh water and marine); some terrestrial. 4. Reproduction: Both asexual (by spores, fragmentation) and sexual. 5. Sex organs: Unicellular, not surrounded by sterile cells (non-jacketed). 6. Nutrition: Autotrophic (algae have chlorophyll); some heterotrophic (fungi). 7. Embryo: Not formed after fertilisation (no embryophytes). Includes: Algae (green, red, brown), Fungi, Lichens (by old classification) Examples: Spirogyra, Chara, Mucor, Ulva, Sargassum.
Q.15What are the important features of Kingdom Fungi?
Answer Fungi are eukaryotic, heterotrophic, spore-bearing organisms without chlorophyll. They obtain nutrition by absorbing organic compounds from their substrate. Important Features: 1. Cell wall: Made of chitin (not cellulose like plants). 2. Nutrition: Heterotrophic — saprophytic (on dead matter), parasitic (on living host), or mutualistic (mycorrhizae). 3. Body: Composed of hyphae (thread-like filaments). Mass of hyphae = mycelium. Hyphae can be septate or coenocytic (non-septate). 4. Reproduction: By spores — asexual (conidia, sporangiospores) and sexual (ascospores, basidiospores). 5. Storage food: Glycogen (like animals, not starch). 6. Flagella: Mostly absent (except water moulds — Oomycetes). 7. Nuclear membrane: Present (eukaryotic). Divisions: Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, Deuteromycetes (Fungi Imperfecti). Examples: Mucor (Phycomycetes), Penicillium, Aspergillus (Ascomycetes), Agaricus (mushroom, Basidiomycetes), Alternaria (Deuteromycetes).
Q.16Describe the different types of viruses based on their host.
Answer 1. Animal viruses: • Infect animals including humans. • Can have DNA or RNA. • Example: Influenza virus (flu), Rabies virus, HIV (AIDS), Hepatitis B, Poliovirus, Poxvirus, Herpesvirus. • Can cause widespread epidemics and pandemics. 2. Plant viruses: • Infect plants — spread by insect vectors (aphids), contaminated tools, seeds. • Usually contain RNA (ssRNA) enclosed in a helical capsid. • Cause mosaic diseases, ring spots, yellowing, stunting. • Example: Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Cauliflower Mosaic Virus, Potato virus X. 3. Bacteriophages (phages): • Infect bacteria. Complex structure — icosahedral head + helical tail. • Contain dsDNA. Use bacterial machinery for replication. • Lytic cycle: phage kills the bacterium. Lysogenic cycle: phage DNA integrates into bacterial chromosome. • Example: T4 phage (infects E. coli), Lambda phage, T7 phage. 4. Cyanophages: • Infect cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). Example: LPP1 phage.
Q.30What are the two subkingdoms of Kingdom Plantae?
Answer Kingdom Plantae is divided into two subkingdoms: 1. Cryptogamae (Hidden reproduction — no seeds or flowers): Further divided into: • Thallophyta — Algae (no differentiated body). E.g., Spirogyra, Ulothrix. • Bryophyta — Mosses and liverworts (no vascular tissue). E.g., Marchantia, Funaria. • Pteridophyta — Ferns (vascular but no seeds). E.g., Dryopteris, Selaginella. 2. Phanerogamae (Visible reproduction — produce seeds): Further divided into: • Gymnospermae — Naked seeds (not enclosed in fruit). E.g., Pinus, Cycas. • Angiospermae — Seeds enclosed in fruit (flowering plants). E.g., Mango, Rose, Wheat.     — Monocotyledons (1 cotyledon): Wheat, Maize.     — Dicotyledons (2 cotyledons): Pea, Bean, Mango.
🫀 Human Physiology — Digestion, Excretion & Skeletal System
Q.6Explain the structure of a typical synovial joint.
Answer A synovial joint is a freely movable joint (diarthrosis) that allows a wide range of movement. It is the most common type of joint in the human body.
    STRUCTURE OF SYNOVIAL JOINT
    Bone 1 ─── [Hyaline cartilage]
                      │
                Synovial cavity
           (filled with synovial fluid)
                      │
    Bone 2 ─── [Hyaline cartilage]
    [Fibrous capsule surrounds the joint]
    [Synovial membrane lines the capsule] Label: Articular cartilage, Synovial membrane, Synovial cavity, Fibrous capsule, Bones
Components: 1. Articular (hyaline) cartilage: Covers the bone ends. Reduces friction and absorbs shock. 2. Synovial cavity: Space between the bones filled with synovial fluid. 3. Synovial membrane (Synovium): Lines the inside of the fibrous capsule. Secretes synovial fluid. 4. Synovial fluid: Viscous, slippery fluid — lubricates the joint, reduces friction, nourishes cartilage. 5. Fibrous capsule (Joint capsule): Outer covering of dense connective tissue that encloses the joint. 6. Ligaments: Dense connective tissue bands that connect bone to bone, provide stability. Examples: Knee joint (hinge), Hip joint (ball and socket), Shoulder joint, Elbow joint.

Q.17With the help of a neat, labelled diagram, explain the structure of a nephron.
Answer A nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each kidney contains about 1 million nephrons. A nephron consists of two main parts: the renal corpuscle and the renal tubule.
          STRUCTURE OF NEPHRON
   Bowman's capsule ← Glomerulus (capillaries)
        (Renal corpuscle = above two)
                ↓
   Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT) — in cortex
                ↓
   Loop of Henle (descending + ascending) — in medulla
                ↓
   Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT) — in cortex
                ↓
   Collecting Duct → Renal Pelvis → Ureter Label all parts in diagram. Afferent arteriole brings blood in; Efferent arteriole takes blood out of glomerulus.
Parts of nephron: 1. Bowman's capsule: Double-walled cup. Inner visceral layer (podocytes) and outer parietal layer. Surrounds the glomerulus. Filtration occurs here. 2. Glomerulus: Tuft of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule. High blood pressure causes filtration of blood (ultrafiltration). Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule = Malpighian body/renal corpuscle. 3. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Lined by cuboidal cells with microvilli (brush border). Reabsorbs 70–80% of water, glucose, amino acids, Na⁺. 4. Loop of Henle: Hairpin-shaped. Descending limb (thin, water permeable) — water exits by osmosis. Ascending limb (thick, impermeable to water) — Na⁺ and Cl⁻ actively pumped out. Creates concentration gradient in medulla. 5. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Fine tuning of urine composition. Selective secretion and reabsorption under hormonal control (ADH, aldosterone). 6. Collecting duct: Multiple DCTs join. Urine flows down collecting duct into renal pelvis. Permeable to water under ADH — concentrates urine.
Q.18Write a note on the sternum.
Answer The sternum (breastbone) is a flat, elongated bone forming the middle part of the anterior thoracic wall. It provides attachment for the ribs and clavicles. Structure — Three parts: 1. Manubrium (Handle): Upper broad part. Articulates with clavicle (collar bone) on each side and with 1st and 2nd ribs. Jugular notch is present at its upper end. 2. Body (Corpus sterni / Gladiolus): Middle, longest part. Articulates with costal cartilages of 2nd to 7th ribs. Has transverse ridges (sternebrae). 3. Xiphoid process (Xiphisternum): Lower, smallest cartilaginous part. Provides attachment for abdominal muscles and diaphragm. Ossifies completely in adults. Functions: • Forms the anterior wall of the thoracic cage — protects heart and lungs. • Provides attachment for ribs via costal cartilages. • Site for bone marrow — haematopoiesis (blood cell production). • Site of sternal puncture for bone marrow biopsy (clinical use).
Q.20Describe the three parts of the small intestine.
Answer The small intestine is the longest part of the alimentary canal (~6–7 m long). It is the main site of digestion and absorption. It has three parts: 1. Duodenum ("twelve" — about 25 cm = 12 finger-breadths): • C-shaped, shortest, and widest part. Fixed to posterior abdominal wall. • Receives chyme from stomach (via pyloric sphincter). • Receives bile from liver (via common bile duct) and pancreatic juice from pancreas (via pancreatic duct) — both open at Hepatopancreatic ampulla (Ampulla of Vater). • Main site for chemical digestion of proteins, fats, and carbohydrates. • Contains Brunner's glands (secrete mucus to protect from acid). 2. Jejunum ("empty" — about 2.5 m): • Middle part. Highly coiled. Reddish in colour (more blood supply). • Has plicae circulares (circular folds) and villi — increase surface area for absorption. • Main site for absorption of nutrients (amino acids, monosaccharides, fatty acids). • Contains Peyer's patches (lymphoid tissue — immunity). 3. Ileum ("coil" — about 3.5 m): • Longest part. Opens into large intestine at ileocaecal valve. • Continues absorption of nutrients, especially vitamin B₁₂ and bile salts. • Has more Peyer's patches than jejunum. • Walls thinner and less villi than jejunum.
Q.21What are joints? Explain their different types.
Answer A joint (articulation) is the point of contact between two or more bones of the skeleton. Joints provide support and allow movement. Classification of Joints: A. Fibrous Joints (Synarthroses — immovable): • Bones joined by fibrous connective tissue. No movement possible. • Types: Sutures (flat bones of skull), Gomphosis (teeth in jaw), Syndesmosis (radius-ulna). B. Cartilaginous Joints (Amphiarthroses — slightly movable): • Bones joined by cartilage. Slight movement possible. • Types: Synchondrosis (sternum-rib junction), Symphysis (pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs). C. Synovial Joints (Diarthroses — freely movable): • Most common type. Have a synovial cavity filled with synovial fluid. • Types:   1. Hinge joint: Movement in one plane. E.g., Knee, Elbow, Ankle.   2. Ball and socket joint: Movement in all directions. E.g., Hip, Shoulder.   3. Pivot joint: Rotation only. E.g., Atlas-axis (neck rotation).   4. Gliding (Plane) joint: Sliding movements. E.g., Wrist carpals, intervertebral.   5. Condyloid (Ellipsoid) joint: E.g., Wrist joint.   6. Saddle joint: E.g., Carpometacarpal joint of thumb.
Q.22Write notes on: (a) Atlas vertebra (b) Axis vertebra.
Answer (a) Atlas (1st cervical vertebra — C1): • Ring-shaped vertebra with no body and no spinous process. • Has two lateral masses (superior and inferior articular facets). • Superior articular facets (concave) articulate with occipital condyles of skull — forms atlantooccipital joint → allows nodding motion ("yes" movement). • Anterior arch has a facet for dens of axis. Posterior arch has a groove for vertebral artery. • Large vertebral foramen provides space for the medulla oblongata. (b) Axis (2nd cervical vertebra — C2): • Has a unique tooth-like projection called the dens (odontoid process) on its body. • Dens projects upward and fits into the ring of atlas — acts as a pivot. • Atlantoaxial joint (atlas rotating around dens) → allows rotation of head ("no" movement). • Has a prominent bifid spinous process and superior articular facets for atlas. • Body is stout. Vertebral foramen is triangular.
Q.23Define excretion and osmoregulation.
Answer Excretion: Excretion is the biological process by which metabolic waste products (especially nitrogenous wastes) are eliminated from the body to maintain homeostasis. • Main excretory product: Urea in humans (ureotelism). • Other products: CO₂ (lungs), water vapour (lungs, skin), salts (sweat). • Main organ: Kidneys (also: skin, lungs, liver). • Urea is produced in the liver from amino acids (urea cycle / ornithine cycle). Osmoregulation: Osmoregulation is the active regulation of water content and osmotic pressure of body fluids within acceptable limits, despite changes in the external environment. • Kidneys are the main osmoregulatory organs in humans. • ADH (antidiuretic hormone) from posterior pituitary — promotes water reabsorption in collecting duct. • Aldosterone from adrenal cortex — promotes Na⁺ reabsorption (and water follows by osmosis). • Example: Drinking salty water → osmolality increases → ADH released → kidneys reabsorb water → concentrated urine.
Q.24What are the different types of nutrition?
Answer 1. Autotrophic Nutrition: Organism synthesises its own food from inorganic substances using an external energy source. • Photosynthesis: Use sunlight as energy source. E.g., green plants, algae, cyanobacteria. • Chemosynthesis: Use chemical energy. E.g., Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter (nitrifying bacteria). 2. Heterotrophic Nutrition: Organism cannot make its own food — depends on other organisms for nutrition. • Holozoic: Ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation of solid food. E.g., all animals including humans. • Saprophytic (Saprotrophic): Feed on dead and decaying organic matter by external digestion. E.g., fungi, some bacteria. • Parasitic: Live in/on host organism and derive nutrition at host's expense. E.g., tapeworm, Cuscuta (dodder plant), lice. • Symbiotic / Mutualistic: Both organisms benefit. E.g., Rhizobium in root nodules, lichen (alga + fungus). • Insectivorous: Carnivorous plants that trap and digest insects. E.g., Drosera (sundew), Nepenthes (pitcher plant).

Q.14Write a note on ureotelism.
Answer Ureotelism is the mode of excretion in which the main nitrogenous waste product is urea (CO(NH₂)₂). Organisms that excrete urea are called ureotelic. Examples of ureotelic organisms: • Humans and all mammals, amphibians (adult frogs), cartilaginous fishes (sharks, rays). Formation of urea — Ornithine Cycle (Urea Cycle): Urea is produced in the liver from excess amino acids and CO₂. NH₃ (ammonia) + CO₂ → carbamoyl phosphate → citrulline → argininosuccinate → arginine → Urea + ornithine Properties of urea: • Less toxic than ammonia. Soluble in water. Concentrated to save water. • Urea:water ratio in urine = approximately 2%. Comparison:

Q.32Sketch and label the human digestive system.
Answer
          HUMAN DIGESTIVE SYSTEM
                         Mouth (lips, teeth, tongue, salivary glands)
                         ↓
                         Pharynx
                         ↓
                         Oesophagus (25 cm)
                         ↓
   Liver ──→         Stomach (cardiac, fundus, pyloric)
   Gall bladder      ↓
   Pancreas ──→   Small intestine (Duodenum → Jejunum → Ileum)
                         ↓
                         Large intestine (Caecum → Ascending colon →
                         Transverse → Descending → Sigmoid colon → Rectum)
                         ↓
                         Anus Accessory organs: Liver (bile), Pancreas (enzymes + insulin), Salivary glands (amylase)
Functions at each level: • Mouth: Mechanical digestion (teeth); salivary amylase digests starch. • Stomach: HCl (pepsin activation, kills bacteria); pepsin digests proteins; gastric lipase. • Small intestine: Main site of chemical digestion (pancreatic enzymes, bile) and absorption (villi, microvilli). • Large intestine: Absorption of water, electrolytes; formation of faeces.
Q.9Sketch and label: (a) Paramecium   (b) Mucor   (c) Spirogyra
Answer (a) Paramecium (Kingdom Protista — ciliated protozoan):
   PARAMECIUM (slipper-shaped)
   Cilia (all over body) ←
   Oral groove → Mouth pore → Gullet → Food vacuole
   Macronucleus (large — for metabolism)
   Micronucleus (small — for reproduction)
   Contractile vacuole (for osmoregulation) — 2 of them
   Pellicle (outer covering)
Shape: slipper/shoe-shaped | Size: 150-300 μm | Unicellular
(b) Mucor (Kingdom Fungi — Phycomycetes):
   MUCOR (black pin mould)
   Sporangiophore (upright hypha) — branches from mycelium
      ↑
   Sporangium (round spore case) at tip — contains spores
   Columella (sterile dome inside sporangium)
   Coenocytic hyphae (no cross walls — multinucleate)
   Mycelium (white/grey cottony mass on substrate) Mycelium → Sporangiophore → Sporangium → Columella + Spores
(c) Spirogyra (Kingdom Plantae — Green algae / Thallophyta):
   SPIROGYRA (pond silk / water silk)
   Cell wall (outer — cellulose; inner — pectin)
   Spiral chloroplast (ribbon-like, with pyrenoids for starch)
   Nucleus (central, suspended by cytoplasmic strands)
   Vacuole (large, central)
   Pyrenoids (protein centre for starch synthesis) along chloroplast
   Filamentous arrangement — unbranched Spirogyra is named after its spiral/helical ribbon-shaped chloroplast.
