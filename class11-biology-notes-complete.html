📗 Unit 1 – Diversity in Living World
Q1. What is chemotaxonomy? Give one suitable example. ▶

💡 Explanation: Traditional taxonomy used shape, size, colour to classify organisms. But chemotaxonomy uses chemicals inside the organism — like proteins and DNA — which are more reliable because chemicals don't change with the environment. Two organisms with similar chemical makeup are closely related even if they look different. This is now the most accurate form of classification.

Chemotaxonomy is the method of classifying organisms based on their chemical constituents — especially proteins, DNA sequences, and secondary metabolites.

  • It compares biochemical similarities and differences among organisms.
  • More accurate than morphological classification alone.

Example: Plants producing reserpine (Rauwolfia serpentina) are grouped together based on this chemical compound.

✍️ Define + example = full marks
Q2. Write a note on useful and harmful bacteria. ▶

💡 Explanation: Bacteria are the most abundant organisms on earth. They are prokaryotes with no nucleus. Most bacteria are helpful to us — they help digest food, fix nitrogen, produce medicines. Some however cause serious diseases by producing toxins or directly invading tissues.

Useful Bacteria:

  • Lactobacillus – converts milk to curd (fermentation)
  • Rhizobium – fixes atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules of legumes
  • Bacillus subtilis – produces antibiotics
  • Used in production of cheese, vinegar, and vitamins

Harmful Bacteria:

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis – causes tuberculosis (TB)
  • Vibrio cholerae – causes cholera
  • Clostridium tetani – causes tetanus
  • Cause food spoilage and plant diseases
Q3. The systematic study of organisms is essential. Give a suitable reason. ▶

💡 Explanation: Without systematic study, we would have no order in the living world. Imagine millions of species with no names or categories — finding a cure for a disease or understanding an ecosystem would be impossible. Taxonomy gives every organism a unique identity and helps scientists communicate globally using the same names.

Systematic study (taxonomy) of organisms is essential because:

  • It helps in identification and naming of organisms
  • Establishes evolutionary relationships among organisms
  • Helps in understanding biodiversity
  • Useful in agriculture, medicine, ecology, and conservation
  • Prevents confusion — every organism gets a unique scientific name
✍️ Give at least 4 reasons for full marks
Q4. Describe the different types of viruses. ▶

💡 Explanation: Viruses are non-cellular, acellular particles — they are on the border of living and non-living. They cannot reproduce on their own — they need a host cell. They inject their DNA/RNA into the host and use the host's machinery to make copies of themselves. This is why viruses are hard to kill — antiviral drugs must target the virus without harming the host cell.

Viruses are classified based on:

A. Based on Host:

  • Plant viruses – TMV (Tobacco Mosaic Virus)
  • Animal viruses – Influenza, HIV, Polio virus
  • Bacteriophages – infect bacteria (e.g., T4 phage)

B. Based on Nucleic Acid:

  • DNA viruses – Adenovirus, Herpes virus
  • RNA viruses – TMV, HIV, Influenza

C. Based on Shape:

  • Spherical (e.g., Poliovirus)
  • Rod-shaped (e.g., TMV)
  • Complex/tadpole-shaped (e.g., Bacteriophage)
Q5. What is a herbarium? ▶

💡 Explanation: A herbarium is like a library of plants. Just as a library stores books for reference, a herbarium stores dried plant specimens for future study. Scientists can compare a newly found plant with herbarium specimens to identify it, even if the plant is extinct in the wild. India's largest herbarium is at BSI (Botanical Survey of India), Kolkata.

A herbarium is a storehouse (repository) of collected plant specimens that are dried, pressed, and preserved on sheets. These sheets are arranged in a systematic order (according to a universally accepted classification system) and stored for reference.

  • Each specimen carries a label with name, date, location of collection
  • Serves as a quick referral system for plant taxonomists
  • Famous herbaria: Kew (London), BSI Kolkata (India)
Q7. Write a note on a museum. ▶

💡 Explanation: A museum is a permanent record of biodiversity. Unlike herbaria (only plants), museums have animals too. Specimens preserved in formalin stop decaying because formalin kills all bacteria and fungi that would otherwise break down the tissue. Taxidermy (stuffing animals) is used for larger animals like birds and mammals to preserve their natural appearance.

A museum is a place where preserved specimens of plants and animals are stored for study and reference.

  • Specimens preserved in formalin, alcohol, or as dry specimens
  • Insects are pinned and kept in insect boxes
  • Skeletons and casts of large animals are displayed
  • Used for teaching, research, and public education
  • Example: Natural History Museum, London
Q26. Write the meaning of taxonomy. ▶

💡 Explanation: Taxonomy is the science of order in biology. Without it, a biologist in India and a biologist in USA could call the same organism by completely different names and never realise they are studying the same species. Linnaeus solved this with binomial nomenclature — a two-part universal Latin name. The hierarchy (Kingdom to Species) organises all life into a logical tree.

Taxonomy (Greek: taxis = arrangement; nomos = law) is the branch of biology that deals with identification, naming, and classification of organisms.

  • Based on observable characteristics
  • Father of taxonomy: Carolus Linnaeus
  • Uses binomial nomenclature — two-part Latin names (Genus + species)
  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species
Q27. What is DNA barcoding? Mention the steps in this process. ▶

💡 Explanation: Traditional taxonomy required physical specimens and expert knowledge. DNA barcoding revolutionised this — just like a barcode on a product identifies it in seconds, a short DNA sequence can identify any species from a tiny tissue sample. It's used in customs to check for smuggled animals, in food industry to verify fish species, and in conservation to monitor endangered species.

DNA barcoding is a technique that uses a short standardized region of DNA (like a barcode) to identify species quickly and accurately.

Gene used: CO1 gene (cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) for animals; rbcL gene for plants.

Steps:

  • 1. Collect tissue sample from organism
  • 2. Extract DNA from the tissue
  • 3. Amplify the target gene by PCR
  • 4. Sequence the DNA
  • 5. Compare with database (BOLD system) for identification
Q30. What are the two subkingdoms of Kingdom Plantae? ▶

💡 Explanation: Kingdom Plantae is divided based on whether plants produce seeds or not. Cryptogamae (hidden reproduction) include simpler plants like algae, mosses, and ferns that reproduce by spores — no flowers, no seeds. Phanerogamae (visible reproduction) include seed-bearing plants — gymnosperms have naked seeds (like pine cones) and angiosperms have seeds enclosed in fruits (like mango, wheat).

  • Cryptogamae – plants without seeds; reproduce by spores. Includes Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta.
  • Phanerogamae – plants with seeds. Includes Gymnosperms and Angiosperms.
📗 Unit 2 – Plant Kingdom
Q11. What are the important features of Bryophytes? ▶

💡 Explanation: Bryophytes are called amphibians of the plant kingdom because like amphibians (frogs), they live in moist areas and need water for reproduction (the sperm must swim to reach the egg). They are the first plants to have moved from water to land but they never fully adapted to dry conditions. Examples like moss (Funaria) are commonly seen on moist walls and rocks.

  • Called amphibians of plant kingdom — need water for reproduction
  • Body is thallus-like, differentiated into stem and leaf-like structures
  • No vascular tissue (xylem/phloem) — no true roots, stems, leaves
  • Attached to soil by rhizoids
  • Dominant phase is gametophyte (haploid)
  • Sporophyte is dependent on gametophyte
  • Examples: Marchantia (liverwort), Funaria (moss), Anthoceros (hornwort)
Q13. Write the characteristics of Thallophyta. ▶

💡 Explanation: Thallophyta are the simplest plants — their body is not divided into root, stem, or leaf. The whole body is called a thallus. Algae like Spirogyra are found in ponds and ditches. They are important because they produce oxygen through photosynthesis and are the base of aquatic food chains.

  • Plant body is a thallus — not differentiated into root, stem, leaf
  • No vascular tissue
  • Mostly aquatic (algae)
  • Reproduce by vegetative, asexual (spores) and sexual methods
  • Chlorophyll present — autotrophic (algae)
  • Examples: Spirogyra, Ulva, Chara, Volvox
  • Also includes fungi (heterotrophic thallophytes)
Q15. What are the important features of Kingdom Fungi? ▶

💡 Explanation: Fungi are unique organisms — they are neither plants nor animals. They don't make their own food (no chlorophyll) — instead they break down dead organic matter (saprophytes) or live on other organisms (parasites). Their cell wall is made of chitin, not cellulose. The mushroom we eat is actually the reproductive part (fruiting body) of a fungus. Fungi are also used to make antibiotics like penicillin.

  • Heterotrophic — saprophytic, parasitic, or symbiotic
  • Cell wall made of chitin
  • Body made of hyphae → collectively called mycelium
  • Reproduce by spores (asexual) and sexual methods
  • Store food as glycogen (not starch)
  • Lack chlorophyll — non-photosynthetic
  • Examples: Rhizopus, Penicillium, Agaricus, Aspergillus
📗 Unit 3 – Cell Biology & Division
Q8. Write the significance of mitosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Mitosis is a copying process. The cell makes an exact copy of all its chromosomes and divides into two identical daughter cells. This is how our body grows — a single fertilised egg divides billions of times by mitosis to form all our organs. It also replaces damaged cells, like when skin heals after a cut.

  • Helps in growth of multicellular organisms
  • Maintains the chromosome number (diploid) in daughter cells
  • Repairs and replaces damaged or worn-out tissues
  • Responsible for asexual reproduction (in lower organisms)
  • Maintains genetic stability — daughter cells are genetically identical to parent
Q12. Define meiosis. Name the person who coined the term "meiosis". ▶

💡 Explanation: Unlike mitosis which makes identical copies, meiosis is a reduction division. It reduces the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n) so that when two gametes (sperm + egg) fuse during fertilisation, the original chromosome number is restored. Without meiosis, chromosome number would double every generation! Crossing over during meiosis also creates new genetic combinations — this is why children are not identical to parents.

Meiosis is a type of cell division in which the chromosome number is reduced to half (haploid). It occurs in reproductive organs to produce gametes.

  • Involves two successive divisions: Meiosis I and Meiosis II
  • Results in 4 haploid daughter cells from 1 diploid parent cell
  • Promotes genetic variation through crossing over

Term coined by: J.B. Farmer and J.E.S. Moore (1905)

Q19. Describe the cell cycle with a neat, labelled diagram. ▶

💡 Explanation: The cell cycle is a carefully controlled sequence. During Interphase the cell doubles its contents (DNA replication in S phase), grows (G1, G2), then divides during M phase. This control is important — if a cell skips steps or divides uncontrollably, it leads to cancer. G0 is a resting state where cells like neurons permanently stop dividing.

The cell cycle is the sequence of events from the formation of a cell to its division into two daughter cells.

Two main phases:

1. Interphase (I-phase): Longest phase — cell prepares for division

  • G₁ phase – cell growth; RNA and protein synthesis
  • S phase – DNA replication (chromosome number doubles)
  • G₂ phase – further growth; organelle duplication

2. M-phase (Mitotic phase):

  • Karyokinesis – division of nucleus (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase)
  • Cytokinesis – division of cytoplasm
G₁ → S → G₂ → M → (repeat)

Q28. Describe prophase of mitosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Prophase is the preparation stage. The cell packages its DNA into tight, visible chromosomes (like coiling a rope to carry it). The nuclear envelope (which protects DNA) breaks down so spindle fibres can reach the chromosomes. Centrioles help in forming the spindle — that is why animal cells (which have centrioles) divide more efficiently than plant cells.

Prophase is the first and longest stage of mitosis. Key events:

  • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes
  • Each chromosome consists of 2 chromatids joined at centromere
  • Nuclear envelope starts to disappear
  • Nucleolus disappears
  • Spindle fibres begin to form (from centrioles in animal cells)
  • Centrioles move to opposite poles
✍️ Draw and label: chromosomes, spindle fibres, disappearing nuclear envelope

💡 Explanation: The key difference is the outcome — mitosis produces 2 identical cells (for growth/repair), while meiosis produces 4 genetically different cells (for reproduction). Q31. Write a note on prophase I of meiosis. ▶

💡 Explanation: Prophase I is the most important and complex phase of meiosis. The most critical event here is crossing over (in Pachytene stage) — where homologous chromosomes exchange segments of DNA. This is the main source of genetic variation in sexually reproducing organisms. Without crossing over, all offspring of the same parents would be genetically identical.

Prophase I is the most complex and longest phase of meiosis. It is divided into 5 substages:

  • Leptotene: Chromosomes become visible; start to condense
  • Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis); bivalents form
  • Pachytene: Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids; chiasmata form
  • Diplotene: Homologs start to separate; chiasmata visible
  • Diakinesis: Chromosomes fully condensed; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms
✍️ Remember: L-Z-P-D-D (Leptotene-Zygotene-Pachytene-Diplotene-Diakinesis)
📗 Unit 4 – Human Physiology
Q10. Explain the structure of a V.S. kidney. ▶

💡 Explanation: The kidney has an outer cortex where filtration happens and an inner medulla where concentration of urine happens. The pelvis collects all the urine from the tubules and drains it into the ureter. The hilum is the entry/exit point — like the "door" of the kidney. Always draw and label a clean diagram for this question.

The kidney is a bean-shaped organ. Its vertical section shows:

  • Cortex (outer region): Contains Malpighian corpuscles (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) and convoluted tubules
  • Medulla (inner region): Contains renal pyramids and loops of Henle
  • Pelvis: Funnel-shaped cavity that collects urine; leads into ureter
  • Hilum: Notch on inner side where renal artery enters and renal vein + ureter exit
  • Renal capsule: Tough outer covering
✍️ Draw and label: cortex, medulla, pelvis, hilum, renal artery, vein, ureter
Q17. Explain the structure of a nephron with diagram. ▶

💡 Explanation: The nephron is like a tiny purification plant. Blood enters the glomerulus at high pressure and gets filtered — water, glucose, urea, salts all pass into the Bowman's capsule (this is filtration). As the filtrate passes through PCT, Loop of Henle and DCT, useful substances like glucose and water are reabsorbed back into blood. What remains is urine — mainly urea, excess salts and water.

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each kidney has ~1 million nephrons.

Parts of a nephron:

  • Bowman's capsule: Cup-shaped structure enclosing the glomerulus
  • Glomerulus: Tuft of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule — filtration occurs here
  • Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Selective reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, water
  • Loop of Henle: Descending (permeable to water) + Ascending limb (permeable to salts) — concentrates urine
  • Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Further reabsorption and secretion
  • Collecting Duct: Collects urine from many nephrons → passes to pelvis
Q14. Write a note on ureotelism. ▶

💡 Explanation: When proteins are broken down in our body, the nitrogen must be removed (since only C, H, O go into energy pathways). This nitrogen becomes ammonia first. In ureotelic organisms like us, ammonia is converted to urea in the liver — a less toxic compound — and excreted in urine. This is more efficient in water conservation compared to ammonia (which needs lots of water to dilute).

Ureotelism is the mode of excretion in which urea is the main nitrogenous waste product.

  • Occurs in: humans, most mammals, frogs, aquatic turtles
  • Urea is formed in the liver by the ornithine cycle (urea cycle)
  • Urea is less toxic and less soluble than ammonia → can be stored before excretion
  • Requires less water for excretion compared to ammonotelism
  • Excreted through kidneys as urine
Q23. Define excretion and osmoregulation. ▶

💡 Explanation: Excretion removes harmful waste products (especially nitrogenous wastes from protein breakdown). Osmoregulation is about balance — maintaining the right amount of water and salts. These two processes are closely linked in the kidney — the same nephron that filters out waste also reabsorbs the right amount of water. ADH (antidiuretic hormone) from the pituitary gland controls how much water is reabsorbed.

Excretion: The biological process by which metabolic waste products (especially nitrogenous wastes like urea, uric acid) are eliminated from the body to maintain internal homeostasis.

Osmoregulation: The process by which an organism maintains the concentration of water and dissolved solutes (osmolarity) in its body fluids within a narrow range, despite external changes.

  • Kidneys are the main organs of both excretion and osmoregulation
  • ADH (antidiuretic hormone) controls water reabsorption in kidneys
Q24. What are the different types of nutrition? ▶

💡 Explanation: All living organisms need energy. Autotrophic organisms (like plants) make their own food using sunlight or chemicals. Heterotrophic organisms (like us) must eat other organisms for energy. The type of heterotrophic nutrition depends on the food source — holozoic organisms eat solid food, saprophytes feed on dead matter, parasites feed on living hosts without immediately killing them.

Two main types:

A. Autotrophic Nutrition: Organism prepares its own food

  • Photosynthesis (plants, algae) — uses sunlight
  • Chemosynthesis (some bacteria) — uses chemical energy

B. Heterotrophic Nutrition: Organism depends on others for food

  • Holozoic – ingest solid food (animals, humans)
  • Saprophytic – feed on dead organic matter (fungi, bacteria)
  • Parasitic – live on/inside host (tapeworm, Plasmodium)
  • Symbiotic – mutual benefit (Rhizobium in root nodules)
Q20. Describe the three parts of the small intestine. ▶

💡 Explanation: The small intestine is the main site of digestion and absorption. The duodenum receives digestive juices (bile from liver, pancreatic juice from pancreas) and starts breaking down food. The jejunum has millions of villi (finger-like projections) that massively increase surface area for absorption. The ileum absorbs special nutrients like Vitamin B12. Food spends 2–4 hours in the small intestine.

The small intestine (~6–7 m long) has three parts:

  • Duodenum (25 cm): C-shaped; receives bile from liver and pancreatic juice from pancreas; partial digestion of proteins, fats, and carbohydrates begins here
  • Jejunum (~2.5 m): Main site of digestion and absorption; has many villi and microvilli for maximum absorption
  • Ileum (~3.5 m): Final part; absorbs vitamin B₁₂ and bile salts; connects to large intestine at ileocaecal junction
Q25. Write down the dental formula of a human being. ▶

💡 Explanation: The dental formula shows the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper and lower jaws. Incisors cut food, canines tear it, premolars and molars grind it. Humans are diphyodont (two sets: milk teeth + permanent teeth) and heterodont (different types of teeth for different functions). Humans are the only primates with 32 permanent teeth including wisdom teeth.

Dental formula shows the number of each type of tooth in one half of each jaw (upper/lower):

I 2/2 · C 1/1 · PM 2/2 · M 3/3 = 16 per side × 2 = 32 total teeth
  • I = Incisors (cutting), C = Canines (tearing), PM = Premolars (grinding), M = Molars (grinding)
  • Humans have 32 permanent teeth (including 4 wisdom teeth)
  • Diphyodont: two sets of teeth (milk + permanent)
Q32. Sketch and label the human digestive system. ▶

💡 Explanation: The human digestive system is basically a long tube (~9 metres total) with specialised sections. Each section has a specific job — mouth breaks food mechanically, stomach uses acid to kill bacteria and begin protein digestion, small intestine is the main site of chemical digestion and absorption, large intestine absorbs water and forms faeces. The liver and pancreas are accessory glands that produce digestive juices but are not part of the tube itself.

Parts of the Human Digestive System (in order):

  • Mouth (oral cavity) → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → Large Intestine (caecum, colon, rectum) → Anus

Accessory glands:

  • Salivary glands – secrete saliva (amylase)
  • Liver – secretes bile (stored in gall bladder)
  • Pancreas – secretes pancreatic juice (lipase, amylase, trypsin)

📗 Unit 5 – Locomotion & Support (Skeletal System)
Q6. Explain the structure of a typical synovial joint. ▶

💡 Explanation: A synovial joint is the most common type of freely movable joint. The synovial fluid acts like engine oil — it lubricates the joint so bones don't grind against each other. Without this fluid, movements like walking or lifting would be painful. Arthritis is a disease where this fluid reduces and joints become painful.

A synovial joint is a freely movable joint found where two bones meet and movement is required.

Structure:

  • Articular cartilage: Covers the ends of bones — reduces friction
  • Synovial cavity: Space between bones filled with synovial fluid
  • Synovial membrane: Lines the capsule; secretes synovial fluid
  • Joint capsule: Tough fibrous outer covering holding the joint together
  • Ligaments: Connect bone to bone; provide stability
  • Synovial fluid: Acts as lubricant; reduces friction during movement

Examples: Knee joint, shoulder joint, hip joint, elbow joint

Q18. Write a note on the sternum. ▶

💡 Explanation: The sternum (breastbone) plays a critical protective role — it forms the front wall of the chest (thoracic cage) along with the ribs, protecting the heart and lungs. The xiphoid process is important in CPR — proper hand placement avoids pressing on it to prevent injury. Remember: Manubrium (top) → Body (middle) → Xiphoid (bottom).

The sternum (breastbone) is a flat bone located in the centre of the chest. It forms the anterior (front) wall of the thoracic cage.

Three parts:

  • Manubrium: Upper part; articulates with clavicle and 1st rib
  • Body (gladiolus): Middle and longest part; articulates with ribs 2–7
  • Xiphoid process: Small lower part; made of cartilage; no rib attachment

The sternum protects the heart and lungs. It joins with ribs to form the ribcage.

Q21. What are joints? Explain their different types. ▶

💡 Explanation: A joint is where two bones meet. Fibrous joints (skull) are fixed because we don't need skull bones to move — they protect the brain. Cartilaginous joints (vertebrae) allow slight movement — needed for bending the spine. Synovial joints (knee, shoulder) are freely movable — essential for locomotion. The type of joint at each location matches its function perfectly.

A joint is the point of contact between two bones or between a bone and cartilage.

Q22. Write notes on: (a) Atlas vertebra (b) Axis vertebra. ▶

💡 Explanation: Atlas and Axis are the top two vertebrae of the spine. Atlas supports the skull (like the Greek god Atlas holding the world) — it allows the nodding "yes" movement. Axis has the dens (odontoid process) which acts as a pivot — it allows the rotation "no" movement of the head. These two vertebrae together give us the ability to turn and nod our head freely.

(a) Atlas vertebra (C1):

  • First cervical vertebra; ring-shaped
  • No body or spinous process
  • Has two large lateral masses with articular facets
  • Articulates with skull (occipital condyles) → allows nodding (yes movement)

(b) Axis vertebra (C2):

  • Second cervical vertebra
  • Has a bony projection called odontoid process (dens) pointing upward
  • Dens fits into the ring of atlas → forms a pivot joint → allows rotation of head (no movement)

Q9. Sketch and label: (a) Paramecium (b) Mucor (c) Spirogyra ▶

💡 Explanation: For sketch questions — always draw a neat, large, clear diagram with all labels pointed by straight lines (not arrows). Paramecium uses its cilia for both movement and feeding. Mucor is a common bread mould — its sporangiophores are like tiny mushrooms carrying spore bags. Spirogyra's spiral chloroplasts are unique — they run the entire length of the cell and contain pyrenoids for starch synthesis. Practice drawing all three!

  • Slipper-shaped, unicellular, covered with cilia

  • Filamentous fungus; aseptate hyphae
  • Label: sporangiophore, sporangium, spores, mycelium (rhizoids)

(c) Spirogyra (Green algae):

  • Filamentous green algae with spiral chloroplasts
  • Label: spiral chloroplast, pyrenoid, nucleus, cell wall, vacuole

Q16. Describe the different types of viruses based on their host. ▶

💡 Explanation: Viruses are classified by their host because a virus that infects tobacco plants cannot infect a human — it is specifically adapted to enter plant cells. Bacteriophages are important in research and medicine — scientists use them to fight antibiotic-resistant bacteria (phage therapy). HIV is a retrovirus — it uses RNA as its genetic material and reverse transcribes it into DNA, which is why it is so difficult to treat.

  • Plant viruses: Infect plants. Usually RNA viruses. E.g., TMV (Tobacco Mosaic Virus) — causes mosaic disease in tobacco and tomato. Rod-shaped.
  • Animal viruses: Infect animals and humans. DNA or RNA type. E.g., Poliovirus (RNA), Adenovirus (DNA), HIV (RNA-retrovirus), Influenza virus.
  • Bacteriophages: Infect bacteria. Usually DNA viruses with complex structure (head, tail, tail fibres). E.g., T4 bacteriophage — infects E. coli.

🌍 Chapter 1 – The Living World
1.1 Characteristics of Living Organisms ▶

💡 Explanation: Metabolism is the most important characteristic of life — even organisms that don't reproduce (like mules or sterile bees) are still alive because they metabolise. The moment metabolism completely stops, the organism is dead. Growth happens internally (from within) — a sand mound may grow by addition of sand from outside but it does not grow from within like a living cell does.

Planet Earth has abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living) components.

1.2 Herbarium ▶

💡 Explanation: A herbarium is like a reference library of plants. Dried specimens last hundreds of years — herbaria from the 1700s are still used by scientists today! The label on each sheet is critical — it tells when and where the plant was found, who collected it, and its classification. This data helps track how species distribution has changed over time. India's largest herbarium is at BSI (Botanical Survey of India), Kolkata with over 2 million specimens.

Herbarium = a dried plant specimen pressed, treated and mounted on a standard size sheet to preserve it.

  • Word coined by Pitton de Tournefort
  • Art initiated by Italian taxonomist Luca Ghini (1490–1556)
  • Effective tools in taxonomic studies

Label (lower right corner) contains:

  • Date and place of collection
  • Detailed classification of the plant
  • Ecological peculiarities and characters
  • Local names and name of the collector

1.3 Botanical Gardens ▶

💡 Explanation: Botanical gardens are living museums of plants. Unlike herbaria (dried specimens), plants here are alive and growing. Scientists can study growth, flowering, fruiting, and behaviour of plants from all over the world in one place. They also serve as gene banks — preserving rare and endangered plant species. The first botanical garden was created in 1543 in Italy and India's famous one is in Howrah which has the largest banyan tree in the world.

  • Places where plants from different parts of world are grown scientifically in in vivo manner
  • Plants labelled with scientific and common names
  • First botanical garden established in 1543 by Prof. Luca Ghini at Pisa, Italy
  • Famous Indian example: Indian Botanical Garden, Howrah (Kolkata)
1.4 Museum ▶

💡 Explanation: Museums preserve biodiversity for future generations — including extinct species that can no longer be found in nature. Formalin (formaldehyde solution) works as a preservative because it cross-links proteins and kills bacteria, preventing decomposition. Taxidermy is used for birds and mammals because stuffing preserves their natural shape and colour better than formalin jars. School and college museums are important for hands-on learning in biology.

  • Collections of preserved plant and animal specimens
  • Specimens preserved in formalin (10%–40% formaldehyde) in transparent jars
  • Larger animals (birds, mammals) are stuffed and preserved — science called Taxidermy
  • Collections include shells, skeletons, insect boxes
  • Reference hubs for biodiversity studies
1.5 Zoological Parks & 1.6 Biodiversity Parks ▶

💡 Explanation: In-situ conservation is always better because organisms are conserved in their natural environment where they can interact with other species and maintain ecological balance. Ex-situ conservation (zoos, herbaria) is a backup when in-situ is not possible. A biodiversity park restores degraded land by planting native species — it brings back natural ecosystems on barren land.

Zoo (Zoological Park):

  • Wild animals kept in captivity; conditions similar to natural habitat

Biodiversity Park:

  • Ecological assemblage of species forming self-sustaining communities on degraded/barren landscapes
  • This is in-situ conservation

💡 Explanation: Cell size and microscopy are basic but frequently tested facts. The smallest cell (Mycoplasma) is a bacterium with no cell wall — it is also the cause of a type of pneumonia. The electron microscope uses beams of electrons instead of light — electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, allowing much greater magnification and resolution.

5.2 Cell Theory & Modern Cell Theory ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The cell is the basic unit of life — just as a brick is the basic unit of a building. Schleiden and Schwann proposed the cell theory but they could not explain where new cells came from. Virchow filled this gap with his statement "Omnis cellula-e-cellulla" — all cells come from pre-existing cells. This became the foundation of modern cell biology and also explained why cancer cells keep dividing uncontrollably.

  • Matthias Schleiden (1838) — German botanist; plant tissues composed of cells
  • Theodore Schwann (1839) — British zoologist; animals & plants made of cells
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855) — new cells arise from pre-existing cells: Omnis cellula-e-cellulla

Postulates of Modern Cell Theory:

  • All living organisms are made up of cells
  • Living cells arise from pre-existing cells
  • Cell is the structural and functional unit of life
  • Total activities of cells = activity of organism
  • Cells show transformation of energy
  • Cells contain DNA & RNA in nucleus and cytoplasm
✍️ Trick: Schleiden=Plants | Schwann=Animals | Virchow=New cells from old
5.3 Totipotency & Stem Cells ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Totipotency is the remarkable ability of a single cell to grow into an entire organism. Stem cells in embryos are totipotent — they haven't yet been told what to become. As the embryo develops, cells become specialised and lose this ability (differentiation). Scientists are trying to use stem cells to regenerate damaged organs — like growing a new heart or kidney from a patient's own cells.

Totipotency: Capacity of a living nucleated cell to differentiate and divide to form any other type of cell and thereby a complete new organism.

  • Cell is totipotent because it has the entire genetic information in its nucleus
  • Embryonic animal cells are totipotent → called Stem Cells
  • Stem cells have great medical applications including cure for diseases

5.6 Prokaryotic Cell – Detailed Structure ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Prokaryotic cell structure is the most primitive and simplest form. The glycocalyx (capsule) protects bacteria from the host's immune system — this is why capsulated bacteria like Streptococcus pneumoniae cause more severe disease. Mesosomes are important because bacteria have no mitochondria — mesosomes take over the functions of respiration and DNA attachment. Plasmids are used in genetic engineering to insert useful genes into bacteria (e.g. insulin production).

Cell Envelope (3-layered):

  • Outer → Glycocalyx (slime layer = loose / capsule = tough)
  • Middle → Cell Wall (Gram +ve: Peptidoglycan | Gram –ve: Murein)
  • Inner → Plasma Membrane (phospholipid bilayer)

Gram Staining — developed by Danish bacteriologist Hans Christian Gram

Mesosomes — infoldings of cell membrane; help in:

  • Cell wall formation
  • Cellular respiration
  • DNA replication

Genetic Material:

  • Single circular coiled DNA attached to mesosome
  • Replication pattern: Theta model
  • DNA NOT with histone proteins → NOT called chromatin
  • Plasmids — extrachromosomal self-replicating DNA: F-plasmid (reproduction) | R-plasmid (antibiotic resistance)
✍️ Basal body of bacterial flagella = smallest motor in the world!
5.7 Plasma Membrane & Fluid Mosaic Model ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The Fluid Mosaic Model explains why the membrane is called "fluid" — the phospholipid molecules and proteins can move sideways (laterally) within the membrane, like icebergs floating in a sea of lipids. This fluidity is essential for functions like vesicle formation, cell signalling, and receptor movement. The hydrophobic tails always face inward — away from water — which is why the bilayer is so stable.

  • Thin, quasifluid structure; thickness → 75 Å
  • Under electron microscope → trilamellate (3 layers)
  • Contains lipids (mostly phospholipids) arranged in bilayer
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic — one hydrophilic polar head + two hydrophobic non-polar tails

Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicholson, 1972):

  • Phospholipid bilayer + proteins; proteins like "icebergs in sea of lipids"
  • Intrinsic/Transmembrane proteins — span entire membrane; form channels
  • Extrinsic/Peripheral proteins — found on two surfaces of membrane

Transport:

  • Passive Transport — no energy; along concentration gradient; water by osmosis
  • Active Transport — against concentration gradient; requires ATP; e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump
✍️ Passive = no Power | Active = ATP needed
5.8 Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: RER is the cell's protein factory — ribosomes on its surface make proteins that are then packaged and sent out. Pancreatic cells that make digestive enzymes have lots of RER. SER handles lipids and detoxification — liver cells that neutralise alcohol have abundant SER. The ER is the largest organelle in a cell and forms a continuous network from nucleus to cell membrane.

Present in all eukaryotic cells except ova and mature RBCs. Forms more than 50% of total membrane of a eukaryotic cell.

5.9 Golgi Complex ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The Golgi complex is the cell's post office and courier service. Products made in the ER arrive at the cis face, get modified (like adding sugar labels), packaged into vesicles, and shipped from the trans face to their destination — secretion outside, lysosomes, or other organelles. The "address label" on each vesicle tells it exactly where to go.

Golgi Complex = assembly + manufacturing + packaging + transport unit of cell ("Post Office of the cell")

  • Consists of stacks of membranous sacs = cisternae
  • Cis face — forming/receiving side (from ER)
  • Trans face — maturing/secreting side (products leave)
  • Cisternae in Golgi are NOT physically connected to each other

Functions:

  • Modification of ER secretions (glycolipids & glycoproteins)
  • Production of own secretions (e.g. pectin in plant cells)
  • Markers imprinted on vesicle membrane to identify target cell/organelle

Cisternal Maturation Model — cisternae themselves mature moving from cis to trans face

✍️ CIS = Come IN (receiving) | TRANS = Transfer OUT (shipping)
5.10 Lysosomes ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Lysosomes are the cell's recycling system. They digest worn-out organelles, bacteria that entered the cell, and foreign particles. The acidic pH inside lysosomes activates the digestive enzymes — if they were active at normal pH they would digest the cell itself. When a cell is damaged or old, autophagic vesicles burst and digest the entire cell — this is called autophagy (self-eating), which is why lysosomes are called "suicide bags."

Lysosomes = dismantling and restructuring units of cell; membrane-bound vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes; enzymes work at acidic pH; arise from Golgi-associated ER.

Types of Lysosomes:

  • Primary — membrane-bound vesicles; enzymes in inactive state
  • Secondary/Heterophagic — formed by fusion with endocytic vesicle
  • Residual Body — vesicle with undigested remains
  • Autophagic Vesicle (Suicide Bags) — digest cell's own damaged organelles ★★

Important facts:

  • Acrosome in sperm = modified lysosome; contains hyaluronidase
  • Tay-Sach's disease — lipase deficiency → fat accumulation → brain damage
  • Help in destruction of malignant cells (T-lymphocytes)
✍️ Lysosome = Suicide Bag / Digestive bag of the cell
5.11 Mitochondria ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Mitochondria produce ATP (the energy currency of cells) through aerobic respiration. The inner membrane folds (cristae) increase surface area to pack more ATP-producing oxysomes. Mitochondria have their own DNA and 70S ribosomes — evidence that they were once free-living bacteria engulfed by a primitive eukaryote (Endosymbiont Theory). This is why mitochondria are called semi-autonomous organelles.

Mitochondria = Powerhouse of the cell; involved in aerobic respiration. Absent in: Prokaryotic cells and RBCs.

  • Double membrane bound organelle
  • Outer membrane — permeable; protein Porin/Parson's particles
  • Inner membrane — selectively permeable; finger-like folds → Cristae
  • Inner chamber → Matrix (circular DNA + RNA + 70S ribosomes + Krebs cycle enzymes)

Oxysomes (F₀-F₁ / Elementary Particles):

  • Head (F₁) = lollipop head → faces matrix → enzyme ATP synthase
  • Foot (F₀) = embedded in inner membrane → proton channel
  • Involved in proton pumping and ATP synthesis

Mitochondria has own DNA + 70S ribosomes → semi-autonomous! (Endosymbiont Theory)

5.12 Plastids & Chloroplast ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Plastids are found only in plant cells and some algae. Chloroplasts (green) do photosynthesis — grana are the stacked thylakoids where light reactions occur. Chromoplasts (coloured) attract insects for pollination with their bright reds, yellows, and oranges. Leucoplasts store nutrients in non-green parts like roots and seeds. Endosymbiont Theory: chloroplasts were once free-living photosynthetic cyanobacteria engulfed by a cell.

Double membraned; contain DNA, RNA and 70S ribosomes. Found in plant cells, algae, some protists.

5.13 Nucleus ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: The nucleus is the control centre of the cell — like the brain. It stores all genetic information (DNA) that directs every activity of the cell. Nucleopores are like security gates — they control what enters and exits the nucleus. The nucleolus is the factory within the factory — it makes ribosomal RNA needed to build ribosomes, which then make proteins. Without a nucleus, a cell cannot survive long (like mature RBCs which have no nucleus and only live 120 days).

Nucleus = Master cell organelle; contains entire genetic information.

Interphase nucleus = nuclear envelope + nucleoplasm + nucleolus + chromatin network

  • Nuclear Envelope — double-layered; two membranes separated by perinuclear space (10–50 nm)
  • Nuclear lamina — network of protein fibres; maintains shape of nucleus
  • Nucleopores — small openings guarded by pore complexes; regulate flow of substances

Nucleolus:

  • Made of rRNA + ribosomal proteins
  • Site of ribosome biogenesis
  • NOT bound by membrane
  • Oocyte → large nucleolus | Sperm → small nucleolus

Chromatin:

  • Chromatin = DNA + histone proteins + non-histone proteins + RNA
  • Euchromatin — more DNA; genetically active
  • Heterochromatin — more proteins, less DNA; genetically inert
  • Normal human cell → 46 chromosomes
5.14 Ribosomes ★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Ribosomes are universal — found in every living cell, from bacteria to humans. They translate mRNA (the genetic message) into proteins. The size difference (70S prokaryotic vs 80S eukaryotic) is medically important — antibiotics like streptomycin and erythromycin specifically block 70S ribosomes, killing bacteria without harming our 80S ribosomes. Free ribosomes make proteins used inside the cell; bound ribosomes (on RER) make proteins to be exported.

Ribosomes = Protein factories of the cell. First observed by Pallade (1953). Made of rRNA + Proteins; no membranous covering.

5.15 Cilia & Flagella – Axoneme (9+2) ★★ ▶

💡 Explanation: Cilia and flagella are both used for movement, but differ in size and number. Cilia beat in coordinated waves — like oars of a rowing team — to move the cell or move fluid over the cell surface (e.g. mucus in respiratory tract). Flagella work like a propeller — few but powerful. The 9+2 axoneme arrangement is universal in all eukaryotic cilia and flagella — the peripheral doublets slide against each other powered by dynein motors to produce the bending motion.

  • Fine hair-like membrane-bound protoplasmic outgrowths
  • Cilia — small, many in number; act as oars
  • Flagella — longer, few in number

💡 Explanation: Centrioles are only in animal cells — plant cells manage cell division without them (they use other proteins instead). The cytoskeleton is the cell's internal skeleton — microtubules are like rigid bones giving shape, microfilaments are like muscles allowing movement, and intermediate filaments provide mechanical strength.

Centrosome — found near nucleus of animal cell; contains a pair of cylindrical structures → centrioles.

  • Proximal end looks like a cartwheel
  • Functions: assembly of spindle apparatus during cell division; form basal body of cilia & flagella

🔬 Taxonomy & Classification
Q.1What is chemotaxonomy? Give one suitable example.
Answer Chemotaxonomy (chemical taxonomy) is the method of classifying organisms based on similarities and differences in their chemical constituents, especially secondary metabolites like alkaloids, terpenes, flavonoids, and proteins. Basis of Chemotaxonomy: • Amino acid sequences of proteins • DNA base composition (G+C ratio) • Presence of specific secondary metabolites • Serotaxonomy (analysis of proteins in serum) Example: • Opium alkaloids (morphine, codeine) are found in Papaver somniferum (opium poppy). These unique chemicals help confirm its classification. • Quinine alkaloid is specific to Cinchona plants — used to classify them. Advantage: More reliable than morphological taxonomy alone. Reveals evolutionary relationships.
Q.2Write a note on useful and harmful bacteria.
Answer Useful Bacteria: 1. Nitrogen fixation: Rhizobium fixes atmospheric N₂ in root nodules of legumes → increases soil fertility. 2. Decomposers: Bacteria decompose dead organic matter → recycle nutrients in ecosystem. 3. Dairy industry: Lactobacillus converts milk to curd (lactic acid fermentation). 4. Medicine: Streptomyces produces antibiotics like streptomycin. E. coli used to produce insulin. 5. Sewage treatment: Aerobic bacteria decompose sewage in treatment plants. 6. Vinegar production: Acetobacter converts alcohol to acetic acid. Harmful Bacteria: 1. Human diseases: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB), Vibrio cholerae (cholera), Salmonella typhi (typhoid), Streptococcus (strep throat). 2. Plant diseases: Xanthomonas oryzae causes blight of rice. 3. Food spoilage: Bacteria cause food to rot and become unfit for consumption. 4. Animal diseases: Brucella causes brucellosis in cattle.
Q.3The systematic study of organisms is essential. Give a suitable reason.
Answer Systematic study of organisms (systematics/taxonomy) is essential for the following reasons: 1. Identification: Helps to correctly identify and name organisms, avoiding confusion. 2. Classification: Organises millions of organisms into groups based on similarities and differences. 3. Evolutionary relationships: Reveals phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships between organisms. 4. Economic importance: Helps identify useful plants (medicinal, agricultural) and harmful organisms (pests, pathogens). 5. Ecological importance: Helps understand biodiversity and ecosystem interactions. 6. Conservation: Helps in identifying endangered species and planning conservation strategies. 7. Communication: Scientific names (binomial nomenclature) allow scientists worldwide to communicate about the same organism.
Q.4Describe the different types of viruses.
Answer Viruses are sub-microscopic, obligate intracellular parasites consisting of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat (capsid). Types based on Nucleic Acid: 1. DNA viruses: Contain DNA as genetic material. E.g., Herpes virus, Pox virus, Bacteriophage (T4 phage). 2. RNA viruses: Contain RNA as genetic material. E.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Polio virus, HIV, Influenza virus. Types based on Shape/Morphology: 1. Icosahedral (Cubical): Spherical appearance with 20 triangular faces. E.g., Adenovirus, Polio virus, Herpes virus. 2. Helical: Rod-shaped, nucleic acid wound in a helix. E.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Influenza virus. 3. Complex: Combination of icosahedral head and helical tail. E.g., Bacteriophage (T4 phage). 4. Enveloped: Have a lipid bilayer envelope outside capsid. E.g., HIV, Herpes virus, Influenza. Types based on Host: 1. Animal viruses: Infect animals. E.g., Rabies, HIV, Pox virus. 2. Plant viruses: Infect plants. E.g., TMV, Cauliflower Mosaic Virus. 3. Bacteriophages: Infect bacteria. E.g., T4 phage, Lambda phage. 4. Cyanophages: Infect cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). E.g., LPP1.
Q.5What is a herbarium?
Answer A herbarium is a collection of dried, pressed, and preserved plant specimens that are mounted on sheets, labelled with scientific information, and systematically arranged for study and reference. Q.26Write the meaning of taxonomy.
Answer Taxonomy (Greek: taxis = arrangement, nomos = law) is the branch of biology that deals with the identification, nomenclature, and classification of organisms. Three main activities of taxonomy: 1. Identification: Determining the identity of an organism (what it is). 2. Nomenclature: Giving a scientific name following binomial nomenclature (devised by Linnaeus). 3. Classification: Placing the organism in appropriate group based on characteristics. Hierarchy of classification: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species Father of Taxonomy: Carolus Linnaeus. His system of binomial nomenclature is universally accepted.
Q.27What is DNA barcoding? Mention the steps in this process.
Answer DNA barcoding is a modern technique used for rapid identification of species by analysing a short, standardised sequence of DNA (a "barcode") from an organism's genome. Standard barcode gene: • Animals: COI gene (Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) — part of mitochondrial DNA. • Plants: rbcL and matK genes — from chloroplast DNA. • Fungi: ITS region (Internal Transcribed Spacer). Steps in DNA barcoding: 1. Collection: Collect a small tissue sample from the organism. 2. DNA extraction: Extract total DNA from the tissue. 3. PCR amplification: Amplify the specific barcode gene using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). 4. DNA sequencing: Determine the nucleotide sequence of the amplified region. 5. Comparison: Compare the sequence with sequences in a reference database (e.g., BOLD — Barcode of Life Data System). 6. Identification: Match gives the identity of the species. Advantage: Fast, accurate, can identify even from small or degraded samples (dried specimens, partial remains).
🔬 Cell Biology, Cell Cycle & Cell Division
Q.8Write the significance of mitosis.
Answer Mitosis is an equational cell division in which one parent cell divides to form two daughter cells with the same chromosome number (2n → 2n). It is significant because: 1. Growth: Responsible for growth of multicellular organisms — increases number of somatic cells. 2. Repair and regeneration: Replaces old, dead, or damaged cells. E.g., healing of wounds, replacement of blood cells, regeneration in Hydra. 3. Maintains chromosome number: Daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell — maintains species identity. 4. Vegetative reproduction: In plants, mitosis helps in asexual reproduction (budding, spore formation). 5. Genetic stability: Exact copies of DNA are passed to daughter cells, maintaining genetic stability. 6. Development: From a single fertilised egg (zygote), mitosis produces all cells of the organism. 7. Replacement of RBCs: In humans, ~200 million red blood cells are produced per hour by mitosis in bone marrow.
Q.12Define meiosis. Name the person who coined the term "meiosis".
Answer Meiosis is a reductional cell division in which a diploid parent cell (2n) undergoes two successive divisions to form four haploid daughter cells (n), each having half the chromosome number of the parent cell. Key features: • Consists of two divisions: Meiosis I (reductional) and Meiosis II (equational). • Results in 4 haploid daughter cells (n). • Crossing over occurs during prophase I → genetic recombination. • Occurs in reproductive organs (gonads, anthers, ovaries). • Produces gametes (eggs and sperms) in animals, spores in plants. The term "meiosis" was coined by Farmer and Moore in 1905. The term is derived from Greek "meion" meaning "less/reduction".

Q.28Describe prophase of mitosis.
Answer Prophase is the first and longest phase of mitosis. It is the phase of preparation where the cell gets ready for division. Events during Prophase: 1. Condensation of chromatin: Chromatin threads condense and shorten to form distinct, visible chromosomes. Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. 2. Spindle formation: Centrioles (in animal cells) move to opposite poles. Spindle fibres (asters and spindle) begin to form. 3. Disappearance of nucleolus: The nucleolus gradually disappears. 4. Nuclear envelope begins to break down (at late prophase/prometaphase). 5. Chromosomes become visible under microscope — each consists of 2 chromatids joined at centromere. Prophase is the longest phase of mitosis. Late prophase is sometimes called prometaphase when nuclear envelope breaks down.

Q.31Write a note on prophase I of meiosis.
Answer Prophase I is the longest and most complex phase of meiosis. It is divided into 5 sub-stages: LZPDD 1. Leptotene (Leptonema) — "thin thread": • Chromosomes begin to condense and become visible as long thin threads. • Chromomeres (bead-like structures) become visible on chromosomes. 2. Zygotene (Zygonema) — "paired thread": • Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) with the help of synaptonemal complex. • Paired homologous chromosomes = bivalent (tetrad = 4 chromatids). 3. Pachytene (Pachynema) — "thick thread": • Chromosomes become shorter and thicker. • Crossing over occurs — exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at chiasmata. Leads to genetic recombination. 4. Diplotene (Diplonema) — "double thread": • Synaptonemal complex dissolves. Homologous chromosomes begin to separate but remain attached at chiasmata. • Chiasmata become visible (X-shaped structures at crossing over points). 5. Diakinesis — "moving through": • Chromosomes are maximally condensed. • Chiasmata move to ends (terminalisation). • Nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear. • Spindle fibres begin to form.
🌱 Plant Kingdom & Biological Classification
Q.11What are the important features of Bryophytes?
Answer Bryophytes are the simplest land plants. They are called "Amphibians of the Plant Kingdom" because they need water for sexual reproduction but can survive on land. Important Features: 1. Habitat: Grow in moist, shady, humid places. Some are aquatic. 2. Plant body: Thalloid (no differentiation into root, stem, leaf) in liverworts; leafy stem in mosses. 3. Vascular tissue: Absent — no xylem or phloem. Transport by diffusion and osmosis. 4. Roots: Absent — unicellular or multicellular rhizoids for anchorage and absorption. 5. Alternation of generations: Dominant gametophyte generation (n). Sporophyte is dependent on gametophyte. 6. Reproduction: Water essential for fertilisation (motile male gametes). Asexual by spores or fragmentation. 7. Economic importance: Sphagnum (peat moss) used as fuel; Marchantia used medicinally. Examples: Marchantia (liverwort), Sphagnum, Funaria (mosses), Anthoceros (hornworts).
Q.13Write the characteristics of Thallophyta.
Answer Thallophyta is the lowest division of the plant kingdom. Organisms have a simple, undifferentiated plant body called a thallus — no root, stem, or leaf. Characteristics: 1. Plant body: Simple, thallus-like — not differentiated into root, stem, and leaf. 2. Vascular tissue: Absent (no xylem and phloem). 3. Habitat: Mostly aquatic (fresh water and marine); some terrestrial. 4. Reproduction: Both asexual (by spores, fragmentation) and sexual. 5. Sex organs: Unicellular, not surrounded by sterile cells (non-jacketed). 6. Nutrition: Autotrophic (algae have chlorophyll); some heterotrophic (fungi). 7. Embryo: Not formed after fertilisation (no embryophytes). Includes: Algae (green, red, brown), Fungi, Lichens (by old classification) Examples: Spirogyra, Chara, Mucor, Ulva, Sargassum.
Q.15What are the important features of Kingdom Fungi?
Answer Fungi are eukaryotic, heterotrophic, spore-bearing organisms without chlorophyll. They obtain nutrition by absorbing organic compounds from their substrate. Important Features: 1. Cell wall: Made of chitin (not cellulose like plants). 2. Nutrition: Heterotrophic — saprophytic (on dead matter), parasitic (on living host), or mutualistic (mycorrhizae). 3. Body: Composed of hyphae (thread-like filaments). Mass of hyphae = mycelium. Hyphae can be septate or coenocytic (non-septate). 4. Reproduction: By spores — asexual (conidia, sporangiospores) and sexual (ascospores, basidiospores). 5. Storage food: Glycogen (like animals, not starch). 6. Flagella: Mostly absent (except water moulds — Oomycetes). 7. Nuclear membrane: Present (eukaryotic). Divisions: Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, Deuteromycetes (Fungi Imperfecti). Examples: Mucor (Phycomycetes), Penicillium, Aspergillus (Ascomycetes), Agaricus (mushroom, Basidiomycetes), Alternaria (Deuteromycetes).
Q.16Describe the different types of viruses based on their host.
Answer 1. Animal viruses: • Infect animals including humans. • Can have DNA or RNA. • Example: Influenza virus (flu), Rabies virus, HIV (AIDS), Hepatitis B, Poliovirus, Poxvirus, Herpesvirus. • Can cause widespread epidemics and pandemics. 2. Plant viruses: • Infect plants — spread by insect vectors (aphids), contaminated tools, seeds. • Usually contain RNA (ssRNA) enclosed in a helical capsid. • Cause mosaic diseases, ring spots, yellowing, stunting. • Example: Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV), Cauliflower Mosaic Virus, Potato virus X. 3. Bacteriophages (phages): • Infect bacteria. Complex structure — icosahedral head + helical tail. • Contain dsDNA. Use bacterial machinery for replication. • Lytic cycle: phage kills the bacterium. Lysogenic cycle: phage DNA integrates into bacterial chromosome. • Example: T4 phage (infects E. coli), Lambda phage, T7 phage. 4. Cyanophages: • Infect cyanobacteria (blue-green algae). Example: LPP1 phage.

Q.23Define excretion and osmoregulation.

1.1 Dipole Moment & Molecular Structure

What is Dipole Moment?

Dipole moment (μ) is a measure of the polarity of a bond or molecule. It is the product of the charge (q) and the distance (d) between the charges.

μ = q × d    Unit: Debye (D)

A molecule has a net dipole moment only if the individual bond dipoles do not cancel each other vectorially.

Important Bond Moments (Table 1.1.2)

Key Molecules & Their Structures

  • CO₂ (μ = 0): Linear structure — two C=O bond moments cancel each other.
  • SO₂ (μ = 1.63 D): Angular structure — bond moments do NOT cancel; net dipole exists.
  • BF₃ (μ = 0): Planar triangular — three B–F bond moments arranged symmetrically cancel.
  • NH₃ (μ = 1.47 D): Pyramidal structure — bond moments + lone pair contribute to net dipole.
  • H₂O (μ = 1.85 D): Angular structure — two O–H bond moments give net dipole.
  • CH₄ (μ = 0): Tetrahedral — all C–H bonds cancel symmetrically.
💡 Exam Trick: If a molecule is symmetrical → μ = 0. If unsymmetrical → μ ≠ 0.

Dipole Moments of Disubstituted Benzenes (o, m, p)

For ortho, meta, and para isomers of dichlorobenzene:

μ_resultant = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cosθ)
ortho (θ=60°): μ = 2.6 D
meta (θ=120°): μ = 1.5 D
para (θ=180°): μ = 0 D
💡 This formula is used to identify isomers — a very common exam numerical!
1.2 Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy

Types of Molecular Vibrations

A. Stretching Vibrations — change in bond length (along bond axis):

  • Symmetric stretching: Both atoms move away from centre simultaneously.
  • Asymmetric stretching: One atom moves toward centre, other moves away.

B. Bending Vibrations — change in bond angle:

  • In-plane: Rocking, Scissoring
  • Out-of-plane: Wagging, Twisting
💡 Stretching requires MORE energy than bending.

Stretching Frequency Formula

ν̃ = (1/2πc) √(k/μ)

where: k = force constant (bond strength)
μ = reduced mass = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)
c = speed of light

Stronger bonds → higher frequency. Heavier atoms → lower frequency.

Fingerprint Region

The spectral region 900–1400 cm⁻¹ is called the fingerprint region. It is unique for each molecule. No two different compounds show the same absorption pattern in this region. Used for identifying unknown compounds.

Isotopic Effect in IR

Replacing a lighter atom with a heavier isotope lowers the stretching frequency (since μ increases).

ν̃(H–X) / ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX / μ_HX) = √2
e.g., O–H absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹, O–D at ~2630 cm⁻¹
1.3 Microwave (Rotational) Spectra & Rigid Rotor

Conditions for Microwave Absorption

Only molecules with a permanent dipole moment can absorb microwave radiation and give rotational spectra. (e.g., HCl, H₂O — YES; N₂, CO₂ — NO)

Rigid Rotor Model

A diatomic molecule is treated as a rigid rotor (two masses joined by a rigid bond). It can rotate about:

  • Bond axis
  • Vertical plane through centre of gravity
  • Horizontal plane
Moment of Inertia: I = μr²
where r = internuclear distance, μ = reduced mass

Vibration-Rotation Spectra & P/R Branch

In a combined vibrational-rotational spectrum:

  • P-branch: ΔJ = –1 (lower frequency side)
  • R-branch: ΔJ = +1 (higher frequency side)
  • Q-branch: ΔJ = 0 (not always observed)

Lines are separated by a spacing of 2B.

B = h / (8π²Ic)
1.4 Molecular Polarisation & Clausius-Mosotti Equation

Types of Polarisation

When a molecule is placed in an electric field, it gets polarised. The total molar polarisation (PT) has three contributions:

PT = Pe + Pa + Po

Pe = Electronic polarisation (due to displacement of electron cloud)
Pa = Atomic / Ionic polarisation (due to displacement of atoms/ions)
Po = Orientation polarisation (due to alignment of permanent dipoles — polar molecules only)
  • Non-polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa (no orientation polarisation)
  • Polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa + Po
💡 Pe and Pa are temperature-independent. Po is temperature-dependent (decreases as T increases).

Clausius-Mosotti Equation

For non-polar molecules (no orientation polarisation), the Clausius-Mosotti equation relates molar polarisation to dielectric constant (εr):

PM = [(εr − 1) / (εr + 2)] × (M / ρ) = (4/3)πNAα

where: εr = dielectric constant (relative permittivity)
M = molecular weight, ρ = density
NA = Avogadro number, α = polarisability

This equation is valid for non-polar molecules only (in the absence of permanent dipoles).

Debye Equation (for Polar Molecules)

Debye extended the Clausius-Mosotti equation to include orientation polarisation for polar molecules:

PT = Po + Pe + Pa

PT = (4/3)πNAα + NAμ² / (3kT)

where: μ = permanent dipole moment
k = Boltzmann constant, T = absolute temperature
α = polarisability

Rearranging (Debye equation):

PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA / 3kT

This is of the form: PT = A + B/T
A = (4/3)πNAα (temperature-independent)
B = μ²NA/3k (gives dipole moment when slope is known)

Plot PT vs 1/T → straight line. Slope = μ²NA/3k → dipole moment (μ) can be calculated.

💡 This is the Debye method for experimental determination of dipole moment!

1.5 Raman Spectroscopy

Raman Effect — Discovery

Discovered by Prof. C.V. Raman in 1928. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1930. When monochromatic light is scattered by a sample, most scattered light has the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering) but a small portion has different frequency — this is the Raman Effect.

Stokes & Anti-Stokes Lines

TypeConditionFrequencyIntensity
RayleighElastic scatteringν₀ (unchanged)Highest (99%)
Stokesν_s < ν₀ν₀ − ΔνStronger
Anti-Stokesν_as > ν₀ν₀ + ΔνWeaker
💡 Stokes lines are more intense because more molecules are in the ground state at room temperature.

Raman Activity Rule

A vibration is Raman active if there is a change in polarisability of the molecule during vibration.

A vibration is IR active if there is a change in dipole moment.

💡 Mutual Exclusion Rule: In centrosymmetric molecules, IR active vibrations are Raman inactive and vice versa (e.g., CO₂).

Applications of Raman Spectroscopy

  • Study of gases, liquids, and solids
  • Complementary to IR — gives information IR cannot
  • Useful for molecules with no dipole moment (N₂, O₂)
  • Combined with IR spectra gives complete molecular structure

Q2. Explain with example why CO₂ has zero dipole moment but SO₂ has 1.63 D. 4 Marks

SO₂ (μ = 1.63 D): Sulphur dioxide has an angular (bent) structure due to the lone pair on sulphur. The two S=O bond moments do not cancel because of the angular geometry → net dipole moment = 1.63 D.

Q5. What is the fingerprint region in IR spectroscopy? What is its significance? 3 Marks
The region 900–1400 cm⁻¹ in an IR spectrum is called the fingerprint region. It contains a large number of absorption bands due to complex vibrations of the entire molecule.

Significance:
  • Unique for every compound — like a fingerprint
  • No two different compounds show the same absorption pattern

Q6. Explain Raman Effect. What are Stokes and Anti-Stokes lines? 6 Marks
Raman Effect (discovered by C.V. Raman, 1928): When monochromatic light is incident on a substance, most scattered light has the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering). A small fraction (~1%) has different frequency — this is called the Raman Effect.

Stokes Lines: Scattered light has lower frequency than incident light (ν_s < ν₀). Molecule gains energy. These lines appear on the low-frequency side.

Anti-Stokes Lines: Scattered light has higher frequency than incident light (ν_as > ν₀). Molecule loses energy. These are weaker and appear on the high-frequency side.

Raman Shift (Δν): Δν = ν₀ − ν_s = ν_as − ν₀ (same magnitude, opposite sides)
Q7. Explain the isotopic effect in IR spectroscopy with an example. 4 Marks
Replacing a lighter atom with its heavier isotope increases the reduced mass (μ) of the bond. Since stretching frequency is inversely proportional to √μ, the frequency decreases.
ν̃ = (1/2πc) √(k/μ)
Example: O–H bond absorbs at ~3600 cm⁻¹. When H is replaced by D (deuterium):
ν̃(H–X) / ν̃(D–X) = √(μ_DX / μ_HX) ≈ √2 ≈ 1.414
So O–D absorbs at ~2630 cm⁻¹ (lower frequency). This isotopic shift is used to confirm presence of O–H or N–H groups.
Q8. State the condition for a molecule to show (a) IR spectrum (b) Raman spectrum. 3 Marks
  • (a) IR Active: A vibration is IR active if it involves a change in the electric dipole moment of the molecule. Only polar molecules or molecules with polar bonds that undergo asymmetric vibrations show IR absorption.
  • (b) Raman Active: A vibration is Raman active if it involves a change in the polarisability of the molecule.
Mutual Exclusion Rule: For centrosymmetric molecules (e.g., CO₂), vibrations that are IR active are Raman inactive and vice versa.
Q9. Explain the Debye equation. How is it used to determine dipole moment experimentally? 5 Marks
Debye Equation: Debye extended the Clausius-Mosotti equation to include orientation polarisation for polar molecules:
PT = (4/3)πNAα + μ²NA / 3kT
This can be written as: PT = A + B/T, where:
  • A = (4/3)πNAα (temperature-independent, from induced polarisation)
  • B = μ²NA/3k (orientation contribution)
Experimental Determination of μ:
  • Measure total molar polarisation PT at several different temperatures
  • Plot PT vs 1/T → straight line
  • Slope = μ²NA/3k → rearrange to find μ
  • Intercept = (4/3)πNAα → gives polarisability α
μ = √(3k × Slope / NA)
Q10. What are the different types of molecular polarisation? Explain each briefly. 4 Marks
Total molar polarisation: PT = Pe + Pa + Po

(i) Electronic Polarisation (Pe): When an electric field is applied, the electron cloud of the atom is displaced relative to the nucleus. Present in ALL molecules. Temperature-independent.

(ii) Atomic/Ionic Polarisation (Pa): Displacement of atoms or ions relative to each other within a molecule under an electric field. Temperature-independent.

(iii) Orientation Polarisation (Po): Polar molecules have permanent dipoles. In an electric field, these dipoles align along the field direction. This is temperature-dependent — at higher temperatures, thermal energy opposes alignment → Po decreases.

Non-polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa only. Polar molecules: PT = Pe + Pa + Po.
🌡️ Chapter 2 – Chemical Thermodynamics: Colligative Properties

What are Colligative Properties?

Colligative properties are properties of solutions that depend only on the number of solute particles dissolved, not on the nature or identity of the solute.

Four Colligative Properties:

  • 1. Lowering of vapour pressure
  • 2. Elevation of boiling point
  • 3. Depression of freezing point
  • 4. Osmotic pressure
💡 All four depend only on the number of moles of solute, not the type of solute.
2.1 Lowering of Vapour Pressure – Raoult's Law

Vapour Pressure of a Liquid

The pressure exerted by vapour above a liquid in equilibrium with the liquid at a given temperature is called vapour pressure.

Factors affecting VP:

  • Nature of liquid: Weaker intermolecular forces → higher VP (more volatile)
  • Temperature: Increases with temperature

Raoult's Law

For a solution of a non-volatile solute in a solvent:

p = p° × x₁   (where x₁ = mole fraction of solvent)

Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure:
(p° – p) / p° = x₂   (where x₂ = mole fraction of solute)

This is called Raoult's Law — the relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the mole fraction of the solute.

Mole Fraction & Molecular Weight Determination

x₂ = n₂ / (n₁ + n₂)
For dilute solution: (p° – p) / p° ≈ n₂/n₁ = (W₂/M₂) / (W₁/M₁)

∴ M₂ = (W₂ × M₁ × p°) / (W₁ × (p° – p))

This formula is used to determine molecular weight of solute from VP measurements.

2.2 Elevation of Boiling Point & Depression of Freezing Point

Elevation of Boiling Point (ΔT_b)

Addition of non-volatile solute lowers VP → solution needs higher temperature to boil → boiling point is elevated.

ΔT_b = K_b × m
where K_b = ebullioscopic / boiling point elevation constant
m = molality of solution

K_b = (RT_b² × M₁) / (1000 × l_v)
where l_v = latent heat of vaporization per gram

Depression of Freezing Point (ΔT_f)

Solute lowers the vapour pressure → solid and liquid phases cannot be in equilibrium at the normal freezing point → freezing point is depressed.

ΔT_f = K_f × m
where K_f = cryoscopic constant (molal depression constant)
m = molality of solution

K_f = (RT_f² × M₁) / (1000 × l_f)
where l_f = latent heat of fusion per gram of solvent

Determination of Molecular Weight (Rast Method)

The Rast method uses camphor as solvent (K_f = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹ — very high). Steps:

  • Known mass of solute (W₂) is dissolved in known mass of camphor (W₁)
  • Melting point depression (ΔT_f) is measured
  • Molecular weight is calculated:
M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)

Beckmann Method uses a thermometer that can be set at any temperature range (not just fixed points).

2.3 Osmotic Pressure & Van't Hoff's Equation

Osmosis & Osmotic Pressure

Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane (SPM) from dilute solution to concentrated solution (or from pure solvent to solution).

Osmotic Pressure (π): The minimum pressure that must be applied to the solution side to stop the flow of osmosis.

💡 Example: NaCl solution separated from pure water by SPM — water flows towards NaCl solution. To stop this, pressure equal to osmotic pressure must be applied on the NaCl side.

Van't Hoff's Laws of Osmotic Pressure

Boyle's Law: At constant temperature, osmotic pressure varies inversely with volume:

π ∝ 1/V   →   πV = constant

Charles' Law: At constant volume, osmotic pressure varies directly with temperature:

π ∝ T   →   π/T = constant

Van't Hoff's Equation: (analogous to ideal gas law)

πV = nRT
π = (n/V)RT = CRT
πV = (W/M)RT   →   M = WRT/πV

Used to determine molecular weight of solute from osmotic pressure measurements.

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

If pressure applied on solution side is greater than osmotic pressure, solvent molecules flow from solution to pure solvent — this is Reverse Osmosis.

  • Feed water: The solution from which pure water is to be extracted
  • Permeate: Purified/desalinated water obtained
  • Reject stream: Concentrated salt-containing water that doesn't pass through RO membrane
💡 RO is the basis of water purifiers used in homes!

Van't Hoff Factor (i) – For Electrolytes

Electrolytes dissociate in solution → more particles → greater colligative effect. The Van't Hoff factor (i) accounts for dissociation:

i = (Observed colligative property) / (Calculated colligative property assuming no dissociation)

Modified equations:
ΔT_b = i × K_b × m
ΔT_f = i × K_f × m
π = i × CRT

For NaCl → KCl → i ≈ 2; For BaCl₂ → i ≈ 3; For non-electrolytes → i = 1

Q1. State and explain Raoult's Law. Derive an expression for relative lowering of vapour pressure. 6 Marks
Raoult's Law: The vapour pressure of a solvent above a solution is equal to the product of the vapour pressure of the pure solvent and the mole fraction of the solvent in the solution.
p = p° × x₁
Derivation of Relative Lowering:
Since x₁ + x₂ = 1 → x₁ = 1 − x₂
p = p°(1 − x₂) = p° − p°x₂
∴ p° − p = p°x₂
∴ (p° − p)/p° = x₂ = relative lowering of VP
This shows that relative lowering of VP = mole fraction of solute (x₂), which depends only on the number of solute particles — hence it is a colligative property.
Q2. Define osmosis and osmotic pressure. Give Van't Hoff's equation for osmotic pressure and its derivation. 6 Marks
Osmosis: Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution (or from pure solvent to solution).

Osmotic Pressure (π): Minimum pressure applied on the solution to stop osmosis when separated from pure solvent by a SPM.

Van't Hoff's Equation Derivation:
  • Van't Hoff Boyle's Law: πV = constant (at constant T)
  • Van't Hoff Charles' Law: π/T = constant (at constant V)
  • Combining: πV = RT per mole
  • For n moles in volume V: πV = nRT
πV = nRT   →   π = CRT   →   M = WRT/πV
This equation is similar to the ideal gas equation PV = nRT, hence osmotic pressure behaves like gas pressure.
Q3. What is the depression of freezing point? Derive the expression for K_f (cryoscopic constant). 6 Marks
Depression of Freezing Point: When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, the freezing point of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent. The decrease is called depression of freezing point (ΔT_f).
ΔT_f = K_f × m
Derivation of K_f:
From thermodynamic derivation using Clausius-Clapeyron equation:
K_f = RT_f² / (1000 × l_f) × M₁
where:
  • T_f = freezing point of pure solvent (in Kelvin)
  • l_f = latent heat of fusion per gram of solvent
  • M₁ = molecular weight of solvent
  • R = gas constant
Molar depression constant: K_f is defined as the depression in freezing point when 1 mole of solute is dissolved in 1 kg of solvent.
Q4. Describe the Beckmann method for determination of molecular weight by depression of freezing point. 5 Marks
Apparatus: Consists of a freezing tube with a Beckmann thermometer and stirrer, placed inside an outer vessel (cooling bath) containing a freezing mixture.

Procedure:
  • Known weight (W₁) of pure solvent is taken in the freezing tube
  • Cooled to freezing point (T_f°) of pure solvent — noted
  • Known weight (W₂) of solute is added and dissolved
  • Solution is cooled to new freezing point (T_f) — noted
  • ΔT_f = T_f° − T_f is calculated
Calculation:
M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)
Rast Method: Uses camphor as solvent (K_f = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹). Very large K_f allows small depression to be accurately measured even for small amounts of solute.
Q5. Explain Reverse Osmosis. What are its applications? 4 Marks
Reverse Osmosis (RO): When pressure greater than osmotic pressure is applied on the solution side, solvent molecules are forced to flow from the solution through the SPM to the pure solvent side. This reversal of normal osmosis is called Reverse Osmosis.

Key Terms:
  • Feed water = the solution from which pure water is extracted
  • Permeate = purified/desalinated water obtained
  • Reject stream = concentrated brine/salt water left behind
Applications:
  • Desalination of seawater
  • Purification of drinking water (household RO purifiers)
  • Removal of heavy metals, bacteria, salts from water
  • Industrial wastewater treatment
Q6. Explain Van't Hoff factor. How does it modify colligative property equations for electrolytes? 4 Marks
Van't Hoff Factor (i): Electrolytes dissociate in solution, producing more particles than expected. The Van't Hoff factor accounts for this:
i = Total moles of particles in solution / Moles of solute dissolved
Modified colligative equations:
ΔT_b = i × K_b × m
ΔT_f = i × K_f × m
Δp/p° = i × x₂
π = i × CRT
Examples:
  • Non-electrolyte (glucose): i = 1
  • NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻: i = 2
  • BaCl₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻: i = 3
  • K₂SO₄ → 2K⁺ + SO₄²⁻: i = 3
Q7. What is vapour pressure of a liquid? Explain the factors affecting vapour pressure. 4 Marks
Vapour Pressure: The pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid above its surface when liquid and vapour are in dynamic equilibrium at a given temperature.

Factors affecting VP:
  • Nature of liquid: Liquids with weak intermolecular forces (e.g., diethyl ether) have high VP; those with strong forces (e.g., water, glycerol) have low VP.
  • Temperature: VP increases with temperature as more molecules gain enough kinetic energy to escape into the vapour phase.
  • Presence of solute: Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the VP of the solvent (Raoult's Law).
Q8. A solution of 0.033g of solute in 0.450g of camphor depresses freezing point by 17.7 K. Calculate molecular weight. (K_f of camphor = 37.7 K·kg·mol⁻¹) Numerical

M₂ = (1000 × K_f × W₂) / (ΔT_f × W₁)

3.1 Detection of Radioactive Radiations

Methods of Detection

Radioactive radiations (α, β, γ) are invisible but can be detected by their interactions with matter. Main detection methods:

  • Ionisation methods (GM Counter)
  • Photographic methods
  • Scintillation methods (Electrometer, PMT)
  • Fluorescence methods

Photomultiplier Tube (P.M.T.)

  • Also called an electron multiplier tube
  • Surrounded by a photosensitive cathode and anode
  • Dynodes are arranged in increasing order of positive potential
  • Incident electrons hit first dynode → secondary electrons produced
  • Each dynode multiplies electrons → amplified current
  • Aluminum foil acts as light reflector of P.M.T.

Electrometer

  • Connected to anode of PMT
  • Measures amplified current from photomultiplier tube
  • Secondary electrons from cesium/antimony metal and silver oxide are multiplied in multiples of incident electrons

Phosphor / Scintillator

  • Solid organic phosphors: naphthalene, anthracene
  • Liquid: BaPt(CN)₄ can also be used
  • Thin aluminum foil reflects light to photocathode
3.2 Applications of Radioisotopes as Tracers

What are Radioactive Tracers?

Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) are used as tracers because they behave chemically identical to normal isotopes but can be detected by their radioactive emissions. They help track the path of atoms/molecules in chemical and biological processes.

💡 Tracers are like "labels" — same chemistry, but detectable!

(i) Photosynthesis Mechanism

Plants use CO₂ and H₂O to synthesize carbohydrates. Using isotopes ¹⁴C and ¹⁸O:

6 C¹⁶O₂ + 6 H₂¹⁸O → C₆H₁₂¹⁸O₆ + 6 ¹⁶O₂
6 C¹⁸O₂ + 6 H₂¹⁶O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 ¹⁸O₂

4.1 Adsorption

Surface Chemistry — Introduction

Surface chemistry is a branch of physical chemistry dealing with properties of surfaces and phase boundaries. The surface phenomenon involving accumulation of one substance on the surface of another is called adsorption.

  • Adsorbent: The substance on whose surface adsorption occurs (e.g., activated charcoal, silica gel, Pd, Ni)
  • Adsorbate: The substance that gets adsorbed on the surface (e.g., H₂ on Ni, NH₃ on activated charcoal)
  • Desorption: Removal of adsorbed substance from adsorbent surface
  • Sorption: Both adsorption and absorption occurring simultaneously
💡 Adsorption = surface phenomenon; Absorption = bulk phenomenon

Factors Affecting Adsorption

  • Surface area: Greater surface area → greater adsorption. Activated charcoal has large surface area.
  • Temperature: Physisorption decreases with increase in temperature; chemisorption increases initially then decreases.
  • Pressure: Adsorption increases with pressure (for gases).
  • Nature of adsorbate: Gases with higher critical temperature are more easily adsorbed (e.g., SO₂ > CO₂ > H₂).
  • Nature of adsorbent: Activated charcoal, silica gel, alumina are good adsorbents due to high surface area.
4.2 Adsorption Isotherms

Adsorption Isotherm

A graphical curve showing the relationship between the amount of substance adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent and the equilibrium pressure at constant temperature is called an adsorption isotherm.

Five types of adsorption isotherms (Brunauer classification) have been reported for physical adsorption.

Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

An empirical equation given by Freundlich (valid only at low pressures):

x/m = k·P^(1/n)    (n > 1)

Taking log: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)·log P

where x = mass adsorbed, m = mass of adsorbent, P = pressure, k and n are constants.

  • Plotting log(x/m) vs log P gives a straight line
  • Slope = 1/n, Intercept = log k
  • Limitation: Valid only at low pressures; entirely empirical (no theoretical background)

Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm (1916)

Langmuir derived a theoretical equation based on kinetic theory of gases.

Postulates:

  • All gases behave ideally
  • Adsorbed layer is unimolecular (monolayer)
  • Gas molecules adsorbed on selective sites (one site = one molecule)
  • Bond between gas molecule and adsorbent site is sufficiently strong (chemical)
  • Heat of adsorption is same for all sites and independent of surface coverage

Derivation:

Let θ = fraction of surface covered. Then (1 − θ) = fraction uncovered.

Rate of adsorption = k_a × (1 − θ) × P
Rate of desorption = k_d × θ

At equilibrium: k_a(1−θ)P = k_d·θ
Solving: θ = (k_a/k_d)·P / [1 + (k_a/k_d)·P]

Let b = k_a/k_d (proportionality constant):
θ = bP / (1 + bP) ← Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm

Special cases:

  • At low pressure: bP << 1 → θ = bP (linear — follows Freundlich with 1/n = 1)
  • At high pressure: bP >> 1 → θ = 1 (saturation — monolayer complete)

Tyndall Effect

When a beam of light is passed through a colloidal solution, the path of light becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles. This is called the Tyndall Effect.

  • Observed in colloids — NOT in true solutions (particles too small to scatter)
  • The illuminated path is called the Tyndall cone
  • Used to distinguish true solutions from colloids
💡 Examples: Blue colour of sky, visibility of headlight beams in fog, beam of light in dusty room — all Tyndall Effect!

Brownian Motion

The continuous random zigzag motion of colloidal particles in a dispersion medium is called Brownian motion. It was first observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains.

  • Caused by unequal bombardment of colloidal particles by molecules of dispersion medium
  • More pronounced for smaller particles and less viscous medium
  • Brownian motion prevents settling of colloidal particles → responsible for kinetic stability of colloids
💡 Brownian motion is direct evidence of the kinetic molecular theory of matter.

Coagulation (Flocculation) of Colloids

The process of settling of colloidal particles by adding an electrolyte or by other means is called coagulation or flocculation. Colloidal particles carry a charge — adding opposite ions neutralises the charge → particles aggregate and settle.

Methods of coagulation:

  • By adding electrolyte
  • By electrophoresis (particles migrate to electrode and get discharged)
  • By mixing oppositely charged colloids
  • By boiling (increases collision between particles)

Hardy-Schulze Rule

The coagulating power of an electrolyte depends on the valency of the active ion (ion of opposite charge to the colloid).

Hardy-Schulze Rule:
Higher the valency of the coagulating ion → greater the coagulating power

For a negative colloid (e.g., As₂S₃ sol):
Al³⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺    (coagulating power)

For a positive colloid (e.g., Fe(OH)₃ sol):
[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻    (coagulating power)
💡 Hardy-Schulze Rule: Coagulating power ∝ (valency of active ion)⁶ approximately (Schulze rule of six).

Gold Number (Protective Colloids)

A protective colloid is a lyophilic colloid that, when added to a lyophobic colloid, prevents its coagulation by forming a protective layer around the particles.

Gold Number (introduced by Zsigmondy): The minimum mass (in mg) of a protective colloid required to just prevent the coagulation of 10 mL of a standard gold sol (red → blue colour change) on adding 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution.

Smaller the gold number → Better the protective power

Gold numbers of some colloids:
Gelatin: 0.005–0.01 (best protective colloid)
Haemoglobin: 0.03–0.07
Gum arabic: 0.1–0.2
Starch: 25
💡 Gelatin has the smallest gold number → most effective protective colloid. Used in ice cream, medicines, photographic films.

Zeta Potential

The electric potential at the slipping/shear plane between the colloidal particle (with its bound ions layer) and the bulk of the dispersion medium is called zeta potential (ζ).

  • Higher zeta potential → greater electrostatic repulsion between particles → more stable colloid
  • Coagulation occurs when zeta potential is reduced to zero (by adding electrolyte)
  • Zeta potential is related to electrophoretic mobility of colloidal particles

Emulsions

Emulsions are colloidal systems of two immiscible liquids dispersed in each other with the help of an emulsifier.

  • If γ_a > γ_b → water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion is stable
  • If γ_b > γ_a → oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion is stable

  • (ii) Proteins: e.g., casein in milk — lyophilic (water-loving) emulsifier

Demulsification

Breaking of an emulsion into two separate liquid layers is called demulsification. Methods:

  • Physical: Heating, freezing, or centrifugation (e.g., breaking of milk emulsion to get cream)
  • Chemical: Adding electrolytes, surfactants, or detergents to destroy the emulsifier film

4.4 Surfactants — Properties & Applications

What are Surfactants?

Surfactants (surface-active agents) are substances that decrease surface tension when added to a liquid. They concentrate at the interface and have a dual structure:

  • Hydrophilic head (polar/water-loving)
  • Hydrophobic tail (non-polar/water-hating, usually a long hydrocarbon chain)
Gibbs Adsorption Equation:
S = −(c/RT) × (dγ/dc)
where S = excess concentration at surface, γ = surface tension, c = bulk concentration

If dγ/dc is negative (surfactant) → S is positive → substance concentrates at surface = positive adsorption

Classification of Surfactants

Micelle Structure & CMC

At low concentrations, surfactants exist as individual molecules. Above a critical concentration called CMC (Critical Micelle Concentration), surfactant molecules aggregate into spherical structures called micelles:

  • Hydrophilic heads face the water (outward)
  • Hydrophobic tails point inward (away from water)
  • CMC is a definite value for each surfactant
  • Above CMC, conductance of ionic surfactant drops — micelles form
💡 Ionic micelles behave like macromolecular electrolytes — conducts less than expected

Detergents vs Soaps

Soaps: Sodium/potassium salts of higher fatty acids (e.g., sodium stearate). Ineffective in hard water — precipitate with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions.

Detergents: Cleaning agents with sulphonate group. Work even in hard water because sulphonates of Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ are soluble.

Example: Linear alkyl benzene sulphonate (LABS)

Structure: Linear alkyl chain + Benzene ring + –SO₃⁻Na⁺ (sulphonate group)

Donnan Membrane Equilibrium

When a protein (non-diffusible macromolecular ion) is separated from a salt solution by a semipermeable membrane, the distribution of diffusible ions across the membrane is unequal — this is the Donnan Membrane Equilibrium.

  • The non-diffusible ions prevent small ions from escaping through the membrane
  • At equilibrium: [Na⁺]inside × [Cl⁻]inside = [Na⁺]outside × [Cl⁻]outside
  • Creates osmotic pressure difference and electrical potential difference

Coagulation value: The minimum concentration of electrolyte (in millimoles per litre) required to cause coagulation of a colloid is called its coagulation value. Lower coagulation value = higher coagulating power.
Q10. What is the Tyndall Effect? What is Brownian motion? State their significance. 4 Marks
Tyndall Effect: When a beam of light passes through a colloidal solution, the path of the beam becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles. This phenomenon is called the Tyndall Effect.
  • Not observed in true solutions (particles too small to scatter light)
  • Used to distinguish colloids from true solutions
  • Illuminated path = Tyndall cone
  • Examples: blue colour of sky, headlights in fog, beam of light in dusty room
Brownian Motion: The continuous, random zigzag motion of colloidal particles in a dispersion medium, caused by unequal bombardment by medium molecules.
  • First observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains
  • More pronounced for smaller particles in less viscous medium
  • Responsible for kinetic stability of colloids — prevents settling
  • Provides direct evidence for kinetic molecular theory
Q11. What is gold number? How does it measure protective power of a colloid? 4 Marks
Gold Number (introduced by Zsigmondy): The minimum mass in milligrams of a protective colloid that just prevents the coagulation of 10 mL of a standard red gold sol when 1 mL of 10% NaCl is added to it.

Smaller gold number → Greater protective power
Protective ColloidGold Number
Gelatin0.005–0.01 (best)

Starch25 (poor)

Mechanism: The protective colloid forms an adsorbed layer around the lyophobic colloidal particles, preventing the electrolyte ions from reaching and neutralising their charge. This prevents coagulation.

Applications: Gelatin used in photographic films, ice cream, medicines; proteins in blood protect iron oxide particles from coagulating.
Q12. Explain the applications of adsorption in daily life and industry. 5 Marks
  • Gas masks: Activated charcoal in gas masks adsorbs poisonous gases (CO, SO₂, Cl₂) protecting soldiers/workers from toxic environments.
  • Dehumidification: Silica gel (SiO₂) adsorbs moisture from air. Used in packaging of electronics, medicines, and food products.
  • Chromatography: Separation of mixtures by differential adsorption on a stationary adsorbent phase. Alumina and silica gel are common adsorbents.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis: Reactants are adsorbed on catalyst surface (e.g., N₂ and H₂ on Fe surface in Haber process). Adsorption weakens bonds and lowers activation energy.
  • Clarification of sugar: Animal charcoal (bone char) adsorbs coloured impurities from raw sugar solution to produce white refined sugar.
  • Froth flotation: Pine oil selectively adsorbs on sulphide ore particles → they become hydrophobic → float as froth, separated from gangue. Used in metallurgy.
  • Water purification: Activated carbon removes taste, odour, and organic contaminants from drinking water by adsorption.
