Q.1Packing efficiency of Simple Cubic (SC), Body Centred Cubic (BCC) and Face Centred Cubic (FCC).
Answer Packing Efficiency = (Volume occupied by atoms in unit cell / Total volume of unit cell) × 100 (i) Simple Cubic (SC) : • Number of atoms per unit cell = 1  (8 corner × 1/8) • Relation: 2r = a  (r = radius, a = edge length)  → r = a/2 • Volume of atom = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)π(a/2)³ = πa³/6 • Volume of unit cell = a³ Packing efficiency = (πa³/6)/a³ × 100 = π/6 × 100 = 52.4% (ii) Body Centred Cubic (BCC) : • Number of atoms per unit cell = 2  (8×1/8 + 1 body) • Relation: 4r = a√3  → r = a√3/4 • Volume occupied = 2 × (4/3)πr³ = 2 × (4/3)π(a√3/4)³ = (√3π/8)a³ Packing efficiency = (√3π/8) × 100 = 68.0% (iii) Face Centred Cubic (FCC) : • Number of atoms per unit cell = 4  (8×1/8 + 6×1/2) • Relation: 4r = a√2  → r = a√2/4 = a/(2√2) • Volume occupied = 4 × (4/3)πr³ = 4 × (4/3)π(a/2√2)³ Packing efficiency = π/(3√2) × 100 = 74.0% Summary: SC (52.4%) < BCC (68%) < FCC (74%). FCC is most efficiently packed.
Q.2Short note: Schottky Defect
Answer Schottky defect is a type of point defect in ionic crystals where equal numbers of cations and anions are missing from their lattice sites. Conditions: • Occurs in ionic crystals where cation and anion are of similar size (e.g., NaCl, KCl, CsCl, KBr). • To maintain electrical neutrality, equal numbers of cations and anions leave the lattice. Effects: • Density of crystal decreases (missing ions = less mass, same volume). • Crystal as a whole remains electrically neutral. • Electrical conductivity slightly increases (ions move through vacancies). Example: NaCl, KBr, CsCl
Q.3Short note: Frenkel Defect
Answer Frenkel defect is a type of point defect where an ion (usually the smaller cation) is displaced from its normal lattice site to an interstitial site. Conditions: • Occurs in ionic crystals where there is a large difference in size between cation and anion. • The smaller ion (usually cation) occupies the interstitial position. Effects: • Density of crystal does NOT change (no ions leave the crystal). • Electrical neutrality is maintained. • Electrical conductivity increases. • Dielectric constant increases. Example: AgBr, AgCl, ZnS (silver halides show both Frenkel and Schottky)

Q.5What is a solution?
Answer A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances in which the composition is uniform throughout. Components: • Solute — the substance dissolved (present in smaller amount). • Solvent — the substance that dissolves the solute (present in larger amount). Types of Solutions: • Gas in liquid (e.g., O₂ dissolved in water, aerated drinks) • Liquid in liquid (e.g., alcohol in water) • Solid in liquid (e.g., NaCl in water, sugar in water) Concentration Expressions: Molarity, Molality, Mole fraction, Normality, % w/v
Q.6Raoult's Law for volatile solute (Derivation)
Answer Raoult's law states: The partial vapour pressure of each volatile component of a solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction. For a binary solution of volatile A and B: P_A = P°_A × x_A    and    P_B = P°_B × x_B Where P°_A, P°_B = vapour pressures of pure components, x_A, x_B = mole fractions. Total vapour pressure: P_total = P_A + P_B = P°_A·x_A + P°_B·x_B P_total = P°_A·x_A + P°_B·(1 − x_A) P_total = P°_B + (P°_A − P°_B)·x_A Ideal solutions: Solutions that obey Raoult's law at all concentrations and temperatures. ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0. Example: Benzene + toluene, n-hexane + n-heptane
Q.7Raoult's Law for non-volatile solute (Derivation)
Answer When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, vapour pressure of solution is less than that of pure solvent. Statement: The relative lowering of vapour pressure is equal to the mole fraction of the solute. Derivation: Let P° = vapour pressure of pure solvent, P = vapour pressure of solution. By Raoult's law:   P = P° × x_solvent = P° × (1 − x_solute) P = P° − P°·x_solute P° − P = P°·x_solute (P° − P)/P° = x_solute     ...(Relative lowering of vapour pressure) If n = moles of solute, N = moles of solvent: x_solute = n/(n + N) ≈ n/N (for dilute solutions) (P° − P)/P° = n/N = (w/M) / (W/M_s) This is used to find molecular mass of solute from vapour pressure lowering.
Q.8Elevation of Boiling Point (Derivation)
Answer The boiling point of a solution is higher than that of the pure solvent. The increase is called elevation of boiling point (ΔTb). Derivation: Adding non-volatile solute lowers vapour pressure. Solution must be heated more to reach atmospheric pressure → boiling point increases. ΔTb = Tb(solution) − Tb(pure solvent) ΔTb ∝ m  (molality of solution) ΔTb = Kb × m Where Kb = molal elevation constant (ebullioscopic constant) — characteristic of solvent. Kb for water = 0.52 K·kg/mol m = molality = moles of solute per kg of solvent = (w × 1000)/(M × W) Molecular mass of solute: M = (Kb × w × 1000) / (ΔTb × W)
Q.9Depression of Freezing Point (Derivation and State)
Answer The freezing point of a solution is lower than that of pure solvent. The decrease is called depression of freezing point (ΔTf). Derivation: Adding solute lowers vapour pressure of solution. Solid-liquid equilibrium shifts — the solution must be cooled to a lower temperature before it solidifies. ΔTf = Tf(pure solvent) − Tf(solution) ΔTf ∝ m ΔTf = Kf × m Kf = molal depression constant (cryoscopic constant). Kf for water = 1.86 K·kg/mol Molecular mass: M = (Kf × w × 1000) / (ΔTf × W) Applications: Antifreeze in car radiators, salting roads in winter, freezing mixtures.
Q.10Prove that pH + pOH = 14
Answer At 25°C, the ionic product of water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ Taking negative logarithm on both sides: −log Kw = −log[H⁺] − log[OH⁻] −log(10⁻¹⁴) = pH + pOH 14 = pH + pOH ∴ pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C) For neutral water at 25°C: pH = pOH = 7 For acidic solution: pH < 7, pOH > 7 For basic solution: pH > 7, pOH < 7
Q.11Ostwald's Dilution Law for weak acid and weak base (Derivation)
Answer Ostwald's dilution law relates the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte with the concentration and dissociation constant. For weak acid HA: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ Let C = initial concentration, α = degree of dissociation At equilibrium: [HA] = C(1−α), [H⁺] = Cα, [A⁻] = Cα Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] = (Cα)(Cα)/C(1−α) = Cα²/(1−α) For weak acid (α << 1): (1 − α) ≈ 1 Ka = Cα²  →  α = √(Ka/C) For weak base BOH: BOH ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻ Kb = Cα²  →  α = √(Kb/C) Conclusion: Degree of dissociation increases on dilution (as C decreases, α increases).
Q.12Define degree of dissociation
Answer Degree of dissociation (α) is defined as the fraction of the total number of moles of a substance that dissociate into ions at equilibrium. α = (Moles dissociated) / (Total moles taken initially) • α = 0: no dissociation (strong undissociated compound) • α = 1: complete dissociation • 0 < α < 1: partial dissociation (weak electrolytes) • α increases on dilution (Ostwald's law: α = √(Ka/C)) Unit: dimensionless (it is a ratio)
⚗️ Chemical Kinetics
Q.13Open system and Closed system
Answer Open System: A system that can exchange both matter and energy with the surroundings. Example: Hot soup in an open cup (loses heat and steam), a burning candle. Closed System: A system that can exchange only energy (not matter) with the surroundings. Example: Sealed pressure cooker, gases in a closed cylinder with a piston. Isolated System: (for completeness) No exchange of matter or energy. Example: Thermos flask (ideal).
Q.14Integrated Rate Law for First Order Reaction
Answer For first order reaction: A → Products Rate = −d[A]/dt = k[A] Derivation: d[A]/[A] = −k·dt Integrating both sides from [A]₀ to [A] and 0 to t: ln[A] − ln[A]₀ = −kt ln([A]/[A]₀) = −kt k = (2.303/t) × log([A]₀/[A]) Or in terms of concentration at time t: [A] = [A]₀ × e⁻ᵏᵗ • Unit of k for first order = s⁻¹ (time⁻¹) • Plot of log[A] vs t is a straight line with slope = −k/2.303
Q.15Half life for first order reaction
Answer Half life (t₁/₂) is the time required for the concentration of reactant to reduce to half of its initial value. Derivation: At t = t₁/₂, [A] = [A]₀/2. Substituting in integrated rate law: k = (2.303/t₁/₂) × log([A]₀/([A]₀/2)) k = (2.303/t₁/₂) × log 2 k = (2.303/t₁/₂) × 0.3010 t₁/₂ = 0.693/k Key property: For first order reaction, t₁/₂ is INDEPENDENT of initial concentration. Example: Radioactive decay follows first order kinetics.
Q.16Graphical representation for first order reaction
Answer From integrated rate law: k = (2.303/t) × log([A]₀/[A]) log[A] = log[A]₀ − (k/2.303) × t Graph 1: log[A] vs t • Straight line with negative slope • Slope = −k/2.303  →  k = −2.303 × slope • y-intercept = log[A]₀ Graph 2: [A] vs t • Exponential decay curve • [A] decreases exponentially as time increases Graph 3: rate vs [A] • Straight line through origin (rate ∝ [A]) • Slope = k (rate constant)
Q.39Pseudo first order reaction
Answer A reaction which is not of first order but behaves as a first order reaction under certain conditions (when one reactant is in large excess) is called a pseudo first order reaction. Example: Acid hydrolysis of ethyl acetate (ester) CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH Actual rate = k[ester][H₂O]² (second order) But water is present in large excess → [H₂O] ≈ constant Rate = k'[ester] where k' = k[H₂O] → Reaction appears to be first order (pseudo first order). Another example: Inversion of cane sugar in dilute H₂SO₄ C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆
🔥 Thermodynamics
Q.17Derivation of PV work (Work done by gas)
Answer When a gas expands against an external pressure, it does work on the surroundings. This is called PV work or pressure-volume work. Derivation: Consider a gas in a cylinder with a frictionless piston of cross-sectional area A. External pressure = P_ext. Let gas expand by small distance dx. Force on piston = P_ext × A Work done (dW) = Force × displacement = P_ext × A × dx = P_ext × dV For total expansion from V₁ to V₂: W = ∫P_ext dV = P_ext(V₂ − V₁) = P_ext × ΔV W = −P_ext × ΔV   (negative sign: work done BY system) Sign convention: Work done ON the system = +W. Work done BY the system = −W.
Q.18Derivation of Maximum Work (Reversible isothermal expansion)
Answer Maximum work is obtained when a gas expands reversibly and isothermally (temperature constant). Derivation: For reversible process: P_ext = P (gas pressure at every stage). For ideal gas: P = nRT/V W_max = −∫P dV = −∫(nRT/V) dV W_max = −nRT ∫(dV/V) from V₁ to V₂ W_max = −nRT [ln V]_{V₁}^{V₂} W_max = −nRT ln(V₂/V₁) W_max = −2.303 nRT log(V₂/V₁) Also written as: W_max = −2.303 nRT log(P₁/P₂)  [since V ∝ 1/P at constant T] Reversible work is maximum work because it proceeds through infinite steps in equilibrium.
Q.19State and explain Henry's Law
Answer Henry's law states: The solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid at constant temperature. Mathematical Form: p = K_H × x   or   C = K_H × P where p = partial pressure of gas, x = mole fraction of dissolved gas, K_H = Henry's law constant Explanation: • Higher pressure → more gas molecules collide with liquid surface → more dissolve → higher solubility. • K_H is different for different gases and changes with temperature. • Higher K_H → lower solubility at given pressure. Applications: 1. Carbonated drinks — CO₂ dissolved under high pressure. 2. Scuba diving — increased N₂ solubility at depth causes "bends" on rapid ascent. 3. Oxygen supply in blood — O₂ dissolves in blood proportional to its partial pressure.
Q.20State and explain Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation
Answer Hess's law states: The total enthalpy change (ΔH) for a chemical reaction is the same regardless of the path followed, provided the initial and final states are the same. Explanation: Enthalpy is a state function, so ΔH depends only on initial and final states, NOT on the reaction pathway. Example: Combustion of Carbon Route 1: C + O₂ → CO₂    ΔH₁ = −393.5 kJ Route 2: C + ½O₂ → CO    ΔH₂ = −110.5 kJ CO + ½O₂ → CO₂   ΔH₃ = −283.0 kJ ΔH₂ + ΔH₃ = −110.5 + (−283.0) = −393.5 kJ = ΔH₁ ✓ Applications: • Calculate enthalpy of reactions that cannot be measured directly. • Calculate bond enthalpies, enthalpy of formation.
🔬 Organic Chemistry
Q.21Swarts Reaction
Answer Swarts reaction is a method for the preparation of alkyl fluorides by treating an alkyl chloride or bromide with metallic fluoride (AgF, Hg₂F₂, SbF₃). Reaction: R−Cl + AgF → R−F + AgCl CH₃Cl + AgF → CH₃F + AgCl • Alkyl fluorides cannot be prepared by direct fluorination (too vigorous). • Fluoride ions are released slowly from AgF and replace Cl⁻. Reagent: AgF, Hg₂F₂, or SbF₃ (Swarts reagent)
Q.22Wurtz Reaction
Answer Wurtz reaction is used to prepare higher alkanes by treating an alkyl halide with sodium metal in dry ether. Reaction: 2 R−X + 2Na → R−R + 2NaX  (dry ether) 2 CH₃Br + 2Na → CH₃−CH₃ + 2NaBr 2 C₂H₅Br + 2Na → C₂H₅−C₂H₅ + 2NaBr  (butane) • Gives an alkane with double the number of carbons. • If two different alkyl halides are used, a mixture of three alkanes is obtained. • Not suitable for preparing methane (CH₃Na is unstable).
Q.23SN¹ and SN² Reaction Mechanism
Answer SN¹ (Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution): • Two-step mechanism. Rate = k[RX] (first order) • Step 1: Slow — alkyl halide ionises to form carbocation + X⁻ R−X → R⁺ + X⁻  (slow, rate-determining step) • Step 2: Fast — nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation R⁺ + Nu⁻ → R−Nu  (fast) • Racemization occurs (Nu attacks from both faces). • Favoured by: 3° > 2° > 1° alkyl halides, polar protic solvents. SN² (Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution): • One-step mechanism. Rate = k[RX][Nu] (second order) • Nucleophile attacks from the back (180° to leaving group) → transition state Nu⁻ + R−X → [Nu---R---X]‡ → Nu−R + X⁻ • Walden inversion (complete inversion of configuration). • Favoured by: 1° > 2° > 3° alkyl halides, polar aprotic solvents. • Steric hindrance disfavours SN².
Q.24Preparation of Amines
Answer 1. Reduction of Nitro Compounds: R−NO₂ + 6[H] → R−NH₂ + 2H₂O  (Fe/HCl or Sn/HCl) 2. Reduction of Nitriles: R−C≡N + 4[H] → R−CH₂−NH₂  (LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni) 3. Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides (Hofmann): R−X + NH₃ → R−NH₂ + HX  (excess NH₃, then NaOH) Gives mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary ammonium salts. 4. Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis: (gives pure 1° amine) Phthalimide + KOH → Potassium phthalimide → R−X → N-alkylphthalimide → hydrolysis → 1° amine 5. Hofmann Bromamide Reaction: (1° amine with one less C) RCONH₂ + Br₂ + NaOH → R−NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + NaBr
Q.25Preparation of Phenols by all four methods
Answer 1. From Diazonium Salt (most important): C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + H₂O → C₆H₅OH + N₂ + HCl 2. From Chlorobenzene (Dow's process — high temp & pressure): C₆H₅Cl + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa → C₆H₅OH + NaCl  (300°C, 200 atm) 3. From Benzene Sulphonic Acid (alkali fusion): C₆H₅SO₃H + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa → C₆H₅OH  (fusion, then H⁺) 4. From Cumene (Industrial method — most widely used): C₆H₅CH(CH₃)₂ + O₂ → C₆H₅C(CH₃)₂OOH → C₆H₅OH + CH₃COCH₃ Cumene hydroperoxide is decomposed with dilute H₂SO₄ to give phenol + acetone.
Q.26Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis
Answer Gabriel phthalimide synthesis is used to prepare pure primary amines (1° amines) without any secondary or tertiary amine contamination. Steps: Step 1: Phthalimide + KOH → Potassium phthalimide (K-salt) C₆H₄(CO)₂NH + KOH → C₆H₄(CO)₂NK + H₂O Step 2: K-phthalimide + R−X → N-alkyl phthalimide (alkylation) C₆H₄(CO)₂NK + R−X → C₆H₄(CO)₂N−R + KX Step 3: N-alkyl phthalimide + H₂O/KOH → primary amine + phthalic acid C₆H₄(CO)₂N−R + H₂O/KOH → R−NH₂ + C₆H₄(COOK)₂ Advantage: Gives PURE 1° amine. No 2° or 3° amine formed.
Q.27What is Formalin?
Answer Formalin is a 40% aqueous solution of formaldehyde (methanal, HCHO). Properties: • Colourless liquid with pungent smell. • Strong reducing agent (gives silver mirror with Tollens' reagent). • Undergoes Cannizzaro reaction (no α-hydrogen). Uses: 1. Used as a preservative for biological specimens (kills bacteria). 2. Used as a disinfectant and antiseptic. 3. Used in manufacture of Bakelite (phenol-formaldehyde resin). 4. Used to prepare formic acid and various plastics.
Q.28Kolbe's reaction and Riemer-Tiemann reaction
Answer Kolbe's Reaction (Kolbe-Schmitt reaction): Sodium phenoxide is heated with CO₂ under pressure → sodium salicylate → salicylic acid on acidification. C₆H₅ONa + CO₂ → C₆H₄(OH)(COONa) → C₆H₄(OH)COOH  (salicylic acid) Conditions: 130°C, 5 atm, CO₂ pressure. The −COOH group enters ortho position. Use: Used to prepare aspirin (acetyl salicylic acid) from salicylic acid. Riemer-Tiemann Reaction: Phenol treated with CHCl₃ in presence of NaOH → ortho-hydroxy benzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde). C₆H₅OH + CHCl₃ + NaOH → o-HOC₆H₄CHO (salicylaldehyde) + HCl Mechanism: CHCl₃ + NaOH → :CCl₂ (dichlorocarbene, electrophile) → attacks ring → substitution. Both reactions occur at the ortho position of phenol ring.
Q.29Free radical mechanism involved in preparation of addition polymers
Answer Addition polymerisation by free radical mechanism occurs in three stages: Initiation, Propagation, Termination. Stage 1 — Initiation: Initiator (I−I) → 2I•  (free radicals, by heat/UV/peroxide) I• + CH₂=CH₂ → I−CH₂−CH₂•  (activated monomer) Stage 2 — Propagation: I−CH₂−CH₂• + n(CH₂=CH₂) → I−(CH₂−CH₂)n−CH₂−CH₂• Chain grows by addition of monomer units to the free radical end. Stage 3 — Termination: R• + R• → R−R  (combination) R−CH₂• + •CH₂−R → R−CH₃ + R−CH=CH₂  (disproportionation) Example: Formation of polythene from ethene.
Q.30Preparation, uses and properties of Nylon-6, Nylon-6,6, Bakelite, Teflon
Answer (i) Nylon-6 (Polycaprolactam): n(caprolactam) → [−NH−(CH₂)₅−CO−]n  (ring opening polymerisation) Uses: Fibres, ropes, parachutes, tyre cords, brush bristles. (ii) Nylon-6,6: n H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + n HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH → Nylon-6,6 + nH₂O (Hexamethylenediamine + Adipic acid — condensation polymerisation) Uses: Fabrics, stockings, gears, crankshafts, parachutes. (iii) Bakelite (phenol-formaldehyde resin): Phenol + Formaldehyde → Novolac (linear) → Bakelite (cross-linked, thermosetting) Properties: Hard, infusible, electrical insulator, heat resistant. Uses: Electrical switches, handles of utensils, telephone cabinets. (iv) Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE): n CF₂=CF₂ → [−CF₂−CF₂−]n  (addition polymerisation) Properties: Chemically inert, non-stick, high thermal stability, low friction. Uses: Non-stick cookware coating, electrical insulation, gaskets, valves.
Q.31Halogenation, Nitration, Sulphonation of chlorobenzene and anisole
Answer Chlorobenzene (−Cl is ortho/para director, deactivating): Halogenation: C₆H₅Cl + Cl₂ → o-C₆H₄Cl₂ + p-C₆H₄Cl₂  (Fe/FeCl₃ catalyst) Nitration: C₆H₅Cl + HNO₃ → o-ClC₆H₄NO₂ + p-ClC₆H₄NO₂  (H₂SO₄) Sulphonation: C₆H₅Cl + H₂SO₄ → o-ClC₆H₄SO₃H + p-ClC₆H₄SO₃H Anisole (−OCH₃ is strongly ortho/para activating): Halogenation: C₆H₅OCH₃ + Br₂ → o-BrC₆H₄OCH₃ + p-BrC₆H₄OCH₃  (no catalyst needed) Nitration: C₆H₅OCH₃ + HNO₃ → o-O₂NC₆H₄OCH₃ + p-O₂NC₆H₄OCH₃ Sulphonation: C₆H₅OCH₃ + H₂SO₄ → o-HO₃SC₆H₄OCH₃ + p-HO₃SC₆H₄OCH₃
Q.32Aldol Condensation reaction with mechanism
Answer Aldol condensation: When an aldehyde or ketone having α-hydrogen is treated with dilute base or acid, it forms a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound (aldol), which on heating loses water to form α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound. Reaction (Acetaldehyde): 2 CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO  (Aldol, dilute NaOH, cold) ↓ (heat, −H₂O) CH₃CH=CHCHO  (Crotonaldehyde) Mechanism: Step 1: Base removes α-H → carbanion (enolate) CH₃CHO + OH⁻ → ⁻CH₂CHO + H₂O Step 2: Carbanion attacks carbonyl carbon of another molecule ⁻CH₂CHO + CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(O⁻)CH₂CHO Step 3: Protonation → Aldol product CH₃CH(O⁻)CH₂CHO + H₂O → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO + OH⁻ Step 4: Dehydration on heating → α,β-unsaturated aldehyde
Q.33Clemmensen Reduction
Answer Clemmensen reduction is a method of reducing a carbonyl group (C=O) of an aldehyde or ketone to a methylene group (CH₂) using amalgamated zinc (Zn-Hg) and concentrated HCl. Reaction: R−CO−R' + Zn(Hg) + conc. HCl → R−CH₂−R' + ZnCl₂ + H₂O CH₃COCH₃ + Zn(Hg)/HCl → CH₃CH₂CH₃  (propane) C₆H₅COCH₃ + Zn(Hg)/HCl → C₆H₅CH₂CH₃  (ethylbenzene) • Used when molecule is sensitive to base (complement to Wolff-Kishner). • Works in acidic conditions. • Does NOT affect C=C double bonds.
Q.34Cannizzaro Reaction with mechanism
Answer Cannizzaro reaction is a disproportionation reaction in which an aldehyde having no α-hydrogen is treated with concentrated NaOH. One molecule is oxidised to a carboxylic acid (salt) and another is reduced to an alcohol. Reaction: 2HCHO + NaOH(conc) → HCOONa + CH₃OH 2C₆H₅CHO + NaOH(conc) → C₆H₅COONa + C₆H₅CH₂OH Mechanism: Step 1: OH⁻ attacks one HCHO → alkoxide intermediate HCHO + OH⁻ → H₂C(OH)(O⁻)  (nucleophilic addition) Step 2: Hydride (H⁻) transfer from the intermediate to another HCHO H₂C(OH)(O⁻) + HCHO → HCOO⁻ + CH₃O⁻ Step 3: Protonation of alkoxide CH₃O⁻ + H₂O → CH₃OH + OH⁻
Q.35Esterification Reaction
Answer Esterification is the reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol in the presence of a mineral acid catalyst (H₂SO₄) to form an ester and water. Reaction: RCOOH + R'OH ⇌ RCOOR' + H₂O  (H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat) CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O  (ethyl acetate) Mechanism (Nucleophilic Acyl Substitution): Step 1: Protonation of −COOH by H₂SO₄ → activated acid Step 2: Alcohol (nucleophile) attacks the activated carbonyl carbon Step 3: Loss of water molecule → ester formed • Reversible reaction — equilibrium can be shifted by removing water or using excess of one reagent. • Used to prepare soaps, flavours, perfumes.
Q.36Wolff-Kishner Reduction
Answer Wolff-Kishner reduction is a method to reduce a carbonyl group (C=O) to methylene group (−CH₂−) by treating with hydrazine (NH₂NH₂) in the presence of KOH (base) and ethylene glycol (high boiling solvent). Reaction: R−CO−R' + NH₂NH₂ → R−C(=NNH₂)−R'  (hydrazone, first step) R−C(=NNH₂)−R' + KOH → R−CH₂−R' + N₂  (Wolff-Kishner, basic conditions) C₆H₅COCH₃ + NH₂NH₂/KOH → C₆H₅CH₂CH₃  (ethylbenzene) • Used in basic/alkaline conditions (complement to Clemmensen in acidic conditions). • Works when molecule is acid-sensitive. • Both Clemmensen and Wolff-Kishner give the same product.

Thermoplastic PolymersThermosetting Polymers
Can be softened on heating and hardened on cooling repeatedlyOnce set hard on heating, cannot be softened again
Linear or slightly branched chainsHighly cross-linked 3D network structure
Held by weak van der Waals forcesStrong covalent cross-links
Can be remoulded and recycledCannot be remoulded or recycled
Softer, less resistant to heatHarder, more heat resistant
Examples: Polyethylene, PVC, Nylon, TeflonExamples: Bakelite, Melamine, Urea-formaldehyde
Q.38What is Green Chemistry? b) Sustainable development
Answer (a) Green Chemistry: Green chemistry is a philosophy that designs chemical products and processes to reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. Principles: 1. Prevention — prevent waste rather than treat it. 2. Atom economy — design synthesis to incorporate maximum raw material into final product. 3. Use of catalysts — avoid excess reagents. 4. Design for degradation — products should break down into harmless substances. 5. Use renewable feedstocks. 6. Design safer synthesis and chemicals. (b) Sustainable Development: Development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. • Balances economic growth, environmental protection, and social equity. • Uses renewable energy, minimises pollution, conserves biodiversity. • Example: Solar energy, wind energy, biodegradable plastics, organic farming.
