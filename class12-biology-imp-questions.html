Reproduction in Lower and Higher Plants

⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What is vegetative propagation? Give two examples.★★
Show Answer ▾
Vegetative propagation is a type of asexual reproduction in plants where new plants develop from vegetative parts (root, stem, leaf) without involving seeds or gametes.
Examples:
  • Potato — stem tubers (eyes of potato develop into new plants)
  • Bryophyllum — adventitious buds on leaf margins develop into new plants
Q2
Distinguish between microsporogenesis and megasporogenesis.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Microsporogenesis: Formation of microspores (pollen grains) from microspore mother cell (MMC) by meiosis inside the anther. MMC (2n) → meiosis → 4 haploid microspores.
  • Megasporogenesis: Formation of megaspores from megaspore mother cell (MMC) by meiosis inside the ovule. MMC (2n) → meiosis → 4 megaspores; 3 degenerate, 1 functional.
Q3
What is double fertilization? Where does it occur?★★
Show Answer ▾
Double fertilization is a unique feature of angiosperms discovered by Nawaschin (1898). It occurs in the embryo sac (female gametophyte) inside the ovule.
It involves two simultaneous events:
  • Syngamy: One male gamete fuses with the egg cell → diploid Zygote (2n)
  • Triple fusion: Second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei → triploid Primary Endosperm Nucleus (PEN) (3n)
Both fusions together are called double fertilization.
Q4
Define pollination. State its two types.★★
Show Answer ▾
Pollination: Transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of a flower. It is essential for fertilization.
Types:
  • Self-pollination (Autogamy): Pollen transferred to stigma of same flower or same plant. E.g., Wheat, Rice.
  • Cross-pollination (Xenogamy): Pollen transferred to stigma of a different plant of same species. Increases genetic variation.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain the structure of a typical angiosperm ovule.★★★
Show Answer ▾
An ovule is attached to the placenta by a stalk called funiculus. The junction of funiculus and ovule is hilum.
Structure:
  • Integuments: Two protective layers surrounding the ovule — outer and inner integument
  • Micropyle: Small opening at the tip where pollen tube enters
  • Chalaza: Basal part opposite to micropyle
  • Nucellus: Central mass of cells with stored food; surrounds the embryo sac
  • Embryo sac: 7-celled, 8-nucleated female gametophyte embedded in nucellus
In anatropous ovule (most common), the body is inverted so micropyle faces the funiculus.
Q2
Describe the process of triple fusion with its significance.★★
Show Answer ▾
Triple fusion is part of double fertilization in angiosperms.
Process: One male gamete (n) travels through pollen tube and fuses with the two polar nuclei (n + n) present in the central cell of embryo sac → forms triploid (3n) Primary Endosperm Nucleus (PEN).
Significance:
  • PEN divides to form endosperm which provides nutrition to developing embryo
  • Ensures that endosperm develops only when fertilization occurs — no wastage of nutrients
  • Triploid endosperm is unique to angiosperms
Q3
Explain the role of wind and insects as pollinating agents.★★
Show Answer ▾
Wind pollination (Anemophily):
  • Flowers are small, inconspicuous, no colour/scent/nectar
  • Pollen is light, dry, smooth, non-sticky — produced in huge quantities
  • Stigma is feathery/branched to trap pollen
  • Stamens exserted with long filaments
  • Examples: Wheat, Rice, Corn, Maize, Coconut
Insect pollination (Entomophily):
  • Flowers are large, bright coloured, fragrant with nectar to attract insects
  • Pollen is sticky, spiny to attach to insect body
  • Examples: Rose, Sunflower, Bougainvillea
Q4
What is apomixis? Explain its significance in agriculture.★★
Show Answer ▾
Apomixis: Production of seeds without fertilization. The embryo develops without fusion of gametes. E.g., Grasses, Citrus, Mango.
Types:
  • Recurrent apomixis — from diploid egg cell
  • Non-recurrent apomixis — from haploid egg without fertilization
  • Adventive embryony — embryo from nucellus or integument cells (polyembryony)
Significance in agriculture:
  • Maintains superior hybrid characters generation after generation without segregation
  • Farmers can use hybrid seeds without buying new seeds every year
  • Reduces cost of seed production
  • Preserves genetic uniformity
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the development of male gametophyte in angiosperms.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Male gametophyte = Pollen grain
Microsporogenesis: Microspore Mother Cell (MMC, 2n) in pollen sac undergoes meiosis → tetrad of 4 haploid microspores. Each microspore = immature male gametophyte.
Development:
  • Microspore nucleus divides by mitosis (1st pollen mitosis) → large vegetative cell + small generative cell
  • Generative cell divides again (2nd pollen mitosis) → 2 male gametes (sperms)
  • This may occur in pollen grain (3-celled pollen) or in pollen tube after germination (2-celled pollen)
Structure of mature pollen grain:
  • Two-layered wall: outer thick exine (sporopollenin — non-biodegradable) + inner thin intine (cellulose)
  • Germ pores in exine for pollen tube germination
  • Exine may be smooth or sculptured (species-specific)
Pollen tube: Germinates through germ pore, grows through style to reach ovule. Vegetative cell nucleus guides pollen tube. Two male gametes carried to embryo sac for double fertilization.
Q2
Explain the development of female gametophyte (embryo sac) in angiosperms.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Female gametophyte = Embryo sac
Megasporogenesis: Megaspore Mother Cell (MMC, 2n) inside nucellus of ovule undergoes meiosis → 4 haploid megaspores arranged in a row. 3 megaspores degenerate; 1 functional megaspore (chalazal end) survives.
Development of embryo sac (Polygonum type):
  • Functional megaspore nucleus divides 3 times by mitosis → 8 nuclei
  • 8 nuclei arranged in 7 cells: 3 at micropylar end (egg apparatus) + 3 at chalazal end (antipodal cells) + 2 polar nuclei in large central cell
Structure of mature embryo sac (7-celled, 8-nucleated):
  • Egg apparatus: 1 Egg cell + 2 Synergids (have filiform apparatus — helps guide pollen tube)
  • Central cell: Has 2 polar nuclei (fuse to form secondary nucleus)
  • Antipodal cells: 3 cells at chalazal end — nutritive function
After fertilization: Egg → Zygote → Embryo; PEN → Endosperm; Ovule → Seed; Ovary → Fruit.

⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
Define oogenesis. Where does it occur?★★
Show Answer ▾
Oogenesis: The process of formation and development of female gametes (ova/eggs) from oogonia present in the ovary.
Location: It occurs in the germinal epithelium of the ovary.
Steps:
  • Oogonium (2n) → Primary oocyte (2n) by mitosis
  • Primary oocyte undergoes meiosis I → Secondary oocyte (n) + 1st polar body
  • Secondary oocyte undergoes meiosis II → Ovum (n) + 2nd polar body
Oogenesis begins at birth but completes only at fertilization.
Q2
Distinguish between oviparity and viviparity with one example each.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Oviparity: Animals that lay eggs. Embryo development occurs outside the mother's body. E.g., Hen, Frog, Lizard, Fish.
  • Viviparity: Animals that give birth to young ones. Embryo develops inside mother's uterus and is nourished through placenta. E.g., Humans, Cows, Dogs.
Viviparity provides better protection to the embryo; oviparity produces more offspring at once.
Q3
What is the significance of meiosis in sexual reproduction?★★
Show Answer ▾
Significance of meiosis:
  • Reduces chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n) → produces gametes
  • Maintains constant chromosome number in species across generations
  • Crossing over during meiosis I creates new gene combinations → genetic variation
  • Genetic variation is the raw material for evolution and natural selection
Q4
What is parthenogenesis? Give one example.★
Show Answer ▾
Parthenogenesis: Development of an egg into a new individual without fertilization by a sperm.
  • Natural parthenogenesis: Honeybee — unfertilized eggs develop into haploid drones (males); fertilized eggs develop into diploid queens or workers (females)
  • Also seen in: Aphids, Rotifers, some Lizards
Artificial parthenogenesis: Induced by chemicals or physical stimuli in laboratory.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the process of spermatogenesis.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Spermatogenesis: Formation of sperms from spermatogonia in the seminiferous tubules of testes.
Steps:
  • Spermatogonia (2n) — germ cells lining seminiferous tubules, multiply by mitosis
  • Some spermatogonia grow → Primary spermatocytes (2n)
  • Primary spermatocyte → Meiosis I → 2 Secondary spermatocytes (n)
  • Each secondary spermatocyte → Meiosis II → 2 Spermatids (n)
  • 4 Spermatids → Spermiogenesis → 4 Spermatozoa (mature sperms)
Parts of sperm: Head (nucleus + acrosome) + Middle piece (mitochondria — energy for motility) + Tail (flagellum — movement).
Q2
Explain the menstrual cycle with the role of hormones FSH, LH, estrogen and progesterone.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Menstrual cycle: Cyclic changes in the female reproductive system, approximately 28 days.
Phases:
  • Menstrual phase (Day 1–5): Breakdown of uterine lining (menstruation); falling levels of progesterone and estrogen
  • Follicular phase (Day 1–13): FSH from pituitary stimulates ovarian follicle to grow; follicle secretes estrogen → endometrium thickens
  • Ovulation (Day 14): Surge of LH triggers release of ovum from mature Graafian follicle
  • Luteal phase (Day 15–28): Ruptured follicle → corpus luteum → secretes progesterone → maintains endometrium for implantation; if no fertilization → corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → menstruation begins
Q3
What is IVF? Explain its steps briefly.★★
Show Answer ▾
IVF (In Vitro Fertilization): Fertilization of egg and sperm outside the human body in laboratory conditions. Commonly known as "test tube baby".
Steps:
  • Ovaries stimulated with hormones to produce multiple eggs
  • Eggs collected from ovary by laparoscopy
  • Fertilized with sperm in a Petri dish (in vitro)
  • Fertilized egg develops into 8-cell embryo
  • Embryo transferred into uterus of the mother for implantation
Use: Helps couples with blocked fallopian tubes, low sperm count, or unexplained infertility.
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the structure of human male reproductive system.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Male reproductive system consists of:
  • Testes (pair): Primary sex organs located in scrotal sac outside abdomen (lower temperature needed for sperm production). Contain seminiferous tubules (sperm production) and interstitial cells/Leydig cells (testosterone secretion)
  • Epididymis: Coiled tube where sperms mature and are stored temporarily
  • Vas deferens: Muscular tube carrying sperms from epididymis to urethra
  • Seminal vesicles: Secrete fluid rich in fructose (energy for sperm)
  • Prostate gland: Secretes alkaline fluid that neutralises acidity of urine in urethra
  • Cowper's gland (Bulbourethral gland): Secretes lubricating fluid
  • Penis: External organ for copulation and urination. Contains urethra.
Semen = Sperm + secretions of seminal vesicle + prostate + Cowper's gland. Normal sperm count: 200–300 million per ejaculation.
Q2
Describe the structure of human female reproductive system.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Female reproductive system consists of:
  • Ovaries (pair): Primary sex organs; produce eggs (oogenesis) and hormones (estrogen, progesterone). Each ovary has cortex (follicles) and medulla.
  • Fallopian tubes (Oviducts): Pair of tubes connecting ovary to uterus. Fertilization occurs here. Funnel-shaped infundibulum with fimbriae collects ovum at ovulation.
  • Uterus (Womb): Pear-shaped muscular organ where embryo implants and develops. Three layers: Perimetrium (outer), Myometrium (muscular middle), Endometrium (inner lining — sheds during menstruation)
  • Cervix: Lower narrow part of uterus opening into vagina
  • Vagina: Birth canal; receives penis during copulation
External genitalia: Labia majora, Labia minora, Clitoris, Hymen.

Genetics
Inheritance and Variation

⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
State Mendel's Law of Segregation.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Mendel's Law of Segregation (Law of Purity of Gametes):
"Each individual has two alleles for each character. During gamete formation, the two alleles of each character segregate (separate) from each other so that each gamete receives only one allele."
The alleles remain pure and do not blend — they are expressed in the same original form in the next generation. This is also called the Law of Purity of Gametes.
Q2
What is incomplete dominance? Give one example with expected ratio.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Incomplete dominance: When neither allele is completely dominant over the other; the heterozygous phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygous phenotypes.
Example: Flower colour in Snapdragon (Antirrhinum):
  • RR (red) × rr (white) → F1: Rr (Pink) — intermediate colour
  • F2: 1 RR (Red) : 2 Rr (Pink) : 1 rr (White) — Phenotypic ratio 1:2:1
Here the phenotypic ratio = genotypic ratio = 1:2:1 (unlike normal 3:1 in complete dominance).
Q3
What is co-dominance? Give example of ABO blood group.★★
Show Answer ▾
Co-dominance: Both alleles are equally expressed in the heterozygous condition — neither is dominant over the other.
Example: ABO blood group — controlled by gene I with three alleles: IA, IB, i.
  • IAIB genotype → Blood group AB — both A and B antigens expressed simultaneously on RBCs
  • This is co-dominance because both IA and IB are fully expressed
Q4
Distinguish between genotype and phenotype.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Genotype: The genetic constitution of an organism — the actual alleles present in its cells. E.g., TT, Tt, tt. Cannot always be seen directly.
  • Phenotype: The observable physical expression of the genotype — the visible characteristics. E.g., Tall, Dwarf. Depends on genotype AND environment.
Two organisms can have same phenotype (Tall) but different genotype (TT or Tt).
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain monohybrid cross with Punnett square. State F2 ratios.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Monohybrid cross: Cross between parents differing in one pair of contrasting characters.
Example: Tall (TT) × Dwarf (tt)
F1: All Tt (Tall) — only dominant character expressed.
F1 × F1: Tt × Tt
Punnett square:
T t | T → TT, Tt | t → Tt, tt
F2 Genotypic ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt
F2 Phenotypic ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf (3:1)
This cross demonstrated Mendel's Law of Segregation — alleles separate during gamete formation.
Q2
What is sex-linked inheritance? Explain colour blindness as an example.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Sex-linked inheritance: Inheritance of characters whose genes are located on sex chromosomes (X or Y). Most sex-linked genes are on X chromosome (X-linked).
Colour blindness: X-linked recessive disorder — inability to distinguish red and green colours. Gene for colour vision is on X chromosome.
  • XB = normal vision (dominant); Xb = colour blind (recessive)
  • Normal female: XBXB; Carrier female: XBXb; Colour blind female: XbXb
  • Normal male: XBY; Colour blind male: XbY
Males need only one defective allele to be colour blind; females need two. So colour blindness is more common in males (affects ~8% males, ~0.4% females).
Q3
What are chromosomal disorders? Explain Down's syndrome and Turner's syndrome.★★
Show Answer ▾
Chromosomal disorders: Abnormalities caused by changes in number or structure of chromosomes.
  • Down's syndrome (Trisomy 21): Extra chromosome 21 → 47 chromosomes total (2n=47). Caused by non-disjunction during meiosis. Features: small round face, mental retardation, short stature, broad short neck, epicanthal eye folds. More common with advanced maternal age.
  • Turner's syndrome (45, XO): Only one X chromosome, no second sex chromosome. Features: Females with underdeveloped ovaries (sterile), short stature, webbed neck, shield-shaped chest. 45 chromosomes.
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain the chromosomal theory of inheritance. Who proposed it? What evidence supported it?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance was proposed by Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri (1902–1903). It states that chromosomes are the carriers of genes and genes are located on chromosomes in a linear order.
Parallel behaviour of chromosomes and Mendel's factors:
  • Both occur in pairs in diploid organisms
  • Both segregate during gamete formation (meiosis)
  • One from each pair is contributed to offspring by each parent
  • Both assort independently during gamete formation
Evidence:
  • Morgan's experiments on Drosophila showed genes on same chromosome are linked
  • Discovery of sex chromosomes (X and Y) explained sex determination
  • Morgan showed crossing over occurs between linked genes — recombination
  • Concept of genetic maps (chromosome maps) confirmed gene location on chromosomes

⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What is Chargaff's rule? State it.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Chargaff's Rule (Base Pairing Rule): In a double-stranded DNA molecule:
  • Amount of Adenine (A) = Amount of Thymine (T) → A = T
  • Amount of Guanine (G) = Amount of Cytosine (C) → G = C
  • Therefore: Purines (A + G) = Pyrimidines (T + C)
This rule confirmed that A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds) and G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds) in the double helix. Proposed by Erwin Chargaff in 1950.
Q2
Distinguish between DNA and RNA — any 4 points.★★★
Show Answer ▾

Sugar = DeoxyriboseSugar = Ribose
Base: Adenine, Guanine, Cytosine, ThymineBase: Adenine, Guanine, Cytosine, Uracil

Q3
What is the Central Dogma of molecular biology?★★
Show Answer ▾
Central Dogma was proposed by Francis Crick in 1958. It describes the flow of genetic information in a cell:
DNA → RNA → Protein
  • Transcription: DNA → mRNA (in nucleus)
  • Translation: mRNA → Protein (in cytoplasm on ribosomes)
In some viruses (retroviruses like HIV), RNA is reverse-transcribed into DNA — this is called Reverse Transcription (Central Dogma exception, shown by Temin and Baltimore).
Q4
What is a codon? Give examples of initiation and termination codons.★★
Show Answer ▾
Codon: A triplet sequence of three nucleotide bases on mRNA that codes for a specific amino acid (or signals start/stop of protein synthesis). There are 64 possible codons.
  • Initiation codon: AUG — codes for Methionine; signals the start of protein synthesis (Start codon)
  • Termination codons (Stop codons): UAA, UAG, UGA — do not code for any amino acid; signal the end of protein synthesis
The genetic code is degenerate (multiple codons for same amino acid), universal (same in all organisms), and non-overlapping.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the Watson-Crick model of DNA.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Proposed by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953 (Double Helix model).
Key features:
  • DNA is a double helix — two antiparallel polynucleotide chains wound around each other
  • The two strands run in antiparallel direction (one 5'→3', other 3'→5')
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone is on the outside; nitrogenous bases project inward
  • Bases are held together by hydrogen bonds: A=T (2 bonds), G≡C (3 bonds)
  • The helix makes a complete turn every 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs per turn; pitch = 3.4 nm; diameter = 2 nm
  • Two grooves: Major groove and minor groove
This model explained DNA replication, mutation, and gene expression.
Q2
Explain the process of DNA replication. Name the enzymes involved.★★★
Show Answer ▾
DNA replication is semi-conservative (each new DNA has one old + one new strand) — proved by Meselson and Stahl (1958).
Process:
  • Initiation: Replication begins at specific site called origin of replication. Helicase unwinds and separates the two strands.
  • Priming: RNA Primase adds short RNA primer to provide 3'-OH for DNA synthesis
  • Elongation: DNA Polymerase III adds complementary nucleotides in 5'→3' direction. Leading strand synthesized continuously; lagging strand synthesized as Okazaki fragments.
  • Termination: RNA primers removed by DNA Polymerase I; gaps filled; Okazaki fragments joined by DNA Ligase
Enzymes: Helicase, Primase, DNA Polymerase I & III, DNA Ligase, Topoisomerase.
Q3
What is the lac operon model? Explain with a diagram.★★
Show Answer ▾
Lac operon was proposed by Jacob and Monod (1961) in E. coli. It is an example of gene regulation by negative control.
Components:
  • Regulatory gene (i): Codes for repressor protein
  • Promoter (P): RNA polymerase binding site
  • Operator (O): Where repressor binds
  • Structural genes (lac Z, lac Y, lac A): Code for β-galactosidase, permease, transacetylase
Working:
  • Absence of lactose: Repressor binds to operator → blocks transcription → enzymes NOT produced (off)
  • Presence of lactose: Lactose = inducer; binds to repressor → repressor cannot bind operator → RNA polymerase transcribes → enzymes produced (on)
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the process of transcription in prokaryotes.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Transcription: Synthesis of mRNA from DNA template. In prokaryotes, it occurs in cytoplasm (no nuclear envelope).
Enzyme: RNA Polymerase (single enzyme for all RNA types)
Strands:
  • Template strand (antisense/coding 3'→5'): used as template by RNA polymerase
  • Coding strand (sense/non-template 5'→3'): same sequence as mRNA (except T→U)
Steps:
  • Initiation: RNA polymerase + sigma factor binds to promoter region (TATA box/Pribnow box). DNA unwinds locally.
  • Elongation: RNA polymerase moves along template strand 3'→5'. Adds complementary ribonucleotides (A, U, G, C) in 5'→3' direction. mRNA forms.
  • Termination: RNA polymerase reaches terminator sequence. mRNA is released. Rho factor (ρ) or intrinsic termination stops transcription.
In prokaryotes, translation begins even before transcription is complete (coupled transcription-translation).
Q2
Explain the process of translation (protein synthesis) in detail.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Translation: Synthesis of polypeptide chain using information in mRNA. Occurs on ribosomes in cytoplasm.
Components needed: mRNA, tRNA (adaptor molecule), Ribosomes, Amino acids, ATP, GTP, initiation/elongation/termination factors.
Steps:
  • Activation: Each amino acid activated and attached to specific tRNA by Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. tRNA has anticodon that recognises mRNA codon.
  • Initiation: Small ribosomal subunit binds to mRNA at 5' cap/AUG codon. Initiator tRNA (met-tRNA) binds AUG. Large subunit joins. P-site occupied by initiator tRNA.
  • Elongation: New aminoacyl-tRNA enters A-site; peptide bond forms between amino acids (peptidyl transferase activity); ribosome moves one codon = translocation; previous tRNA moves to E-site and exits.
  • Termination: Stop codon (UAA/UAG/UGA) in A-site. Release factor binds. Polypeptide released. Ribosome dissociates.

Evolution
Origin and Evolution of Life
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What was the Miller-Urey experiment? What did it prove?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Conducted by Stanley Miller and Harold Urey (1953). They simulated early Earth conditions in a closed flask:
  • Gases: CH₄, NH₃, H₂, H₂O (no oxygen — reducing atmosphere)
  • Electric sparks simulated lightning; heat applied
  • After one week: simple organic molecules formed including amino acids (glycine, alanine)
Proved: Organic molecules (building blocks of life) can be formed spontaneously from inorganic molecules under primitive Earth conditions. This supported the chemical evolution theory (Oparin-Haldane hypothesis).
Q2
Distinguish between homologous and analogous organs with examples.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Homologous organs: Same basic structure and embryonic origin but different functions. Indicates divergent evolution (common ancestry). E.g., forelimbs of whale (flipper), bat (wing), horse (leg), human (arm) — all have same bone arrangement (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals).
  • Analogous organs: Different basic structure and origin but perform same function. Indicates convergent evolution. E.g., wings of bat (modified forelimb, bone + membrane) and wings of butterfly (outgrowth of body wall, chitin).
Q3
What is adaptive radiation? Give an example of Darwin's finches.★★
Show Answer ▾
Adaptive radiation: Evolution of different species from a single ancestral species by adapting to different ecological niches.
Darwin's finches: Darwin observed 14 species of finches on Galapagos Islands, all evolved from a single ancestral finch that arrived from South America. Each species developed different beak shapes suited to different food sources:
  • Large ground finch — thick beak for cracking hard seeds
  • Woodpecker finch — long beak for probing bark for insects
  • Warbler finch — slender beak for catching insects
This is the best example of adaptive radiation and divergent evolution.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain Oparin-Haldane theory (chemical evolution / primordial soup theory).★★★
Show Answer ▾
Proposed independently by A.I. Oparin (1924, Russia) and J.B.S. Haldane (1929, UK).
Main points:
  • Early Earth had a reducing atmosphere — rich in CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, H₂; no free oxygen
  • Energy from UV rays, lightning, heat caused simple inorganic molecules to combine into simple organic molecules (amino acids, sugars)
  • These accumulated in oceans → "Primordial Soup" or "Hot Dilute Soup"
  • Organic molecules combined to form large molecules (proteins, nucleic acids)
  • These formed colloidal aggregates called Coacervates (Oparin) or Protobionts — precursors of cells
  • Eventually enclosed in lipid membrane → primitive cells → first life
Supported by: Miller-Urey experiment (1953) which produced amino acids from inorganic gases.
Q2
Compare Lamarck's theory and Darwin's theory of evolution.★★★

Botany
Plant Water Relation
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
Define osmosis. Distinguish between osmosis and diffusion.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Osmosis: Movement of water (solvent) molecules through a semipermeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration (lower solute) to lower water concentration (higher solute) until equilibrium.
  • Diffusion: Movement of any substance (solid, liquid, gas) from high concentration to low concentration; does NOT require a membrane; passive process.
  • Osmosis: Movement of ONLY solvent (water) molecules; REQUIRES a semipermeable membrane; driven by water potential gradient.
Q2
What is plasmolysis? Under what conditions does it occur?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Plasmolysis: Shrinkage of protoplasm away from the cell wall when a plant cell is placed in a hypertonic (more concentrated) solution. Water leaves the cell by osmosis → protoplast shrinks → cell becomes plasmolyzed.
Condition: Occurs when the external solution is hypertonic (higher solute concentration) compared to cell sap.
Deplasmolysis: When plasmolyzed cell is placed back in hypotonic solution → water enters → protoplast expands back. First observed by De Vries.
Q3
What is transpiration pull theory? Who proposed it?★★
Show Answer ▾
Transpiration pull theory (Cohesion-Tension theory) was proposed by Dixon and Joly (1894).
Water evaporates from stomata (transpiration) → creates tension/negative pressure in leaf mesophyll cells → water pulled up through xylem vessels by cohesion (water molecules stick to each other) and adhesion (water sticks to xylem walls) → water absorbed from soil by roots. This tension (suction) is the driving force for water ascent in plants.
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain the mechanism of opening and closing of stomata.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Stomata are pores on leaf surface surrounded by kidney-shaped guard cells.
Opening of stomata (in light):
  • Light activates proton pumps in guard cell membrane → H⁺ pumped out
  • K⁺ ions enter guard cells from neighbouring epidermal cells (active transport)
  • Water potential of guard cells decreases → water enters guard cells by osmosis
  • Guard cells become turgid → inner thick wall stretches less → outer wall bulges → stomata open
Closing of stomata (in dark/drought):
  • K⁺ ions leave guard cells → water potential increases
  • Water moves out of guard cells → cells become flaccid → stomata close
  • ABA (Abscisic acid) triggers stomatal closure during water stress

Plant Growth and Mineral Nutrition
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What are plant growth regulators? Name and classify them.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Plant growth regulators (PGRs) are chemical substances that regulate plant growth and development at very low concentrations. Also called phytohormones.
Promoters (stimulate growth):
  • Auxins (IAA) — cell elongation, apical dominance
  • Gibberellins (GA₃) — stem elongation, seed germination
  • Cytokinins (kinetin) — cell division, delay senescence
Inhibitors (inhibit growth):
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) — stomatal closure, dormancy, stress response
  • Ethylene — fruit ripening, senescence, abscission
Q2
What is photoperiodism? Define short-day and long-day plants with one example each.★★
Show Answer ▾
Photoperiodism: The response of plants to the relative length of day (light period) and night (dark period) that controls flowering. Term coined by Garner and Allard (1920).
  • Short-day plants (SDP): Flower when day length is shorter than a critical period (need long nights). E.g., Chrysanthemum, Tobacco, Xanthium, Rice.
  • Long-day plants (LDP): Flower when day length is longer than a critical period (need short nights). E.g., Wheat, Barley, Spinach, Lettuce.
  • Day-neutral plants: Flowering not affected by day length. E.g., Tomato, Maize, Cucumber.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
What is nitrogen fixation? Explain biological nitrogen fixation with the role of Rhizobium.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Nitrogen fixation: Conversion of atmospheric N₂ into ammonia (NH₃) or other nitrogen compounds that plants can use.
Biological nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium:
  • Rhizobium is a symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacterium found in root nodules of leguminous plants (Pea, Bean, Groundnut)
  • Plant provides carbohydrates (energy) to Rhizobium; Rhizobium fixes N₂ for the plant
  • Enzyme: Nitrogenase (requires anaerobic conditions, Mg²⁺, ATP)
  • Reaction: N₂ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ + 16ATP → 2NH₃ + H₂ + 16ADP + 16Pi
  • Leghemoglobin in root nodules protects nitrogenase from O₂
Free-living nitrogen fixers: Azotobacter (aerobic), Clostridium (anaerobic), Anabaena, Nostoc.

Zoology
Respiration and Circulation
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What is tidal volume and vital capacity? Give their normal values.★★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Tidal Volume (TV): Volume of air inhaled or exhaled in one normal breath at rest. Normal value = 500 mL (0.5 L).
  • Vital Capacity (VC): Maximum volume of air a person can expire after maximum inspiration. VC = IRV + TV + ERV. Normal value = 3400–4800 mL (~4.5 L in adults). It indicates the functional capacity of lungs.
Q2
What is the role of haemoglobin in oxygen transport?★★
Show Answer ▾
Haemoglobin (Hb) is a red, iron-containing protein in RBCs that transports oxygen.
  • Each Hb molecule has 4 polypeptide chains each with a heme group containing Fe²⁺
  • In lungs (high pO₂): O₂ binds to Fe²⁺ of heme → forms Oxyhaemoglobin (HbO₂): Hb + 4O₂ → Hb(O₂)₄
  • In tissues (low pO₂, high pCO₂): O₂ released → Deoxyhaemoglobin formed
  • 1g of Hb carries ~1.34 mL O₂. Blood with 15g/dL Hb carries ~20 mL O₂ per 100 mL blood.
  • CO has 200× higher affinity for Hb than O₂ → forms carboxyhaemoglobin (toxic)
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the mechanism of breathing — inspiration and expiration.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Breathing = Inspiration (inhalation) + Expiration (exhalation) — driven by pressure differences.
Inspiration (active process):
  • Diaphragm contracts → flattens downward
  • External intercostal muscles contract → ribs move up and out
  • Thoracic volume increases → lung volume increases → intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric
  • Air flows INTO lungs
Expiration (passive process):
  • Diaphragm relaxes → domes upward
  • Internal intercostal muscles relax → ribs move down and in
  • Thoracic volume decreases → intrapulmonary pressure rises above atmospheric
  • Air flows OUT of lungs
Q2
Explain the conducting system of the human heart — SA node, AV node, Bundle of His, Purkinje fibres.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Conducting system of heart ensures coordinated contraction:
  • SA node (Sinoatrial node): Located in right atrium wall; called pacemaker of heart — generates electrical impulse automatically at 70–72 beats/min; impulse spreads to both atria → atria contract
  • AV node (Atrioventricular node): Located at junction of atria and ventricles; delays impulse slightly (0.1 sec) to allow atria to empty before ventricles contract; only pathway for impulse to reach ventricles
  • Bundle of His: Bundle of conducting fibres in interventricular septum; carries impulse from AV node down to ventricles
  • Purkinje fibres: Network of fibres spreading through ventricular walls; carry impulse to all parts of ventricles → both ventricles contract simultaneously
⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the structure of human heart with the cardiac cycle.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Heart structure: Cone-shaped muscular organ in thoracic cavity, slightly left of midline. Size of a fist. Protected by pericardium (double membrane with pericardial fluid).
4 chambers:
  • Right atrium — receives deoxygenated blood from body via superior and inferior vena cava
  • Right ventricle — pumps deoxygenated blood to lungs via pulmonary artery
  • Left atrium — receives oxygenated blood from lungs via pulmonary veins
  • Left ventricle — pumps oxygenated blood to body via aorta (thickest wall)
Valves: Tricuspid (right AV), Bicuspid/Mitral (left AV), Pulmonary and Aortic semilunar valves.
Cardiac cycle (0.8 sec):
  • Atrial systole (0.1 sec): Atria contract → blood pushed to ventricles
  • Ventricular systole (0.3 sec): Ventricles contract → blood pumped to lungs and body
  • Joint diastole (0.4 sec): All chambers relax and refill
Heart sounds: "Lubb" (AV valves close) + "Dupp" (semilunar valves close).

Q1
What is a reflex action? Name the components of a reflex arc.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Reflex action: An automatic, involuntary, rapid response to a stimulus that does not involve conscious thinking. Mediated by spinal cord.
Components of reflex arc:
  • 1. Receptor — detects stimulus (e.g., pain receptor in skin)
  • 2. Afferent (sensory) neuron — carries impulse to spinal cord
  • 3. Nerve centre — in spinal cord (integration centre)
  • 4. Efferent (motor) neuron — carries impulse from spinal cord to effector
  • 5. Effector — muscle or gland that responds (e.g., withdrawing hand)

Name the hormones secreted by the pituitary gland (anterior and posterior).★★

  • GH (Growth Hormone) — body growth
  • TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)
  • ACTH (Adrenocorticotrophic Hormone)
  • FSH (Follicle Stimulating Hormone)
  • LH (Luteinizing Hormone)
  • Prolactin — milk production
Posterior pituitary (Neurohypophysis):
  • ADH / Vasopressin — water reabsorption in kidneys

⬤ 5 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the structure of human brain — forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Human brain is protected by skull (cranium), meninges (3 layers), and CSF (cerebrospinal fluid).
Forebrain:
  • Cerebrum: Largest part; 2 hemispheres connected by corpus callosum. Controls thinking, memory, speech, voluntary movement, emotions. Cerebral cortex (grey matter) has motor, sensory, association areas.
  • Thalamus: Relay station for sensory impulses; pain, temperature
  • Hypothalamus: Controls body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep; master endocrine gland — controls pituitary
Midbrain:
  • Connects forebrain and hindbrain. Controls visual and auditory reflexes. Superior colliculi (vision) and inferior colliculi (hearing).
Hindbrain:
  • Cerebellum: Balance, coordination, posture, fine motor movements
  • Pons: Regulates breathing rhythm; relay between cerebellum and cerebrum
  • Medulla oblongata: Controls involuntary functions — heartbeat, breathing, blood pressure, swallowing, vomiting

Human Health and Diseases
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What is immunity? Distinguish between innate and acquired immunity.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Immunity: Ability of an organism to resist disease-causing organisms (pathogens) and their products.
  • Innate (Non-specific) immunity: Present from birth; non-specific; first line of defence. Includes physical barriers (skin), chemical barriers (tears, saliva, HCl), cellular defenses (phagocytes, NK cells), fever.
  • Acquired (Specific/Adaptive) immunity: Develops after exposure to a specific pathogen. Has memory. Two types: Humoral (B cells → antibodies) and Cell-mediated (T cells).
Q2
Name the causative organisms of malaria, typhoid and AIDS.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Malaria: Plasmodium vivax (benign tertian), P. falciparum (malignant/cerebral — most dangerous), P. malariae, P. ovale. Vector: Female Anopheles mosquito.
  • Typhoid: Salmonella typhi (bacterium). Spreads through contaminated food and water. Widal test for diagnosis.
  • AIDS: HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) — a retrovirus. Destroys helper T-cells (CD4+ T-lymphocytes). Confirmed by ELISA test, Western blot test.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the life cycle of Plasmodium (malarial parasite) in humans.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Plasmodium has a complex life cycle with two hosts: human (intermediate) and female Anopheles mosquito (primary/definitive host).
In human body:
  • Female Anopheles bites → injects sporozoites into blood
  • Sporozoites reach liver → multiply → merozoites (schizogony)
  • Merozoites enter RBCs → multiply → RBCs rupture → fever, chills (every 48-72 hrs)
  • Some merozoites → gametocytes (male/female) in blood
In mosquito:
  • Mosquito bites infected person → gametocytes enter mosquito gut
  • Gametocytes → gametes → fertilization → zygote → ookinete → oocyst → sporozoites
  • Sporozoites migrate to salivary glands → ready to infect next human
Q2
Explain the structure of HIV. How does it cause AIDS?★★★
Show Answer ▾
HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) is a retrovirus.
Structure:
  • Two strands of RNA (genetic material)
  • Enzyme: Reverse transcriptase (RNA→DNA)
  • Protein capsid surrounding RNA
  • Outer lipid envelope with glycoprotein spikes (gp120) for attachment to host cells
How HIV causes AIDS:
  • HIV enters body via blood, sexual contact, mother to child (vertical transmission)
  • Attaches to CD4 receptors on Helper T-cells (T₄ cells)
  • HIV RNA → DNA by reverse transcriptase → integrates into host DNA
  • Viral DNA replicates → new HIV particles → T-cells destroyed
  • CD4+ T-cell count falls below 200/μL → severe immunodeficiency
  • Patient becomes susceptible to opportunistic infections (TB, Pneumocystis pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma)
  • Window period: 6 months to years between infection and AIDS symptoms

Applied Biology
Enhancement of Food Production
▼
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
What is the Green Revolution? Who led it? Achievements and limitations?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Green Revolution: Large increase in crop production (especially wheat and rice) in 1960s-70s using HYV seeds, fertilizers, and irrigation. M.S. Swaminathan is the father of Green Revolution in India; Norman Borlaug led it globally.
Achievements:
  • India became self-sufficient in food grain production
  • Wheat production increased from 11 MT (1960) to 73 MT (2006)
  • Development of semi-dwarf HYV wheat (Sonalika, Kalyan Sona) and rice (IR-8)
Limitations:
  • Excessive use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides → soil degradation
  • Groundwater depletion due to irrigation
  • Loss of genetic diversity — only HYV crops grown
  • Benefits mainly reached large farmers
Q2
What is tissue culture? State its applications.★★
Show Answer ▾
Tissue culture (Micropropagation): Growing plant cells, tissues or organs in sterile nutrient medium (in vitro) under controlled conditions to develop into complete plants. Based on totipotency of plant cells.
Applications:
  • Production of disease-free plants (especially virus-free through meristem culture)
  • Rapid mass multiplication of commercially important plants (orchids, banana, potato)
  • Production of haploid plants via anther culture (useful in plant breeding)
  • Somatic hybridization (protoplast fusion) to produce new varieties
  • Conservation of endangered plant species
  • Production of secondary metabolites (alkaloids, antibiotics)

Applied Biology
Biotechnology
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What are restriction enzymes? Give one example. What do they do?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Restriction enzymes (Restriction endonucleases): Enzymes that cut DNA at specific recognition sequences (palindromic sequences). Called "molecular scissors" of genetic engineering.
  • Example: EcoRI (from E. coli) — recognises sequence GAATTC and cuts between G and A on both strands → leaves "sticky ends" (overhangs)
  • Used in recombinant DNA technology to cut DNA at precise locations
  • Sticky ends allow fragments to join with complementary sequences from other organisms
  • Different enzymes cut at different sequences; over 900 restriction enzymes known
Q2
What is PCR? State its uses.★★
Show Answer ▾
PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction): A technique to amplify (make millions of copies of) a specific DNA sequence in vitro in a short time. Developed by Kary Mullis (Nobel Prize 1993).
3 steps (repeated 30-40 cycles): Denaturation (94°C) → Annealing of primers (55°C) → Extension by Taq DNA polymerase (72°C)
Uses:
  • DNA fingerprinting (forensic science, paternity testing)
  • Diagnosis of genetic diseases and infectious diseases
  • Amplifying ancient/fossil DNA
  • Research and cloning of genes
  • COVID-19 RT-PCR test for diagnosis
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain how insulin is produced using recombinant DNA technology (Humulin).★★★
Show Answer ▾
Recombinant Insulin (Humulin) — first recombinant pharmaceutical product approved for use in humans (1982, Eli Lilly).
Human insulin structure: Two polypeptide chains — Chain A (21 amino acids) + Chain B (30 amino acids) linked by disulfide bonds. Insulin was earlier extracted from pig/cow pancreas — caused allergic reactions.
Steps of production:
  • Human insulin gene (A and B chain genes) synthesized chemically
  • Each chain gene inserted into separate plasmid vectors using restriction enzymes and DNA ligase
  • Recombinant plasmids introduced into E. coli bacteria separately
  • E. coli cultures produce chain A and chain B insulin proteins
  • Both chains extracted, purified, and combined in vitro
  • Disulfide bonds form between A and B chains → functional human insulin
Q2
What are Bt crops? Explain how Bt toxin protects crops from insects.★★
Show Answer ▾
Bt crops: Genetically modified crops containing genes from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) that produce insecticidal proteins (Bt toxins/cry proteins).
How Bt toxin works:
  • Bt bacterium produces Cry proteins (crystal proteins/δ-endotoxins) as inactive protoxins
  • When insect larvae eat Bt crop leaves → alkaline pH of insect gut activates the protoxin
  • Activated Bt toxin binds to specific receptors in midgut epithelium → forms pores → gut cells lyse → insect dies
  • Bt toxin is specific to certain insects — harmless to mammals, birds, humans
Examples: Bt cotton (bollworm resistance), Bt maize, Bt brinjal. cry1Ac and cry2Ab genes used in cotton.

Ecology
Organisms and Populations
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
Distinguish between mutualism and commensalism with one example each.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • Mutualism (+/+): Both species benefit. E.g., Lichen (algae + fungi — algae photosynthesises, fungi provides water and minerals); Rhizobium in legume root nodules; Mycorrhizal fungi in plant roots.
  • Commensalism (+/0): One species benefits, other is neither harmed nor helped. E.g., Barnacles on whale skin (barnacle gets transport and access to food; whale unaffected); Orchid growing on tree branches (epiphyte gets light; tree unaffected).
Q2
What is the logistic growth model? Write the equation and explain each term.★★
Show Answer ▾
Logistic growth model (S-curve / sigmoid curve): Population growth with limited resources — growth slows as population approaches carrying capacity.
Equation: dN/dt = rN (K–N)/K
  • dN/dt = rate of change of population size
  • r = intrinsic rate of natural increase
  • N = current population size
  • K = Carrying capacity (maximum population environment can support)
  • (K–N)/K = fraction of still unexploited resources
When N approaches K, growth rate approaches zero. Proposed by Verhulst.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Compare exponential growth and logistic growth in populations.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Exponential (J-curve)Logistic (S-curve)
Unlimited resources assumedLimited resources (realistic)
dN/dt = rNdN/dt = rN(K-N)/K
Curve is J-shaped; no upper limitCurve is S-shaped; levels off at K
Seen in introduced species in new environmentSeen in most natural populations
Logistic growth has three phases: lag phase → exponential phase → stationary phase (at K).

Ecology
Ecosystems and Energy Flow
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What is the 10% law of energy transfer? Who proposed it?★★★
Show Answer ▾
10% Law (Lindeman's Efficiency Law) proposed by Raymond Lindeman (1942).
"Only about 10% of the energy available at each trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level. The remaining 90% is lost as heat during respiration, movement, and other metabolic activities."
Example:
  • Producers (Plants) — 1000 J
  • Primary consumers (Herbivores) — 100 J (10%)
  • Secondary consumers (Carnivores) — 10 J (10%)
  • Tertiary consumers — 1 J (10%)
This explains why food chains are limited to 4-5 trophic levels.
Q2
What is ecological succession? Distinguish between primary and secondary succession.★★
Show Answer ▾
Ecological succession: Gradual and predictable change in species composition of an ecosystem over time, eventually reaching a stable climax community.
  • Primary succession: Begins on a bare, lifeless area where no soil exists (e.g., bare rock after lava flow, newly emerged volcanic island). Very slow — thousands of years. Pioneer species: lichens, mosses.
  • Secondary succession: Begins in an area where a community was destroyed but soil remains intact (e.g., after forest fire, flood, abandoned farmland). Faster — decades. Pioneer species: grasses, shrubs.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Describe the ecological pyramids — pyramid of numbers, biomass and energy.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Ecological pyramids show the relationship between trophic levels in an ecosystem.
  • Pyramid of Numbers: Shows number of organisms at each trophic level. Usually upright (many plants, fewer herbivores, even fewer carnivores). Can be inverted (e.g., one tree → many insects → few birds).
  • Pyramid of Biomass: Shows total dry weight (biomass) at each trophic level. Usually upright in terrestrial ecosystems. Inverted in aquatic ecosystems (phytoplankton biomass less than zooplankton due to rapid turnover).
  • Pyramid of Energy: Shows energy available at each trophic level. Always upright — can never be inverted. Based on 10% law. Most fundamental pyramid.

Biodiversity, Conservation and Environmental Issues
▼
⬤ 2 Mark Questions
Q1
What are biodiversity hotspots? Name two hotspots found in India.★★★
Show Answer ▾
Biodiversity hotspots: Regions of Earth that have exceptionally high species richness, high endemism (species found nowhere else), AND have lost 70%+ of their original habitat. Concept by Norman Myers (1988).
World has 34 biodiversity hotspots.
Two hotspots in India:
  • Western Ghats & Sri Lanka — high endemism in plants, reptiles, amphibians; tea/coffee region
  • Eastern Himalayas (Indo-Burma region) — high diversity in mammals, birds, reptiles
India also has part of Indo-Burma hotspot. India is one of 12 megadiverse countries.
Q2
Distinguish between in-situ and ex-situ conservation with two examples each.★★
Show Answer ▾
  • In-situ conservation: Conservation of species in their natural habitat. E.g., National parks (Jim Corbett, Kaziranga), Wildlife sanctuaries, Biosphere reserves (Nilgiri, Sundarban), Sacred groves.
  • Ex-situ conservation: Conservation of species outside their natural habitat. E.g., Botanical gardens (Kew Gardens, Indian Botanic Garden Howrah), Zoological parks, Seed banks (National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources, New Delhi), Cryopreservation, Tissue culture.
⬤ 3 Mark Questions
Q1
Explain the causes of biodiversity loss — the 4 E's (Evil quartet).★★★
Show Answer ▾
Coined by Paul Ehrlich. The four major causes of biodiversity loss:
  • 1. Habitat loss and fragmentation: Most important cause. Deforestation, urbanization, agriculture expansion destroy natural habitats. Tropical forests being cleared at alarming rates.
  • 2. Over-exploitation: Hunting, poaching, overfishing. E.g., Dodo extinct due to hunting; commercial whaling reduced whale populations.
  • 3. Alien/Invasive species introduction: Non-native species introduced into new habitats can outcompete, prey on, or infect native species. E.g., Nile perch introduced to Lake Victoria → 200 cichlid fish species extinct; Water hyacinth (Eichhornia) in Indian water bodies.
  • 4. Co-extinctions: When one species goes extinct, other species that depend on it also go extinct. E.g., host-specific parasites, pollinators, mutualistic partners.
Q2
What is ozone depletion? How does it occur? What are its effects?★★★
Show Answer ▾
Ozone layer is in stratosphere (15–35 km altitude); absorbs harmful UV-B and UV-C radiation from sun.
Depletion caused by:
  • CFCs (Chlorofluorocarbons) — from refrigerators, AC, aerosol sprays. Released into stratosphere.
  • UV radiation breaks CFC → releases Cl atoms → Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂ (one Cl atom destroys 1 lakh ozone molecules)
  • Antarctica ozone hole discovered in 1980s
Effects of ozone depletion:
  • Increased UV-B → skin cancer (melanoma), cataracts, sunburn in humans
  • DNA damage → mutations
  • Damage to marine ecosystems — affects phytoplankton, disrupts food chain
  • Reduced crop yields
Control: Montreal Protocol (1987) — international treaty to phase out CFCs.
